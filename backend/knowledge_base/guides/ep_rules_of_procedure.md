# The European Parliament's Rules of Procedure

## QUICK FACTS

- **What it is:** The European Parliament's Rules of Procedure (RoP) are Parliament's own internal rulebook. They set out how Parliament organises itself and works: the role of Members, its internal bodies, how legislative, budgetary and discharge procedures run inside Parliament, how plenary sittings and committees function, and how Parliament deals with the Commission, the Council and other institutions.
- **Legal basis:** Article 232 of the Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union (TFEU) gives Parliament the right to adopt its own Rules of Procedure by a majority of its component Members. The Rules are NOT a treaty and are NOT EU legislation: they are internal, self-imposed organisational rules with no CELEX number and no legal effect on citizens or Member States.
- **Who adopts and amends them:** Parliament itself. Any Member may table an amendment to the Rules. The Committee on Constitutional Affairs (AFCO) examines every proposed amendment and decides whether to report it to plenary. Plenary then adopts amendments by a majority of Parliament's component Members (an absolute majority of the full House, not just those present and voting), per Rule 243(2) RoP.
- **Current edition:** 10th parliamentary term (2024-2029) Rules of Procedure, entered into force 16 July 2024, the first day of the 10th term. Known informally as the "Parliament 2024" reform. Adopted by the outgoing 9th-term Parliament on 10 April 2024, based on an AFCO report (20 March 2024) implementing 116 amendments recommended by the cross-party "Parliament 2024" working group set up by President Roberta Metsola in January 2023. The Rules have been lightly amended again since (a January 2025 consolidated edition exists); always check the live edition on Parliament's website rather than assuming a fixed rule number is permanent.
- **Structure:** 15 Titles (I to XV) plus 8 Annexes, roughly 250 numbered Rules.
- **Official text:** European Parliament, Rules of Procedure, current edition: https://www.europarl.europa.eu/doceo/document/RULES-10-2024-07-16-TOC_EN.html (table of contents; use the "most recent version" link at the top for the live consolidated text). EUR-Lex summary: https://eur-lex.europa.eu/EN/legal-content/summary/european-parliament-rules-of-procedure.html
- **Size of Parliament governed by these Rules:** 720 Members for the 2024-2029 term (up from 705 in 2019-2024), following Council Decision (EU) 2023/2061 reallocating seats among Member States.
- **Distinct from the treaties:** the treaties (TEU, TFEU, Charter of Fundamental Rights) are primary EU law and set Parliament's powers vis-a-vis other institutions and the public. The Rules of Procedure are secondary, self-adopted, internal, and can be changed by Parliament alone without treaty change, provided the change does not exceed the powers the treaties already give Parliament.

## What the Rules of Procedure are, and are not

The Rules of Procedure are Parliament's constitution for its own internal life. They answer questions like: how is the President of Parliament elected; how many Vice-Presidents and Quaestors are there; how does a political group form; how does a bill move through committee and plenary; what is a quorum; how does a Member ask the Commission a question; what happens if a Member misbehaves in the Chamber. None of this is treaty law. The treaties (chiefly TFEU Articles 223-234 and the ordinary/special legislative procedure articles) establish that Parliament exists, is directly elected, co-legislates with the Council, and has scrutiny powers over the Commission. The Rules of Procedure then operationalise those treaty powers into a working parliamentary machine.

Because the Rules are self-adopted, Parliament can and does revise them regularly to reflect changing political practice, without needing Council or Commission agreement, and without a treaty revision (an Intergovernmental Conference). The only external constraint is that the Rules cannot grant Parliament powers the treaties do not give it, and disputes about how Parliament applies its own Rules internally are generally treated as questions of internal organisation, though the Court of Justice has on occasion been asked to review whether a choice of legal basis or procedure complied with the treaties.

## Who amends the Rules, and how (AFCO's role)

- **Rule 243 RoP** ("Amendment of the Rules of Procedure") governs the process. Any Member of Parliament may table an amendment to the Rules or the Annexes.
- The **Committee on Constitutional Affairs (AFCO)** is the standing committee with responsibility for Parliament's own Rules of Procedure (see Annex VI, the list of standing committee competences). AFCO examines a proposed amendment and decides whether it should go to plenary as a committee report.
- Plenary then votes. Under **Rule 243(2)**, amendments to the Rules of Procedure require the backing of a **majority of Parliament's component Members** (an absolute majority of the full 720-seat House, i.e. the votes of Members present and those absent both count against the threshold), a higher bar than the simple majority of votes cast that applies to most ordinary business.
- Amendments and any related interpretations normally take effect on the first day of the part-session following their adoption, unless the decision specifies a different date (as with the 2024 reform, which was timed to enter into force on the first day of the new parliamentary term).
- Historically, major overhauls of the Rules have clustered around the start of a new parliamentary term (e.g. after each European election) plus periodic "housekeeping" amendments in between, driven by AFCO reports responding to institutional friction observed in practice.
- **Rule on interpretation:** where the meaning or application of a Rule is disputed, the President may refer the question to AFCO for an opinion; AFCO's interpretations, once approved by Parliament (or unopposed within the set period), become annexed to the relevant Rule as an explanatory note and guide future application, without being a formal amendment.

## The "Parliament 2024" reform (16 July 2024)

The Rules currently in force are the product of the biggest revision in years, nicknamed "Parliament 2024". Timeline:

1. **January 2023:** President Roberta Metsola tasks the Conference of Presidents with setting up the Parliament 2024 working group, made up of representatives of all political groups, to prepare reforms ahead of the June 2024 elections.
2. **December 2023:** the Conference of Presidents endorses the working group's recommendations.
3. **20 March 2024:** AFCO adopts a report implementing the recommendations: 116 amendments to the Rules of Procedure.
4. **10 April 2024:** the outgoing 9th-term Parliament formally adopts the decision reforming the Rules.
5. **16 July 2024:** the new Rules enter into force, the first sitting day of the new 10th term.

Headline changes from the 2024 reform, useful for anyone tracking how Parliament actually works today:

- **Temporary legislative committees (Rule 214):** where a legislative file cuts across the competence of more than three standing committees, the Conference of Presidents can, as a last resort, set up a temporary committee to handle it instead of leaving it stuck in a jurisdictional dispute between committees.
- **Co-rapporteurship (Rule 54):** up to three co-rapporteurs may now, exceptionally, share a single report, formalising a practice that previously happened informally on some cross-cutting files.
- **Budgetary assessment (Rule 58):** proposals with budgetary implications must undergo a specific assessment by the Committee on Budgets (BUDG); the Committee on Budgetary Control (CONT) is also drawn in for proposals using non-traditional, "off-budget" financial instruments.
- **Parliament statements (Rule 167):** a new plenary debate format allowing Parliament to make institutional statements on matters of EU-wide interest.
- **Special scrutiny hearings (Rule 141)** and a new **expected cooperation duty (Rule 127)**: Union institutions, bodies and other persons invited to committee meetings or special scrutiny hearings are now expected to attend and to supply relevant documents.
- **New Article 122 TFEU statements (Rule 138):** a dedicated procedure for Parliament to receive statements when the Council relies on Article 122 TFEU (the "emergency measures" legal basis) to legislate without the ordinary legislative procedure, addressing complaints that Parliament had been sidelined on Article 122 files (energy price measures, COVID-era instruments).
- **Urgent procedure tightened (Rule 170, ex Rule 163):** use of the urgent procedure in plenary is now limited to clearly justified cases, while committees were given more scope to use simplified or accelerated procedures for less contentious files, shifting speed-ups earlier in the process rather than at the plenary vote stage.
- Numerous committee-management changes: conflicts-of-competence handling, coordinator and shadow rapporteur provisions tidied up, and streamlined agenda-setting rules.

Full EPRS analysis: European Parliament's new Rules of Procedure, Parliament 2024 reforms, EPRS Briefing PE 762.396 (September 2024): https://www.europarl.europa.eu/RegData/etudes/BRIE/2024/762396/EPRS_BRI(2024)762396_EN.pdf

## Structure: the 15 Titles

The current (10th term) Rules of Procedure run roughly as follows. Exact rule numbers can shift slightly at the margins with future amendments, so always treat these as the July 2024 baseline, not an immutable citation.

- **Title I: Members, Parliament bodies and political groups** (Rules 1-38). Members' mandate, immunity, standards of conduct; election of the President, Vice-Presidents and Quaestors; the Bureau, the Conference of Presidents, the Conference of Committee Chairs, the Conference of Delegation Chairs; formation and dissolution of political groups; non-attached Members; seating in the Chamber.
- **Title II: Legislative, budgetary, discharge and other procedures** (Rules 39-116). The largest Title. Covers general provisions on legislative procedures, first reading, second reading, third reading/conciliation, completion of a legislative act, the annual budgetary procedure, the discharge procedure (approving the Commission's spending of the EU budget), euro-derogation opinions, EU trust funds for external action, social-dialogue procedures, codification and recasting of existing law, and delegated and implementing acts.
- **Title III: External relations** (Rules 117-123). International agreements; provisional application or suspension of agreements; special representatives and international representation of the Union; recommendations on the Union's external policies; Parliament's role in the Common Foreign and Security Policy (CFSP); debates on breaches of human rights, democracy and the rule of law abroad.
- **Title IV: Transparency of business** (Rules 124-127). Transparency of Parliament's own activities, public access to documents, access to Parliament's premises, cooperation duties in committee meetings and special scrutiny hearings.
- **Title V: Relations with other institutions and bodies and political accountability** (Rules 128-155). Election of the Commission President and approval of the College of Commissioners; scrutiny statements (including the Rule 138 procedure for Article 122 TFEU); statements by the Court of Auditors and the European Central Bank; special scrutiny hearings and the full toolkit of parliamentary questions (oral questions with debate, Question Time, written questions, major interpellations, ECB and Single Supervisory/Resolution Mechanism questions); consideration of other institutions' annual reports; motions for resolutions and the Rule 150 debates on breaches of human rights, democracy and the rule of law; consultation of the European Economic and Social Committee and the Committee of the Regions; interinstitutional agreements; referrals to the Court of Justice of the European Union.
- **Title VI: Relations with national parliaments** (Rules 156-158). Exchange of information and reciprocal facilities with national parliaments; the Conference of Parliamentary Committees for Union Affairs (COSAC); conferences of parliaments.
- **Title VII: Sessions** (Rules 159-211). The mechanics of plenary: parliamentary term, sessions, part-sessions and sittings; convening Parliament; the draft agenda and how it is adopted and amended; procedure without amendment and debate; short presentations; Parliament statements; extraordinary and topical debates; the urgent procedure; joint debates; time limits; conduct of sittings (languages, distribution of documents, speaking time, one-minute speeches, personal statements); measures against non-compliance with standards of conduct; quorum, amendments and voting (including roll-call votes, secret ballots, electronic voting, tied votes, explanations of vote); points of order and procedural motions (referral back to committee, closure of debate, adjournment, suspension of sitting); and the public record of proceedings (minutes, texts adopted, verbatim reports, audiovisual record).
- **Title VIII: Committees and delegations** (Rules 212 onward). Setting up standing committees, special committees, temporary legislative committees and committees of inquiry; committee composition, duties, subcommittees, bureaux, coordinators and shadow rapporteurs; committee meetings; interparliamentary delegations.
- **Title IX: Petitions** (Rules 232-236). The right of petition, examination of petitions, fact-finding visits, notice of petitions, and the European Citizens' Initiative.
- **Title X: Ombudsman** (Rules 237-239). Election, activities and dismissal of the European Ombudsman.
- **Title XI: Parliament's Secretariat** (Rule 240).
- **Title XII: Powers and responsibilities relating to European political parties and European political foundations** (Rule 241).
- **Title XIII: Application and amendment of the Rules of Procedure** (Rules 242-243). How the Rules are interpreted and applied day to day, and how they are amended (see above).
- **Title XIV: Extraordinary circumstances** (Rules 244-247). Extraordinary measures Parliament may take, disturbance of the political balance in Parliament, the remote-participation regime, and holding a part-session or sitting in separate meeting rooms.
- **Title XV: Miscellaneous provisions** (Rules 248-251). The symbols of the Union, gender mainstreaming, unfinished business at the end of a term, and corrigenda.

Plus **8 Annexes**, including the Code of Conduct for Members on integrity and transparency (Annex I), the Code of Appropriate Behaviour (Annex II), the discharge procedure detail (Annex V), the powers and responsibilities of standing committees (Annex VI, the definitive list of which committee handles which policy area), and the confirmation-hearing procedure for Commissioners-designate (Annex VII).

## Key mechanisms a policy professional should know

- **Rule 150, debates on breaches of human rights, democracy and the rule of law:** the recurring plenary debate (usually held in Strasbourg) on urgent human-rights situations outside the EU. These are commonly called "urgencies" in Brussels shorthand. Political groups table joint or separate short motions for resolution, which are debated briefly and voted as a whole without going through a full committee report or the standard first/second-reading legislative machinery, so in practice they move faster and lighter than an ordinary own-initiative report. Annex IV sets the criteria used to choose which cases are debated.
- **Urgent procedure (Rule 170, ex Rule 163):** allows Parliament to fast-track a decision on a Commission or Council proposal following unforeseen developments, at the request of the President, a committee, a political group, a threshold of Members, the Commission or the Council. The committee responsible gets priority and may use the simplified procedure; exceptionally Parliament can decide without a report or on the basis of an oral report. Since the 2024 reform, use of this procedure in plenary is limited to clearly justified cases.
- **Own-initiative reports (Rule 55, ex Rule 54):** the mechanism by which a committee produces a report and resolution on a subject where Parliament has no formal legislative role, used to set out political positions, propose future legislation, or press the Commission to act. Distinct from a legislative report responding to a Commission proposal.
- **Oral and written questions (Rules 141-147):** the parliamentary-questions toolkit covers Question Time (Rule 143, direct oral exchange with the Commission or Council at set plenary slots), questions for oral answer with debate (Rule 142), questions for written answer (Rule 144), major interpellations for written answer (Rule 145), and dedicated question channels to the European Central Bank (Rule 146) and to the Single Supervisory Mechanism/Single Resolution Mechanism (Rule 147). See `ep_parliamentary_questions_rules` for the full mechanics.
- **Points of order (Rule 202) and procedural motions (Rule 203):** the tools any Member can use in plenary to challenge how a sitting is being conducted (rather than the substance of a debate), including motions to refer a matter back to committee (Rule 204), to close a debate (Rule 205), to adjourn a debate or vote (Rule 206), or to suspend or close a sitting (Rule 207).
- **Quorum (Rule 185):** Parliament has a quorum, in principle one third of component Members, but the Rules presume a quorum exists unless the President, at the request of at least 20 Members present, has the number of Members present verified before a vote; if a quorum is found lacking, the vote is postponed. In practice quorum challenges are rare and largely a procedural/political tool.
- **Voting methods (Rules 189-201):** ordinary vote (show of hands or electronic), roll-call vote (Rule 197, where each Member's individual vote is recorded and published, generally required for final votes on legislative texts and used whenever a political group or a threshold of Members requests it), secret ballot (Rule 198, used for elections of Officers and similar personal votes), and explanations of vote (Rule 201, where Members explain their vote after the fact, often for the public record on contentious files).

## Why it matters to a policy professional

The Rules of Procedure determine the operational reality of every EU legislative file that passes through Parliament: which committee gets it, whether it needs a temporary legislative committee because it crosses jurisdictions, whether it can be fast-tracked, how many co-rapporteurs are involved, what majority is needed to amend it in plenary, and what happens if the vote is disputed. A lobbyist, in-house public-affairs professional, or law firm tracking a file needs to know these mechanics to predict timing, identify who to engage (rapporteur, shadow rapporteurs, committee coordinators), and understand why a vote was delayed, referred back to committee, or fast-tracked under the urgent procedure. Because AFCO periodically revises the Rules, always confirm current rule numbers against the live edition before citing a specific Rule number in client-facing material: the numbering shifts at the margins with almost every amendment round.
