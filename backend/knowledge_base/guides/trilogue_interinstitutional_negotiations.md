# Trilogues: Informal Interinstitutional Negotiations

## QUICK FACTS
- **What it is**: a **trilogue** ("trilogue meeting" / "tripartite meeting") is an **informal, closed-door negotiation** between the **European Parliament**, the **Council of the EU** and the **European Commission** to agree a common text on a legislative proposal under the **ordinary legislative procedure** (Article 294 TFEU, OEIL code `(COD)`).
- **Not a formal Treaty step**: trilogues have **no Treaty basis** of their own. They are a practice that grew up to shortcut the formal reading system and now produce the **vast majority** of ordinary-legislative-procedure outcomes.
- **Who negotiates**: EP side, the **negotiating team** led by the **rapporteur**, with the committee **Chair or a Vice-Chair presiding**, and **shadow rapporteurs** from each political group that wants to take part (Rules of Procedure of the European Parliament, **Rule 71** general provisions, **Rule 74** conduct of negotiations). Council side, the **rotating six-month Presidency**, usually via its Permanent Representative or a Deputy Permanent Representative, working from a mandate given by **Coreper** or the Council itself. Commission side, present at every trilogue as **mediator/honest broker**, not as a co-decision-maker.
- **Mandates**: EP mandate comes from the **committee report adopted in committee** (or, since Lisbon, directly from **plenary** for a first-reading position), Council mandate comes from a **general approach** or negotiating mandate agreed by **Coreper/Council**, Commission mandate comes from the **College of Commissioners**.
- **Core working document**: the **four-column document** (also called multi-column table), column 1 = Commission proposal, column 2 = Council position, column 3 = EP position, column 4 = the evolving compromise text.
- **Outcome**: a **provisional political agreement**, which still needs **formal adoption** by both co-legislators (committee vote, plenary vote, Council adoption) before it becomes law and is signed and published in the **Official Journal**.
- **Legal transparency framework**: **Interinstitutional Agreement on Better Law-Making**, signed 13 April 2016, CELEX `32016Q0512(01)`: [EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32016Q0512(01)).
- **Landmark transparency case**: **De Capitani v European Parliament**, Case **T-540/15**, General Court judgment **22 March 2018** (Emilio De Capitani sought access to four-column trilogue tables; Parliament's refusal was annulled). Followed by **De Capitani v Council**, Case **T-163/21** (2023), which extended the same logic to Council working-group documents. These are **General Court ("T-")** cases, not Court of Justice ("C-") cases.
- **Scale**: roughly **85-90% of ordinary-legislative-procedure files** are now concluded at first reading (89% in the 2014-2019 term, 86% in 2019-2024), almost always via trilogue. In the 2019-2024 term alone, **973 trilogue meetings** took place.
- **Official EP source**: [Understanding trilogue, EPRS briefing PE 690.614](https://www.europarl.europa.eu/RegData/etudes/BRIE/2021/690614/EPRS_BRI(2021)690614_EN.pdf); [EP Interinstitutional Negotiations portal](https://www.europarl.europa.eu/olp/en/interinstitutional-negotiations); [EUR-Lex glossary entry](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/EN/legal-content/glossary/trilogue.html).

## What a trilogue is, and where it sits in the procedure
A trilogue is a small, closed meeting, typically a handful of people per institution plus advisers, held to find a compromise text that all three institutions can live with before the formal reading stages of the ordinary legislative procedure are used to ratify it. It exists because the Treaty's formal reading system (see `eu_legislative_procedures_explained`) is slow and can run to a second or even third reading with a Conciliation Committee. Since the practice consolidated after the 1999 Amsterdam reforms and especially after Lisbon, the co-legislators found it faster and politically more efficient to agree the substance informally first, then rubber-stamp it through the formal readings. This is why **first-reading early agreement** (an agreement reached and formally adopted at first reading, without the file ever needing a full second reading) is now the statistical norm rather than the exception for `(COD)` files.

A trilogue can happen at several points:
- **Before or during EP first reading**: the most common case today. The EP committee votes a negotiating mandate (often together with the decision to enter negotiations) before or instead of a full plenary first-reading vote, so the trilogue text becomes the plenary vote itself.
- **Before Council's first reading**: rarer, since Council's first-reading position is usually not the trigger point.
- **Between EP first and second reading, or during second reading**: when first-reading agreement fails, trilogues can resume ahead of EP's second reading, governed by EP Rule 74 ("Negotiations ahead of Parliament's second reading").
- **In conciliation**: if second reading also fails to converge, a formal **Conciliation Committee** (Article 294(10)-(12) TFEU) is convened, made up of equal numbers of Council and EP representatives, again with the Commission facilitating. This is now rare precisely because trilogues absorb almost all of the negotiating work earlier in the process; conciliation is a Treaty-mandated fallback, not the everyday mechanism.

## Who is in the room
**European Parliament**: the negotiating team is led by the **rapporteur** (the MEP who steered the file through committee, see `ep_rapporteur_shadow_system`), presided over by the committee **Chair or a designated Vice-Chair**, and includes **shadow rapporteurs** appointed by each political group that wishes to be represented, plus EP officials (committee secretariat, legal service, group advisers). The team operates under a **Code of Conduct** set by the Conference of Presidents and reports back to the committee after each trilogue round.

**Council**: represented by the **country holding the rotating six-month Presidency** (Coreper Ambassador/Deputy, or a working-party chair for technical points), backed by the Council General Secretariat. The Presidency negotiates on the basis of a mandate: either a **general approach** (a first-reading negotiating position agreed unanimously or by qualified majority in Council before trilogues start) or a mandate refined and monitored through **Coreper** meetings between trilogue rounds (see `council_voting_qmv_mechanics` for how that mandate is built).

**European Commission**: attends every trilogue as the institution that tabled the original proposal, but formally as **mediator, not co-decider**. Its role is to facilitate compromise between EP and Council, defend the coherence of its own proposal, and, crucially, retain the right to **withdraw the proposal** at any point before Council has acted upon it, which gives it real leverage. Commission officials from the lead DG and the Commission's Secretariat-General are present.

## The four-column document
The technical backbone of every trilogue is the **four-column table** (multi-column document):
1. **Column 1**: the Commission's original legislative proposal, article by article.
2. **Column 2**: the Council's negotiating position (from its general approach or evolving mandate).
3. **Column 3**: the European Parliament's position (from the committee report or first-reading vote).
4. **Column 4**: the compromise text, filled in progressively as trilogue rounds converge, showing what has been provisionally agreed, what is still bracketed/open, and any Presidency compromise proposals.

Four-column tables are **not published as a matter of course**. This is the single biggest transparency criticism of the trilogue system: the substantive negotiating record of most EU legislation exists mainly in these internal tables, which historically were withheld even after the legislation was adopted, on the argument that disclosure could undermine the institutions' ongoing decision-making space.

## From provisional agreement to law
A trilogue does not itself adopt legislation. It produces a **provisional political agreement** (sometimes called a "trilogue agreement" or "informal agreement"), a text both negotiating teams are prepared to recommend to their principals. That text then has to run the **formal** gauntlet:
- **EP side**: the committee **confirms** the agreed text (a further committee vote, "confirmation of the provisional agreement"), followed by a **plenary vote** at first (or second) reading. Under EP Rule 74, if the negotiating mandate was given without a prior plenary vote, the plenary vote on the trilogue outcome effectively **is** the first-reading vote.
- **Council side**: the Presidency brings the text back to **Coreper** for endorsement, then to a **Council formation** for formal adoption of its position at first or second reading, or, once EP has voted, straight to final adoption.
- **Formalities**: once both institutions have formally adopted matching texts, the act is **signed** by the Presidents of EP and Council and **published in the Official Journal** (see `official_journal_explained` if present), entering into force per its own commencement provisions.

If the trilogue process fails to produce a workable compromise, the file falls back on the **full formal reading system**: EP second reading amendments, Council second reading, and, if still unresolved, the **Conciliation Committee** for a mandatory joint text within a 6-week window (extendable to 8), followed by a third reading if conciliation itself fails. This full escalation is now rare for exactly the reason trilogues exist: the informal channel resolves the vast majority of disagreements long before a formal reading deadline would force the issue.

## The 2016 Interinstitutional Agreement on Better Law-Making
The **Interinstitutional Agreement (IIA) on Better Law-Making** was signed by the Presidents of the European Parliament, the Council and the Commission on **13 April 2016** and entered into force the same day (OJ L 123, 12.5.2016, CELEX [`32016Q0512(01)`](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32016Q0512(01))). Its trilogue-relevant commitments (paragraphs 32-34 and 38-40 of the Agreement) include:
- A pledge that the three institutions "will ensure the transparency of legislative procedures... including an appropriate handling of trilateral negotiations (trilogues)".
- A commitment to **improve communication to the public** throughout the legislative cycle.
- A commitment to work towards a **joint database/platform tracking legislative files**, so a user can follow a proposal's progress across all three institutions from the same reference point, feeding directly into what OEIL now does (see `oeil_legislative_observatory`).

Critics at the time, and since, judged the IIA's trilogue-specific language as vague ("appropriate handling" commits to nothing concrete) compared with what campaigners for openness wanted: automatic publication of four-column documents, trilogue calendars, and negotiating mandates as a matter of course.

## Transparency criticism and the De Capitani litigation
Civil-society groups (Corporate Europe Observatory, ClientEarth, access-to-documents campaigners) and MEPs, notably **Emilio De Capitani**, a former EP committee secretariat official, argued the IIA did not go far enough and that Regulation (EC) 1049/2001 on public access to documents already required disclosure of trilogue records once requested.

**De Capitani v European Parliament, Case T-540/15** (General Court, judgment 22 March 2018): De Capitani requested access to four-column tables from ongoing trilogues on the Data Protection Package and the European Border and Coast Guard Regulation. Parliament refused, citing protection of the decision-making process under Article 4(3) of Regulation 1049/2001. The **General Court annulled Parliament's refusal**, holding that:
- The principles of **publicity and transparency** apply to the legislative process, including trilogues, because trilogues constitute a "decisive stage" of that process.
- Exceptions to public access must be interpreted **restrictively**, and a general, abstract risk to the decision-making process is not enough: the institution must show it is **reasonably foreseeable, not purely hypothetical**, that disclosure would specifically and actually undermine that process.
- Access to trilogue documents must **in principle be granted** on request, unless that stricter test is met.

**De Capitani v Council, Case T-163/21** (2023) extended the same reasoning to documents produced within **Council working groups** during the legislative process, reinforcing that the Council cannot treat its internal legislative preparatory documents as categorically exempt either.

**Aftermath and reform state (as of mid-2026)**: the judgments did not create an automatic publication regime. Parliament and civil society have repeatedly called on all three institutions to comply fully with T-540/15's logic and to build the joint public database on trilogue documents envisaged (loosely) by the 2016 IIA. Implementation has been incremental rather than systemic: individual four-column documents can still be requested case by case under Regulation 1049/2001 and are increasingly leaked or proactively published for high-salience files (e.g. the AI Act, the Data Act, Migration and Asylum Pact files), but there is still no default, real-time public register of trilogue documents. Trilogue transparency remains an active reform debate, not a closed file.

## Why it matters to a policy professional
- **Timing**: if a file has entered trilogue, the substantive negotiation is happening behind closed doors, not on the floor of committee or plenary. Formal votes that follow are largely confirmatory. A lobbying or advocacy strategy has to reach the **negotiating team** (rapporteur, shadows, Coreper-level Council contacts) before or during trilogue rounds, not after.
- **Access**: four-column documents are not routinely public. Where you need to know exactly what is being traded between institutions, request access under Regulation 1049/2001 and expect the De Capitani standard (institution must show concrete, foreseeable harm, not blanket refusal) to apply.
- **Reading the procedure file**: OEIL (`oeil_legislative_observatory`) publishes trilogue dates and, once concluded, the resulting compromise text once formally tabled, but rarely the intermediate four-column drafts.
- **Predicting outcomes**: whether a file is heading for first-reading early agreement (fast, trilogue-driven) or a drawn-out second reading/conciliation (slow, formal) is one of the strongest signals for timeline forecasting (see Brubru's Predictions feature).
