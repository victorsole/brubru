## What the Digital Euro Is

The digital euro would be a central bank digital currency (CBDC) issued by the ECB and available to consumers and businesses across the euro area, alongside cash and existing private payment methods. It is designed as "digital cash": a risk-free public means of payment directly backed by the central bank, usable both online and offline, working in shops, online, and person-to-person.

It is not a cryptocurrency and not tied to any blockchain speculation narrative -- it is a sovereign, euro-area-wide retail payment instrument intended to reduce dependence on non-European card schemes and big-tech payment platforms, and to preserve the "anchor" role of central bank money as private and foreign digital payment options expand.

## Why the Project Exists

Two linked drivers cited consistently by the ECB and the Commission:

1. **Strategic/monetary autonomy.** Euro-area retail payments are heavily dependent on non-EU card networks (Visa, Mastercard) and increasingly on non-EU big-tech wallets. A pan-European public payment rail reduces this dependency and keeps central bank money -- the anchor of the two-tier monetary system -- relevant as cash usage declines.
2. **Preserving public money's role.** As cash use falls, especially for online payments, the ECB argues a digital form of central bank money is needed so that everyone retains access to risk-free public money, not only commercial bank deposits or private stablecoins.

## Legislative Basis: The Single Currency Package

The Commission presented the package on 28 June 2023 as two parallel Regulations under the ordinary legislative procedure (codecision):

| Proposal | Reference | Purpose |
|----------|-----------|---------|
| Digital euro Regulation | 2023/0212(COD), COM(2023) 369, CELEX 52023PC0369 | Establishes the legal framework, legal tender status, and governance for a possible digital euro |
| Legal tender of cash Regulation | 2023/0208(COD), COM(2023) 364, CELEX 52023PC0364 | Safeguards mandatory acceptance and accessibility of euro banknotes and coins |

The two proposals are deliberately paired: cash retains legal tender status and coexists with the digital euro; the digital euro Regulation is what would extend legal tender status to the digital form of the currency for the first time. Neither Regulation had completed adoption as of mid-2026 -- both remain in the Parliament/Council legislative process. Verify current status before citing either as adopted.

## Design Features Under Debate / Confirmed in the ECON Position

- **Privacy.** The scheme uses pseudonymous identifiers so the ECB itself cannot link online transactions to individuals; design work references privacy-enhancing techniques (including zero-knowledge-proof approaches referenced in 2026 ECON reporting). An **offline mode** is intended to offer cash-like privacy: payment details stay on the payer's and payee's devices, with no third party (including the ECB) seeing transaction details, similar to handing over a banknote.
- **Holding limits.** To protect bank funding and financial stability, individual holdings would be capped; the ECB's own analysis has tested hypothetical limits up to around EUR 3,000 per person without finding financial-stability harm at that level, but the legally binding figure is a matter for the co-legislators, not the ECB alone, and reporting in 2026 describes MEPs backing periodic (roughly two-yearly) review of the cap rather than a single fixed number. Businesses would face a stricter regime (for example, a short window such as 24 hours to sweep incoming digital-euro payments above the cap into a linked bank account).
- **No programmability of personal money.** The digital euro is presented as a payment instrument, not a programmable-money or conditional-spending tool for individuals' holdings -- an explicit line drawn to counter "programmable money" concerns.
- **Free basic use / no direct fees to consumers** for basic digital-euro services, with costs borne by the ECB (infrastructure) and distributed within the PSP compensation model set out in the Regulation.
- **Pan-euro-area acceptance and offline availability**, including a stated minimum roll-out period (reported as around 24 months) after any decision to issue, intended to give merchants and PSPs time to adapt.
- **Intermediated distribution**, described above: no ECB retail accounts; supervised PSPs are the customer-facing layer.

Design details above reflect the ECON committee's negotiating position and ECB technical publications as of mid-2026; the final text is subject to trilogue negotiation with the Council and may change. Do not present any single holding-limit figure as final law.

## Institutional Roles

| Actor | Role |
|-------|------|
| European Commission | Proposed the Single Currency Package (28 June 2023); DG FISMA leads |
| ECB | Designs and would operate the digital euro infrastructure; decides independently whether and when to issue, but only once the enabling Regulation is in force |
| European Parliament (ECON, opinion from LIBE) | Co-legislator; ECON holds the file, rapporteur Fernando Navarrete Rojas (EPP) |
| Council of the EU (Eurogroup/ECOFIN configuration) | Co-legislator; adopted its negotiating position in December 2025 |
| Payment service providers (banks, non-bank PSPs) | Would distribute the digital euro to end users under the intermediated model |
