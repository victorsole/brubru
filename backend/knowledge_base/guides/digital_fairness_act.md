## Overview

The Digital Fairness Act (DFA) is a forthcoming EU legislative initiative announced in the Commission's 2026 Work Programme. It aims to address unethical commercial practices in digital environments that current consumer protection law does not adequately cover.

President von der Leyen's September 2024 mission letter to Commissioner McGrath called for developing "a Digital Fairness Act to tackle unethical techniques and commercial practices related to dark patterns, marketing by social media influencers, the addictive design of digital products and online profiling especially when consumer vulnerabilities are exploited."

The DFA will supplement (not replace) existing consumer protection frameworks: the Unfair Commercial Practices Directive (UCPD), Consumer Rights Directive (CRD), Unfair Contract Terms Directive, and relevant provisions of the DSA, AVMSD, and DMA.

## Background: 2024 Fitness Check

The Commission's 2024 fitness check of three core consumer protection directives found that:

- Core laws remain relevant but "only partially achieved the objectives" of adequate protection
- Consumers behave differently online than offline
- Technological developments and increased tracking enable businesses to more effectively persuade consumers
- Problems include insufficient enforcement, legal uncertainty, and regulatory fragmentation across Member States
- Existing rules do not adequately address dark patterns, addictive design, or influencer marketing

## Key Policy Areas

### 1. Dark Patterns

Dark patterns are deceptive design techniques that manipulate users into unintended decisions. The DFA would go beyond DSA Art. 25 (which only covers platform interface manipulation) to address dark patterns across all digital traders.

**Prevalence data:**
- 97% of most popular EU websites/apps deployed at least one dark pattern (Commission 2022 study)
- 75.7% in 2024 EU-wide sweep; 66.8% employed two or more
- Common types: sneak into basket (pre-selected items), confirmshaming (guilt-based language), forced continuity (difficult cancellations), roach motel (easy entry, hard exit), hidden costs, trick questions, bait and switch, misdirection, shadow banning

**Consumer impact:**
- 40% reported confusing website/app design about contract terms
- 66% saw unverified low-stock or high-demand claims
- 61% saw false limited-time availability claims
- 32% paid more due to final price changes
- 48% experienced pressure through repeated requests
- 42% received messages making them doubt cancellation decisions
- 37% encountered visually obscured information

### 2. Addictive Design

Features encouraging excessive engagement or spending, exploiting psychological vulnerabilities similar to gambling addiction mechanisms.

**Key practices:** Infinite scroll, autoplay videos, push notifications, "fear of missing out" mechanics, gamification, personalised recommendations exploiting dopamine responses, streaks, penalties for inactivity.

**Consumer impact:** 31% of consumers reported spending more time or money than intended due to addictive features (autoplay, rewards, penalties for inactivity).

**EP Resolution (December 2023):** Parliament called for:
- Legislation against addictive design, particularly reviewing UCPD, CRD, and Unfair Contract Terms Directive
- "Right Not to Be Disturbed": enable consumers to disable attention-seeking features by default, requiring opt-in with mandatory warnings
- Ethical design standards: chronological feeds, user control, neutral recommendations, automatic time-limits for minors
- Research funding on addictive design effects

### 3. Influencer Marketing

The global influencer market reached USD 24 billion in 2024. The DFA would address regulatory gaps in commercial disclosure, transparency, and platform responsibilities.

**Problematic practices identified:**
- Failing to disclose commercial intent (2024 EU sweep: 97% of reviewed influencers posted commercial content, but only 20% systematically indicated it as advertising)
- 38% failed to use platform-provided labels like "paid partnership"
- Only 36% registered as traders at national level
- Pretending to have brand deals; inflating follower counts with purchased fake followers
- Reinforcing unrealistic beauty standards; promoting harmful or illegal products
- Exploiting child influencers ("kidfluencers") as a new form of child labour
- Accelerating spread of false and misleading claims (63% of creators admit not verifying information before sharing, UNESCO 2024)

**Current EU legal framework (fragmented):**
- UCPD: hidden advertising as misleading omission (Art. 7(2)); Annex I prohibits paid editorial promotion without disclosure (point 11)
- e-Commerce Directive: commercial information must be clearly identifiable (Art. 6)
- AVMSD: audiovisual commercial communications must be readily recognisable; special attention to minors
- DSA: Art. 26 obliges platforms to provide commercial declaration functionalities; Art. 28 prohibits targeting ads at minors; Art. 30 obliges platforms to collect trader identity
- Commission Influencer Legal Hub: five principles for social media marketing disclosures

**National legislation:**
- France (Law 2023-451, June 2023): first dedicated influencer law; mandatory commercial disclosure; "images retouchees"/"images virtuelles" disclaimers; bans on promoting certain products
- Spain (Royal Decree 444/2024, April 2024): high-profile influencers (>1M followers or >EUR 300K revenue) under audiovisual regime; must register nationally
- Italy (AGCOM guidelines, July 2025): binding code of conduct for influencers with >500K followers; standard advertising labels
- Netherlands: Social Media and Influencer Advertising Code; detailed platform-specific disclosure rules
- Belgium: Belgian Advertising Council guidelines (updated August 2025)
- Finland: Consumer Ombudsman updated guidelines (2025)
- Ireland: CCPC guidance (2023)

**Council conclusions (14 May 2024):** Called on Commission and Member States to foster responsible influencer behaviour, support media literacy, and study kidfluencer wellbeing.

### 4. Virtual Currencies, Loot Boxes, and In-App Purchases

In-game purchases using intermediate currencies obscure real costs. Gaming industry in-app/online revenues increased from 74% of video game turnover (2018) to 83% (2022).

**Key concerns:** Loot box mechanics exploiting psychological vulnerabilities; children as particularly vulnerable consumers; opaque pricing through virtual currencies.

### 5. Subscription Traps

Making cancellation as easy as signup. Consumer data: 40% considered cancellation very difficult; 69% found it technically difficult to cancel; 62% experienced automatic renewal of inactive subscriptions without reminders.

### 6. Behavioural Profiling and Personalisation

Preventing AI-driven exploitation of psychological vulnerabilities. 70% of consumers concerned about how their personal data is used (21 percentage point increase since 2018).

## Scope

The DFA would apply more broadly than the DSA, covering:
- E-commerce websites
- App developers
- Digital advertising firms
- Online services beyond content intermediaries
- All digital traders (not just platforms)

## Stakeholder Positions

| Stakeholder | Position |
|-------------|---------|
| BEUC (European Consumer Organisation) | Binding rules on influencers; define "influencer marketing" in law; joint liability across advertising chain; ban influencer marketing for unhealthy food/gambling/alcohol targeting minors; under-15s should not be influencers; standardised EU-wide ad labelling |
| AIM (European Brands Association) | Clear EU definition of "influencer"; shared liability across advertising chain; better consumer tools to flag unsafe/counterfeit products |
| EIMA (Influencer Marketing Alliance) | Practical guidance and stronger enforcement rather than new legislation |
| TikTok | Limited need for further regulatory intervention; opposes overlapping obligations; wants centralised supervisory authority |
| EESC | Prevent minors' access to harmful content; "retouched image"/"virtual image" labels (following French model) |
| Consumer protection authorities | 2024 EU sweep revealed widespread non-compliance; support stronger rules |
