# The European Parliament's Budgetary Powers and the Discharge Procedure

## QUICK FACTS
- **What it is**: the European Parliament is one of the two arms of the EU's budgetary authority (the Council is the other). It co-decides the EU's annual budget under Article 314 TFEU, gives consent to the multiannual financial framework (MFF) under Article 312 TFEU, and alone decides, under Article 319 TFEU, whether to grant "discharge" (formal sign-off) to the Commission and other EU bodies for how they implemented a given year's budget.
- **Legal basis**: Article 314 TFEU (annual budgetary procedure), Article 312 TFEU (MFF, consent procedure), Article 319 TFEU (discharge), Article 318 TFEU (Commission's annual accounts), Article 106a of the Euratom Treaty. Detailed rules: Regulation (EU, Euratom) 2024/2509 (the Financial Regulation, recast) and the Interinstitutional Agreement on budgetary discipline, cooperation in budgetary matters and sound financial management.
- **Who runs it in Parliament**: the **Committee on Budgets (BUDG)**, chaired by Johan Van Overtveldt (ECR, Belgium), handles the annual budget and the MFF. The **Committee on Budgetary Control (CONT)**, chaired by Andreas Schwab (EPP, Germany, elected 24 February 2026, succeeding Niclas Herbst), runs the discharge procedure and oversees anti-fraud coordination (OLAF, EPPO).
- **Annual budget timeline (pragmatic calendar)**: Commission draft budget presented in spring/early summer (formal deadline 1 September under Article 314(2) TFEU); Council position by early autumn (formal deadline 1 October); Parliament has 42 days to read and amend; if Council does not accept the EP reading within 10 days, a **Conciliation Committee** (Council members plus an equal number of MEPs) has 21 days to agree a joint text; final approval follows within 14 days. The whole cycle normally closes in November/December for entry into force on 1 January.
- **Discharge timeline (financial year "n")**: by 31 July of year n+1 the Commission sends its consolidated accounts to Parliament, Council and the European Court of Auditors (ECA); the ECA's Annual Report follows in the autumn; the Council (ECOFIN, qualified majority) adopts a non-binding recommendation around February of year n+2; Parliament, via its CONT committee, votes to grant, postpone or refuse discharge, normally in the April plenary part-session, with a formal deadline of 15 May of year n+2.
- **Key number**: discharge (financial year 2024) was voted in the April 2026 plenary. Parliament granted discharge to the Commission (418 in favour, 207 against, 14 abstentions), all 6 executive agencies, all 33 EU decentralised agencies and the European Public Prosecutor's Office, but again **postponed** discharge for the Council and European Council. Parliament has not granted the Council discharge since 2009 because the Council refuses to submit to the same scrutiny (written questionnaires, hearings) as other institutions.
- **Official sources**: EP Fact Sheet on the budgetary procedure (`https://www.europarl.europa.eu/factsheets/en/sheet/10/the-budgetary-procedure`), EP Fact Sheet on budgetary control / discharge (`https://www.europarl.europa.eu/factsheets/en/sheet/29/budgetary-control`), BUDG committee (`https://www.europarl.europa.eu/committees/en/budg/home`), CONT committee (`https://www.europarl.europa.eu/committees/en/cont/home`), Financial Regulation (EU, Euratom) 2024/2509: `https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32024R2509`.

## Why the Parliament has budgetary powers at all

The EU budget is adopted jointly by two institutions acting as a single "budgetary authority": the European Parliament and the Council of the EU. This is distinct from most EU legislation, where the Commission proposes and Council/Parliament co-decide under the ordinary legislative procedure (see `eu_legislative_procedures_explained`). The budget has its own, separate procedural track under Article 314 TFEU, and its own separate long-term ceiling-setting track for the multiannual financial framework under Article 312 TFEU. On top of both, Parliament holds a third, distinct power that Council does not: it alone grants "discharge" on how the Commission spent the money it was given, under Article 319 TFEU.

These three powers together are why Parliament is often described as holding the EU's "power of the purse": it shapes the annual envelope, it must consent to the seven-year ceiling, and it has the final political word on whether spending was properly managed.

## 1. The annual budgetary procedure (Article 314 TFEU)

### Stage one: the Commission's draft budget
The Commission drafts the annual budget, based on estimates submitted by every EU institution, and forwards it to Parliament and Council. The formal treaty deadline is 1 September of the year before the budget year, but in practice, under a "pragmatic calendar" agreed annually between the three institutions, the Commission tables its draft budget in spring or early summer to give more negotiating time. The Commission can still update the draft up to the point the Conciliation Committee is convened.

### Stage two: the Council's position
The Council adopts its position (acting by qualified majority, see `council_voting_qmv_mechanics`) and forwards it to Parliament, with reasons for any changes from the Commission draft. Formal treaty deadline: 1 October. In practice, Council typically finalises its position by late July.

### Stage three: Parliament's reading
Parliament has 42 days to act. It can:
- Approve the Council's position (or take no decision), in which case the budget is deemed adopted; or
- Adopt amendments by a majority of its component members (not just those voting), which are then forwarded to Council and the Commission.

The **BUDG committee** leads this reading, coordinating with every sectoral committee's rapporteur for its own budget line, before the amendments go to plenary.

### Stage four: Conciliation Committee
If the Council does not approve Parliament's amended text within 10 days, a **Conciliation Committee** is convened. It brings together the members of the Council (or their representatives) and an equal number of MEPs, with the Commission taking part to help reconcile positions. The committee has 21 days to agree a **joint text**, with Council acting by qualified majority and the Parliament's delegation by a majority of its members.

### Stage five: final adoption
If a joint text is agreed, both Parliament (by a majority of votes cast) and Council have 14 days to approve it. If both approve, or if neither formally rejects it, the budget is deemed definitively adopted; the President of the European Parliament signs it into force.

Crucially, Parliament holds a power the Council does not: if the Conciliation Committee fails to reach a joint text, or if Parliament rejects the joint text outright by a majority of its component members, **the whole draft budget is rejected** and the Commission must submit a new draft. This is the sense in which Parliament has the final say over the EU's yearly budget: it is the only one of the two arms of the budgetary authority that can, on its own, kill the whole document and force a restart. If no budget is adopted by 1 January, spending proceeds on a "provisional twelfths" basis (Article 315 TFEU) pending final adoption.

### 2027 budget: current cycle (as at 16 July 2026)
The 2027 budget is the last annual budget under the current 2021-2027 MFF. Commissioner Piotr Serafin presented the Commission's draft 2027 budget to the BUDG committee on 10 June 2026, formally launching the Article 314 TFEU procedure. Parliament had already adopted its guidelines for the 2027 budget (BUDG report, adopted 5 March 2026, 20 in favour, 6 against, 2 abstentions; confirmed in plenary). By mid-July 2026 Coreper was expected to approve the Council's draft position. See `mff_2028_2034` for how the 2027 annual budget interacts with the parallel, much larger negotiation over the post-2027 MFF ceiling.

## 3. Parliament's consent role in the MFF (Article 312 TFEU)

The annual budget must stay within the ceilings set by the multiannual financial framework (MFF), the EU's 7-year spending plan. The MFF Regulation is adopted under the **special legislative (consent) procedure**: the Council must agree unanimously, and Parliament can only approve or reject the whole package, it cannot amend it. This is a materially different (weaker, in one sense) power than the annual budget, where Parliament negotiates and amends line by line. In exchange, in modern practice the Council and Commission negotiate informally with Parliament's BUDG rapporteurs well before the final unanimous Council vote, precisely because Parliament's up-or-down veto over the whole MFF gives it real leverage even without formal amendment rights. See `mff_2028_2034` for the ongoing MFF 2028-2034 negotiation and `eu_own_resources_reform_2028_2034` for the parallel revenue-side reform that determines how the EU pays for that ceiling.

## 4. The discharge procedure (Article 319 TFEU)

### What discharge is
Discharge is the EU's annual "accounts closed and approved" moment. Under Article 319 TFEU, the European Parliament, acting on a recommendation from the Council, decides whether the Commission (and, separately, every other EU institution, decentralised agency, executive agency and joint undertaking) properly implemented its section of the budget for a given financial year. It is the accountability bookend to the annual budgetary procedure described above: first Parliament and Council decide how much money can be spent and on what (Article 314 TFEU), then, roughly 16 months later, Parliament decides whether the money was actually spent properly (Article 319 TFEU).

### Legal basis and documents
Article 319 TFEU is implemented in detail by the Financial Regulation (Regulation (EU, Euratom) 2024/2509, Articles 260-263) and Annex V of Parliament's Rules of Procedure. The core evidence base is:
- The Commission's **integrated financial and accountability reporting package** (consolidated accounts, sent to Parliament, Council and the European Court of Auditors by 31 July of year n+1, per Article 318 TFEU);
- The **European Court of Auditors' Annual Report**, including its Statement of Assurance (DAS) on the reliability of the accounts and legality/regularity of underlying transactions, published in the autumn following the financial year;
- The Council's **recommendation**, adopted by qualified majority in its Ecofin configuration, usually around February of year n+2;
- CONT committee reports built from written questionnaires to the Commission, hearings with individual Commissioners, and, where relevant, special ECA reports on specific spending areas.

### Timeline for financial year "n"
1. **By 31 July, year n+1**: Commission adopts and transmits its consolidated accounts.
2. **Autumn, year n+1**: ECA publishes its Annual Report (and Statement of Assurance).
3. **~February, year n+2**: Council (Ecofin, QMV) adopts its recommendation to Parliament.
4. **By 15 May, year n+2** (in practice, the April plenary part-session): Parliament, on the basis of CONT's reports, votes to **grant**, **postpone** or **refuse** discharge.
5. **If postponed**: CONT prepares a fresh report addressing the reasons for postponement; Parliament revotes, typically in October of year n+2, and must then either grant or refuse discharge.
6. **If refused**: a later part-session considers a formal proposal to close the accounts for year n, and the Commission (or other body concerned) is invited to make a statement to plenary.

### Who is discharged
Discharge decisions are voted separately for: the European Commission (the headline vote); the European Parliament's own administration; the other institutions (Council, European Council, Court of Justice of the EU, European Court of Auditors, European Economic and Social Committee, Committee of the Regions, European Ombudsman, European Data Protection Supervisor, European External Action Service); the European Public Prosecutor's Office; the 6 Commission executive agencies; all EU decentralised (regulatory) agencies (33 as of 2026); and joint undertakings. Discharge for the European Development Funds (EDFs) is voted separately because the EDFs sit outside the general EU budget.

### The Council problem
Since 2009, Parliament has not granted discharge to the Council/European Council. The dispute is structural: the Council does not consider itself bound to answer CONT's written questionnaires or attend hearings on the same terms as the Commission and other institutions, and Parliament regards this as non-cooperation with its Article 319 TFEU oversight role. Each year CONT recommends postponement (rather than an outright refusal, which would be a stronger political signal) and the cycle repeats. The 2024 financial-year discharge, voted in the April 2026 plenary, followed exactly this pattern: discharge granted to the Commission and all other institutions/agencies, postponed again for the Council and European Council.

### Why discharge is political leverage
Discharge is one of the very few points in the EU calendar where Parliament passes a binding, individualised political verdict on the Commission's conduct, separate from any legislative file. Because the vote and its accompanying resolution are adopted by simple majority of votes cast (not a supermajority), Parliament can use the accompanying resolution to extract commitments even while granting discharge. In the 2024 discharge (April 2026), MEPs approved the Commission's management (418-207-14) but paired the vote with a resolution warning about rule-of-law backsliding and systemic corruption in several member states, and pressed the Commission on the lack of reliable data on final recipients of Recovery and Resilience Facility (RRF) funding. Rapporteur Daniel Freund (Greens/EFA, Germany) described the vote as sending "a clear and unequivocal message on accountability and the rule of law." A **refusal** of discharge (rare, and never yet applied to the Commission itself in the EU's history, though threatened in the 1990s and directly triggering the resignation of the Santer Commission in 1999) would be a far graver political event, effectively a vote of no confidence in the management of EU funds. Even the routine tool of **postponement** functions as leverage: it keeps a body (most often the Council) formally under a cloud of unresolved parliamentary scrutiny for another six months, and forces a fresh CONT report addressing the specific objections raised.

## Committees: BUDG and CONT

Parliament splits its budgetary work between two standing committees (full list of committees: `ep_committees_overview`):

- **BUDG (Committee on Budgets)**: leads the annual budgetary procedure (Article 314 TFEU) and the MFF consent procedure (Article 312 TFEU), plus own-resources legislation. Chaired by Johan Van Overtveldt (ECR, Belgium). Every sectoral committee appoints a rapporteur for its own section of the budget, but BUDG owns the overall reading and negotiates the Conciliation Committee's Parliament-side mandate.
- **CONT (Committee on Budgetary Control)**: leads the discharge procedure (Article 319 TFEU), oversees the EU's anti-fraud framework (coordinating with OLAF, the European Anti-Fraud Office, and EPPO, the European Public Prosecutor's Office), and scrutinises Court of Auditors special reports year-round, not only in the discharge season. Chaired by Andreas Schwab (EPP, Germany) since 24 February 2026.

BUDG and CONT increasingly meet jointly on cross-cutting issues: on 14 July 2026, for example, the two committees held a joint exchange of views on the Commission's plan to unlock up to EUR 16 billion in EU funds for Hungary, a question that touches both ongoing budget execution (CONT's remit) and the political conditions attached to future spending (BUDG's remit).
