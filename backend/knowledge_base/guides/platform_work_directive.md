# Platform Work Directive (Directive (EU) 2024/2831)

## QUICK FACTS
- Full title: Directive (EU) 2024/2831 of the European Parliament and of the Council of 23 October 2024 on improving working conditions in platform work
- CELEX: 32024L2831. Adopted: 23 October 2024. Published in the Official Journal: 11 November 2024. Entered into force: 1 December 2024
- Transposition deadline for Member States: **2 December 2026**
- Legal basis: Article 153(1)(b) and 153(2) TFEU (working conditions, ordinary legislative procedure)
- Two pillars: (1) correct determination of employment status via a rebuttable legal presumption of an employment relationship; (2) rules on algorithmic management (transparency, human oversight, data protection limits, worker representative consultation)
- Legal presumption: triggered when facts indicating "control and direction" are present, per criteria Member States must set (the Commission's negotiated text lists five indicative control criteria; national law may add more). The burden of proof shifts to the platform to rebut the presumption
- Scope: covers people performing platform work through a digital labour platform operating in the EU, regardless of formally stated contractual status (genuinely self-employed platform workers ARE covered by the algorithmic-management provisions, even where the employment-status presumption does not apply to them)
- Estimated affected population: the Commission's impact assessment put the EU platform economy at roughly 28-30 million people (2024 estimate), with growth projected to around 43 million by 2025; between 1.7 and 4.1 million people are estimated to be misclassified as self-employed
- Algorithmic management restrictions cover automated monitoring and automated decision-making systems (ADM); platforms are barred from processing certain categories of personal data (emotional or psychological state, private conversations, biometric data other than for authentication, data to infer race/ethnic origin, migration status, political opinions, religious beliefs, health status, or trade union activity)
- Right to human review: workers can request human review of significant automated decisions (account restriction, suspension, deactivation, decisions affecting pay or contractual status); a human contact point must be designated
- Responsible DG: DG EMPL (Employment, Social Affairs and Inclusion). EP lead committee: EMPL. Council configuration: EPSCO
- Companion Brubru guide: `employment_future_of_work.md` carries this directive plus the wider EU labour-policy landscape (ESF+, Youth Guarantee, AI Act interplay, Posting of Workers Directive). This guide is the deep-dive on Directive (EU) 2024/2831 specifically

Directive (EU) 2024/2831 is the EU's first binding instrument aimed squarely at digital labour platforms: ride-hailing, food delivery, care and cleaning platforms, freelance/task marketplaces and similar gig-economy intermediaries. It responds to two connected problems the Commission identified through 2021-2024 consultations and impact assessment: widespread bogus self-employment (platform workers formally classified as independent contractors while being subject to platform control comparable to employees) and the largely unregulated use of algorithms to allocate tasks, set prices, monitor performance and terminate access to the platform.

The file was politically contentious for over two years, largely over how prescriptive the employment-status presumption should be. France pushed for a weaker, more discretionary presumption; Belgium (holding the Council presidency at the time of the final deal) and Spain pushed for a stronger, more automatic one. The final compromise leaves the operative criteria for triggering the presumption to Member States, while requiring that the presumption exist, be rebuttable only by the platform, and never be used to worsen a worker's situation or to disapply the directive to genuinely self-employed people.

## Pillar 1: Employment Status Presumption

### Mechanism

- A **rebuttable legal presumption** of an employment relationship applies where facts indicate that a digital labour platform is exercising "control and direction" over how work is performed.
- Member States must lay down a national framework for how the presumption is triggered, using criteria reflecting control and direction. The negotiated text points Member States towards indicative control criteria such as:
  - the platform sets or caps the level of remuneration;
  - the platform electronically supervises or monitors the performance of work (including by algorithmic means);
  - the platform effectively restricts how the person organises their work (hours, availability, presence, appearance);
  - the platform effectively restricts the ability to work for other parties or to build a client base of one's own;
  - the platform effectively restricts the ability to use subcontractors or substitutes to perform the work.
- Presumption applies as soon as facts indicating control and direction are established under the applicable national trigger mechanism; Member States decide the exact threshold (a common approach used in national implementations discussed at Council was requiring at least two of the above criteria to be met, though the directive text itself leaves this operationalisation to national law).
- **Burden of proof**: once the presumption is triggered, it is for the platform, not the worker, to prove that the contractual relationship is not an employment relationship. This reverses the default burden that usually sits with the worker in misclassification disputes.
- The presumption also applies in **administrative and judicial proceedings** concerning platform workers' employment status, including in the context of labour inspections and tax/social security enforcement.
- Member States must ensure the presumption does not lead to reclassification obligations being placed on the platform worker themselves, and must provide accessible, effective procedures (including free legal aid where relevant) for platform workers, their representatives and labour/social security authorities to invoke the presumption.
- Reclassification does not automatically retroactively convert every past period of platform work into employment; national law governs the effects on prior periods, but Member States must ensure workers are not worse off.

### Anti-retaliation and enforcement

- Platforms are prohibited from dismissing, deactivating, or otherwise penalising a worker for exercising rights under the directive (raising a reclassification claim, requesting human review, exercising data protection rights).
- Member States must designate or reinforce competent authorities (labour inspectorates, social security bodies) with access to platform data needed to establish the existence of an employment relationship and the identity of the persons performing platform work, including in cross-border situations involving posted or mobile platform workers.
- Digital labour platforms must declare platform work to the competent national authorities in accordance with national law and practice.

## Pillar 2: Algorithmic Management

This pillar applies more broadly than Pillar 1: it covers **all** people performing platform work, including genuinely self-employed platform workers who are not captured by the employment-status presumption.

### Transparency obligations

Platforms must inform platform workers (and their representatives, and job applicants) in writing about:
- the existence and use of automated monitoring systems and automated decision-making (ADM) systems;
- the categories of decisions taken or supported by such systems (task allocation, pricing, working-time organisation, instructions, performance evaluation, restriction/suspension/deactivation of accounts);
- the main parameters such systems use and how a worker's conduct or performance affects those decisions;
- the grounds for decisions to restrict, suspend or terminate the worker's account, or refuse remuneration for work performed.

### Human oversight and human review

- Platforms must ensure **human monitoring** of the impact of ADM systems on working conditions, health and safety, and fundamental rights, using personnel who are adequately trained and have the authority, competence and independence to override automated decisions.
- Platform workers have the **right to obtain an explanation** from the platform for any significant decision taken or supported by an ADM system that affects them.
- Platform workers have the **right to request human review** of decisions that restrict, suspend or terminate their account, that refuse remuneration, or that affect their contractual status; a natural person at the platform must respond within a reasonable period, with a statement of reasons; if the decision is found incorrect, it must be rectified without delay, including compensation where required by national law.
- Decisions to suspend or terminate an account must not be based solely on automated processing; a human must be involved.

### Restricted categories of personal data

Digital labour platforms are prohibited from processing, by automated means, personal data concerning:
- emotional or psychological state of the platform worker;
- private conversations, including with other workers or with worker representatives;
- data to predict trade union or worker-representative activity, or to identify individual union membership, or to take action against workers on that basis;
- data to infer racial or ethnic origin, migration status, political opinions, religious or philosophical beliefs, disability, state of health (including chronic illness or HIV status), or emotional or psychological state;
- biometric data, other than for the strict purposes of authentication of the worker's access to the platform's account.
- health data beyond the period a worker is not available for work, and no personal data once the contractual relationship has ended (subject to legal retention obligations).

This is a lex specialis layered on top of the GDPR: platforms remain fully subject to Regulation (EU) 2016/679 for any processing not specifically restricted here, and the directive's Article on data protection must be read together with GDPR Articles 5, 6, 9 and 22 (automated individual decision-making). See `gdpr_data_protection.md`.

### Information and consultation of worker representatives

- Platforms must inform and consult worker representatives (or, absent representatives, the workers directly) before introducing or materially changing automated monitoring or ADM systems that could substantially affect working conditions, health and safety, or fundamental rights.
- Worker representatives may be assisted by an expert of their choice, at the platform's reasonable expense where the platform is an SME-exempt threshold does not apply, subject to national implementing rules.

## Interplay with Other EU Instruments

| Instrument | Relationship to the Platform Work Directive |
|-----------|----------------------------------------------|
| GDPR (Regulation (EU) 2016/679) | Baseline data protection law; the directive adds sector-specific processing restrictions and transparency duties on top |
| AI Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689) | AI systems used for recruitment, task allocation, performance evaluation or termination in an employment context are classified high-risk (Annex III); platform ADM systems will typically also need to meet AI Act risk-management, data-governance and human-oversight requirements |
| Transparent and Predictable Working Conditions Directive (EU) 2019/1152 | General baseline information rights for all workers; the Platform Work Directive adds platform-specific algorithmic transparency on top |
| Posting of Workers Directive 96/71/EC (amended by (EU) 2018/957) | Relevant where platform work is cross-border; competent-authority data-access and cooperation duties in the Platform Work Directive support enforcement of posting rules for mobile platform workers |
| Working Time Directive 2003/88/EC | Once a platform worker is reclassified as an employee, standard working-time protections apply |

## Transposition and Implementation Watch

- Deadline: **2 December 2026**. Member States must communicate the text of the transposing measures to the Commission.
- Key national discretion points to watch during transposition: the precise operational criteria/threshold for triggering the employment-status presumption; which authority is designated competent for reclassification disputes; sanctions regime for non-compliant platforms; whether SMEs get any adapted timelines for algorithmic-management information/consultation duties.
- Through 2025, Member State experts and social partners held technical exchanges (coordinated with DG EMPL) on transposition options; national labour ministries in several Member States (including Belgium, Spain, France, Germany, Italy) had draft or consultation-stage implementing measures under discussion.
- The European Parliament's EMPL committee began monitoring implementation from May 2025 onward, tracking Member State transposition progress against the December 2026 deadline.
- Non-transposition or late/incorrect transposition exposes Member States to standard Article 258 TFEU infringement action by the Commission after the deadline passes.
