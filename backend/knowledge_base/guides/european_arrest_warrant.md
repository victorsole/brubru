# European Arrest Warrant: Framework Decision 2002/584/JHA

## QUICK FACTS
- Legal basis: Council Framework Decision 2002/584/JHA of 13 June 2002 on the European arrest warrant and the surrender procedures between Member States (CELEX 32002F0584), amended by Council Framework Decision 2009/299/JHA (in absentia trials)
- Operational since: 1 January 2004, replacing traditional bilateral extradition between EU Member States
- Mechanism: judicial surrender based on mutual recognition of judicial decisions, not a political/diplomatic extradition request
- 32 offence categories (Article 2(2)): no double-criminality check required if punishable by a maximum of at least 3 years' imprisonment in the issuing state (list includes terrorism, trafficking in human beings, corruption, cybercrime, murder, organised crime, among others)
- Non-listed offences: double criminality can still be required as an optional ground for refusal
- Time limits: 10 days for a final surrender decision if the person consents; 60 days if not (extendable by a further 30 days in exceptional circumstances); physical surrender within 10 days of the final decision
- 2023 operational data: average time to surrender was 19.93 days with consent and 60.12 days without; roughly 14,071 warrants issued and 5,450 executed across the EU
- Mandatory non-execution grounds (Article 3): amnesty covering the offence in the executing state, prior final judgment for the same acts (ne bis in idem), person below the age of criminal responsibility in the executing state
- Optional non-execution grounds (Article 4): lack of double criminality for non-listed offences, ongoing prosecution in the executing state for the same act, statute of limitations expired, territorial jurisdiction issues, in absentia judgments without adequate guarantees (post-2009/299/JHA)
- Own nationals: EAW abolished the traditional bar on extraditing nationals; Member States may condition surrender of own nationals/residents on return to serve any sentence in the executing state
- Proportionality: not a formal ground for refusal in the text, but flagged repeatedly by the Commission and Council as a practical concern (EAWs issued for minor offences straining resources)
- Key CJEU case law: Aranyosi and Caldararu (C-404/15 and C-659/15 PPU, 2016): real risk of inhuman/degrading detention conditions can justify postponing/refusing surrender; LM/Celmer (C-216/18 PPU, 2018) and Openbaar Ministerie (C-354/20 PPU and C-412/20 PPU, 2020): systemic rule-of-law/judicial-independence deficiencies in the issuing state require a two-step individualised assessment before refusal; Melloni (C-399/11, 2013): Member States cannot use national fundamental-rights standards to refuse surrender for in-absentia convictions that meet EU minimum guarantees; OG and PI (C-508/18 and C-82/19 PPU, 2019): a "issuing judicial authority" must be independent of the executive, excluding some prosecutor's offices subject to ministerial instruction
- Complementary bodies: Eurojust facilitates consultation between issuing and executing judicial authorities, advises on competing warrants, and monitors time-limit compliance; the European Judicial Network (EJN) maintains the Atlas and the "Fiches Belges" practical guidance on national implementing rules
- Post-Brexit: the EAW no longer applies to the United Kingdom; surrender between the EU and the UK now runs under Part Three, Title VII of the EU-UK Trade and Cooperation Agreement, a similar but distinct mechanism (includes an explicit proportionality test and a nationality-refusal option some Member States use)

## What the European Arrest Warrant Is

The European Arrest Warrant (EAW) is the EU's flagship instrument of judicial cooperation in criminal matters. Established by Council Framework Decision 2002/584/JHA, adopted swiftly after the 11 September 2001 attacks and operational from 1 January 2004, it replaced the slow, politically mediated extradition treaties that previously governed surrender of suspects and convicts between EU Member States.

The defining shift is from **extradition** (a state-to-state, often ministerially-approved process) to **surrender** (a judicial authority-to-judicial authority process). A judicial authority in one Member State issues a warrant that is directly enforceable across the whole EU; the executing judicial authority in the state where the person is found decides whether to hand them over, based on a closed and harmonised list of grounds for refusal, not on a fresh assessment of the underlying case.

## The 32-Offence List and Double Criminality

Article 2(2) of the Framework Decision lists 32 categories of offence (including participation in a criminal organisation, terrorism, trafficking in human beings, corruption, fraud, money laundering, cybercrime, murder, illicit trafficking in weapons, and rape) for which the executing state cannot refuse surrender on the ground that the conduct is not a crime under its own law, provided the offence carries a maximum sentence of at least three years' imprisonment in the issuing state. For any offence outside this list, the executing state may still insist on double criminality (the act must be an offence in both states) as an optional ground to refuse.

## Grounds for Non-Execution

**Mandatory grounds (Article 3):** the offence is covered by an amnesty in the executing state; the person has already been finally judged for the same acts by a Member State (ne bis in idem); the person is below the age of criminal responsibility in the executing state.

**Optional grounds (Article 4):** absence of double criminality for non-listed offences; the person is already being prosecuted in the executing state for the same act; the statute of limitations has expired under the executing state's law; territorial jurisdiction disputes where the offence was partly or wholly committed in the executing state; and, following the 2009/299/JHA amendment, judgments rendered in absentia without adequate procedural guarantees having been offered to the person.

## Time Limits and Procedure

The Framework Decision sets tight deadlines to make surrender genuinely faster than extradition: if the requested person consents to surrender, the executing judicial authority must take a final decision within 10 days; without consent, the deadline is 60 days, extendable by a further 30 days in exceptional cases. Physical handover must then occur within 10 days of the final decision. In practice (2023 data), the EU-wide average was 19.93 days for consensual surrenders and 60.12 days for contested ones, against roughly 14,071 warrants issued and 5,450 executed that year.

## Own Nationals

Unlike classic extradition treaties, which frequently barred surrender of a state's own nationals, the EAW abolished that blanket exception. Member States may still attach a condition: several require that, if a national or resident is surrendered for prosecution, they be returned to serve any custodial sentence in the executing (home) state rather than the issuing state.

## Proportionality Concerns

The Framework Decision does not include a formal proportionality test as a ground for refusal, which has drawn sustained criticism from the Commission, the Council and civil-liberties groups: EAWs have at times been issued for comparatively minor offences (theft of low-value goods, minor drug possession), consuming judicial and detention resources disproportionate to the offence. The Council has issued non-binding guidance encouraging issuing authorities to weigh proportionality before issuing a warrant, but this remains a soft-law recommendation rather than a Framework Decision amendment.

## Key CJEU Case Law

- **Aranyosi and Caldararu** (Joined Cases C-404/15 and C-659/15 PPU, 5 April 2016): the CJEU held that where there is a real risk that the requested person would face inhuman or degrading treatment because of detention conditions in the issuing state, the executing authority must postpone the surrender decision and seek further information before deciding whether to refuse it.
- **Minister for Justice and Equality v LM ("Celmer")** (Case C-216/18 PPU, 25 July 2018) and **Openbaar Ministerie** (Joined Cases C-354/20 PPU and C-412/20 PPU, 17 December 2020): the Court extended the Aranyosi logic to systemic deficiencies in judicial independence and the rule of law in the issuing state, requiring a two-step test: first, whether there is a real, systemic risk of breach of the fundamental right to a fair trial, and second, an individualised assessment of whether that risk actually applies to the requested person's case.
- **Melloni** (Case C-399/11, 26 February 2013): a Member State cannot invoke its own, higher national constitutional standard of fundamental-rights protection to refuse surrender for an in-absentia conviction that already satisfies the EU's harmonised minimum guarantees under the 2009/299/JHA amendment; EU law primacy prevails.
- **OG and PI (Public Prosecutor's Offices of Lübeck and Zwickau)** (Joined Cases C-508/18 and C-82/19 PPU, 27 May 2019): the concept of "issuing judicial authority" under Article 6(1) requires an authority that acts independently of the executive branch; a public prosecutor's office exposed to individual instructions from a government ministry does not qualify, a ruling that forced several Member States to change which body issues their EAWs.

## Complementary Bodies

**Eurojust** facilitates practical cooperation on EAW cases: it helps resolve competing warrants issued by different Member States for the same person, provides legal information and translation support, flags cases where statutory time limits are at risk of being breached, and helps clarify or correct incomplete or contradictory warrant documentation.

**The European Judicial Network (EJN)** maintains the EAW Atlas, a directory of competent issuing and executing authorities in every Member State, alongside the "Fiches Belges", practical country-by-country notes on how each Member State has transposed the Framework Decision into national law, procedural quirks, and points of contact.

## Post-Brexit: EU-UK Surrender

Since the United Kingdom's withdrawal from the EU, the EAW no longer applies to the UK. Surrender between EU Member States and the UK is now governed by Part Three, Title VII of the EU-UK Trade and Cooperation Agreement (TCA). The TCA mechanism mirrors much of the EAW's structure (judicial surrender, a broadly similar offence-based framework, defined time limits) but is legally distinct: it includes an explicit proportionality test that issuing authorities must apply, and several Member States (including Germany and others with constitutional protections for nationals) have used a nationality-based refusal option that is not available under the EAW itself.
