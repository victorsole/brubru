# EU Entry/Exit System (EES): Regulation 2017/2226

## QUICK FACTS

- **LATEST (July 2026: EES fully operational, summer strain reported):** The Entry/Exit System reached **full operational status on 10 April 2026**, ending the progressive rollout that began on 12 October 2025. Since then, the European Commission has reported that **more than 40,000 third-country nationals have been refused entry** at Schengen external borders on EES-related grounds (expired or fraudulent documents, inability to justify the purpose of stay). During the 2026 summer travel peak, several major airport hubs reported extended queues and biometric-processing bottlenecks as EES load reached full volume; the Commission and Member States activated emergency flexibility measures allowing temporary local adjustments to EES procedures where congestion threatens an airport's functional viability. Verify current queue/delay reports against national border authority advisories before citing specific airports.
- **Instrument:** Regulation (EU) 2017/2226 (CELEX 32017R2226), OJ L 327, 9.12.2017, pp. 20-82; establishes the Entry/Exit System (EES)
- **Full title:** Regulation of the European Parliament and of the Council establishing an Entry/Exit System (EES) to register entry and exit data and refusal of entry data of third-country nationals crossing the external borders of the Member States and determining the conditions for access to the EES for law enforcement purposes, and amending the Convention implementing the Schengen Agreement and Regulations (EC) No 767/2008 and (EU) No 1077/2011
- **Adopted:** 30 November 2017
- **Entered into force:** 29 December 2017
- **Application:** Delayed for years pending the technical build; progressive start of operations began 12 October 2025 under a temporary derogating regulation, reaching full operation 10 April 2026
- **Progressive-start regulation:** Regulation (EU) 2025/1534 of 18 July 2025 (temporary derogations from Regulations (EU) 2017/2226 and (EU) 2016/399 to allow a phased, milestone-based EES launch); entered into force 26 July 2025. (Note: this replaces an earlier hard "big bang" start date model; there is no Regulation 2024/2667 in the EES file, do not cite that number.)
- **Companion instrument:** Regulation (EU) 2017/2225 amended the Schengen Borders Code (Regulation (EU) 2016/399) the same day to integrate EES checks into external-border-crossing procedures
- **Who is covered:** Third-country nationals making short stays (90 days in any 180-day period) at the Schengen external borders, both visa-required and visa-exempt travellers. EU/EEA/Swiss citizens, holders of residence permits/long-stay visas, and other persons enjoying the EU right of free movement are NOT registered in EES
- **What is captured:** Traveller's name and travel-document data, a facial image, four fingerprints (for most travellers; some exemptions apply, e.g. children under 12 and persons physically unable to give fingerprints), date/time/place of entry and exit, and refusal-of-entry data
- **What it replaces:** Manual passport stamping at Schengen external borders for in-scope travellers
- **Core function:** Automatically calculates the traveller's authorised stay and remaining days, and generates overstayer alerts to national authorities when authorised stay has expired
- **Retention period:** 3 years from the date of the last entry/exit or refusal-of-entry record; extended to 5 years for a traveller flagged as an overstayer
- **Registration channels:** Border-guard booth, self-service kiosks pre-registering passport/biometric data before reaching the booth, and the official "Travel to Europe" mobile app (partial pre-registration of passport and facial data only; fingerprints must still be captured at the physical border). Kiosk/app pre-registration is voluntary and being trialled/expanded market by market (e.g. Sweden and Portugal for the app as of 2025-2026)
- **Managing agency:** eu-LISA (EU Agency for the Operational Management of Large-Scale IT Systems in the Area of Freedom, Security and Justice)
- **Interoperability:** EES is one of the large-scale EU border/security IT systems (alongside VIS, SIS, ETIAS, Eurodac and ECRIS-TCN) linked through the interoperability framework (Regulations (EU) 2019/817 and 2019/818) via the shared European Search Portal (ESP), Common Identity Repository (CIR), shared Biometric Matching Service (sBMS) and Multiple-Identity Detector (MID)
- **Coverage:** Operated across the Schengen area external borders; participating countries include the Schengen Member States plus the non-EU Schengen Associated Countries (Iceland, Liechtenstein, Norway, Switzerland). Ireland and Cyprus are outside the Schengen border-check area and are not part of EES in the same way

## Context: from Smart Borders to a working system

The Entry/Exit System has one of the longest gestation periods of any Schengen-area IT instrument. The European Commission first proposed a "Smart Borders Package" in 2013, withdrew it after criticism of cost and technical feasibility, and re-proposed a revised package on 6 April 2016. Regulation (EU) 2017/2226 was adopted on 30 November 2017 and entered into force on 29 December 2017, but its actual application was made conditional on the Commission certifying that the underlying IT system was technically ready, a threshold that was repeatedly missed through 2022, 2023 and 2024 owing to delays in the central system build managed by eu-LISA and in national border-infrastructure readiness (kiosks, e-gates, connectivity to the Visa Information System).

To break the cycle of further blanket delays, the co-legislators adopted Regulation (EU) 2025/1534 on 18 July 2025. Rather than a single "go live" date across all border crossing points simultaneously, this regulation authorised a **milestone-based progressive start**: operations began on 12 October 2025, with escalating minimum thresholds of border crossings recorded in EES (with and without biometrics) over the following six months, reaching full operation across all Schengen external border crossing points by **10 April 2026**. The gradual roll-out was voluntary in pace: Member States were free to move faster than the minimum milestones, and several did.

## What EES Registers

### Data categories (Article 16-20)

- Surname, forename(s), date of birth, nationality, sex, travel document type and number
- Facial image captured live at the border (or, for visa holders, retrieved from VIS)
- Four fingerprints (visa-exempt travellers; visa holders' fingerprints are retrieved from the Visa Information System rather than recaptured)
- Date, time and border crossing point of each entry and exit
- Refusal-of-entry data: date, border crossing point, and grounds for refusal
- Entities responsible for the entry, and any authority that carried out an identity check

### Who is exempt from fingerprinting

- Children under the age of 12
- Persons for whom fingerprinting is physically impossible
- Heads of State/government and members of official delegations in specified circumstances, where fingerprinting is waived under Article 16(2)

## The Two Functions Underneath the Registration

### 1. Overstay calculation and alerts

EES automatically calculates the remaining days of the 90/180-day short-stay allowance for each in-scope traveller. Where a traveller's authorised stay has expired without an exit record, the system generates a list of overstayers, which is transmitted to the competent national authorities for follow-up (Article 12). This is the central operational rationale for EES: under manual stamping, an unrecorded, ambiguous, or illegible stamp made overstay detection unreliable.

### 2. Identity and document verification

Because EES stores biometric templates, border guards and (for law-enforcement access requests) national authorities can verify a traveller's identity against previously captured data, and check travel-document validity and prior refusals of entry, reducing reliance on document inspection alone.

---

## Access and Law Enforcement Use

EES data is primarily used by border and immigration authorities for border-management purposes. Chapter IV of the Regulation sets out a separate, more restrictive regime for law-enforcement access: national police and Europol may query EES data for the prevention, detection or investigation of terrorist offences or other serious crime, but only where prior searches of national and other EU databases have not resolved the query, and subject to a designated authority and verifying authority structure with judicial or independent administrative authorisation in each Member State (Articles 29-33).

## Relationship to Other Systems

- **VIS (Visa Information System, Regulation (EC) 767/2008):** EES and VIS are interconnected so that visa holders' fingerprints and photograph on file in VIS do not need to be recaptured at first EES registration; entry/exit and overstay data flow both ways for consistency checks.
- **ETIAS (Regulation (EU) 2018/1240):** ETIAS pre-screens visa-exempt third-country nationals before travel; EES is the border-crossing registration layer that then records their actual entries and exits and enforces the authorised-stay calculation ETIAS relies on.
- **SIS (Schengen Information System):** consulted at the same border check for alerts (refusals, wanted persons, lost/stolen documents); EES and SIS remain operationally distinct but are cross-searchable through the interoperability components.
- **Interoperability framework (Regulations (EU) 2019/817 and 2019/818):** EES, VIS, ETIAS, SIS, Eurodac and ECRIS-TCN are linked via the European Search Portal, the shared Biometric Matching Service, the Common Identity Repository and the Multiple-Identity Detector, allowing a single query to check a traveller's biometric and biographic data across all connected systems and flag identity fraud or multiple identities.
- **eu-LISA:** develops and operates the EES central system and its interfaces with the national border infrastructure of every participating country, alongside VIS, SIS, ETIAS, Eurodac and ECRIS-TCN.

## Timeline

- **2013** -- first "Smart Borders Package" proposed, later withdrawn
- **6 April 2016** -- revised Smart Borders Package proposed, including EES
- **30 November 2017** -- Regulation (EU) 2017/2226 adopted
- **29 December 2017** -- entered into force
- **2018-2024** -- technical build by eu-LISA and Member States; repeated delays to the "start of operations" date originally expected around 2022
- **18 July 2025** -- Regulation (EU) 2025/1534 adopted, establishing the progressive-start mechanism
- **26 July 2025** -- Regulation (EU) 2025/1534 entered into force
- **12 October 2025** -- EES operations began (progressive start, first milestone: minimum share of crossings recorded within one month)
- **10 April 2026** -- EES reached full operational status across all Schengen external border crossing points; passport stamping ends for in-scope travellers
- **Ongoing (2026)** -- operational bedding-in through the summer travel peak; flexibility measures available where local congestion threatens airport functionality

## Practical Effects for Travellers and Operators

- **First registration takes longer.** A traveller's first EES crossing after go-live requires biometric capture (facial image plus fingerprints), adding processing time compared with a passport stamp. Subsequent crossings, where biometrics are already on file, are faster.
- **No more physical stamps.** Border officers, carriers and immigration authorities can no longer rely on stamp pages to establish a traveller's Schengen history; the authoritative record is now the EES entry/exit log.
- **Airports and land/sea crossing points needed new infrastructure.** Self-service kiosks, biometric e-gates and connectivity upgrades were required at every external border crossing point, which is the main reason the "start of operations" date slipped for years before the 2025 progressive-start compromise.
- **Voluntary pre-registration reduces queue time.** Kiosks and the "Travel to Europe" app let travellers submit some data before reaching the booth, though full biometric capture for first-time registrants still requires an in-person step.
