## Context

The DSA replaced the 20-year-old liability framework of the 2000 e-Commerce Directive with a comprehensive, tiered system of due diligence obligations. The Commission proposed it on 15 December 2020 alongside the Digital Markets Act. The political impetus was twofold: (a) fragmentation, as Member States were legislating diverging national rules on content moderation and disinformation; and (b) accountability, as the liability exemptions that allowed online intermediaries to scale had outpaced regulatory oversight.

The DSA is a regulation and is directly applicable. Member States may not add national requirements in areas it fully harmonises. It applies to any provider with a "substantial connection" to the Union, regardless of where that provider is established.

---

## Structure: Four Tiers of Intermediary Services

The DSA uses a layered, asymmetric framework. Each tier inherits all the obligations of the tier below it.

**Tier 1 -- All intermediary services (Arts 9-15):** Mere conduit, caching and hosting providers, however small. Basic obligations on responding to authority orders, transparency reporting, terms and conditions, and points of contact.

**Tier 2 -- Hosting services (Arts 16-18):** Services that store content at user request (web hosting, cloud services, social media, app stores, marketplaces). Additional obligations: notice-and-action mechanism, statement of reasons for content restrictions, and notification of life-threatening criminal content to law enforcement.

**Tier 3 -- Online platforms (Arts 19-28):** Hosting services that disseminate stored content to the public, such as social networks, video-sharing platforms, app stores and online marketplaces. Additional obligations: internal complaint-handling, out-of-court dispute settlement, trusted flagger prioritisation, prohibition on dark patterns, advertising transparency, recommender system transparency, minor protection.

**Tier 4 -- VLOPs and VLOSEs (Arts 33-43):** Online platforms and online search engines with at least 45 million average monthly active recipients in the Union, designated by Commission decision. Additional obligations: annual systemic risk assessment and mitigation, independent annual audit, non-profiling recommender option, ad repository, data access for vetted researchers, independent compliance function, enhanced transparency reporting, supervisory fees.

Online platforms that operate as **online marketplaces** (allowing consumers to conclude distance contracts with traders) carry an additional set of trader traceability obligations under Arts 29-32.

## The Liability Regime (Arts 4-8)

The DSA preserves the three conditional liability exemptions from the 2000 e-Commerce Directive.

**Mere conduit (Art 4):** No liability for transmitted information if the provider does not initiate, select recipients or modify content.

**Caching (Art 5):** No liability for temporary storage, provided the provider does not modify, complies with access conditions, and expeditiously removes cached content when the original is removed or blocked.

**Hosting (Art 6):** No liability for stored content if the provider: (a) does not have actual knowledge of illegal content; or (b) upon obtaining such knowledge, acts expeditiously to remove or disable access. The exemption is lost if the recipient acts under the provider's authority or control, or if a marketplace presents third-party content in a way that leads an average consumer to believe it is the platform's own content.

**Article 7 (Good Samaritan):** Voluntary proactive investigations or compliance measures do not strip a provider of its liability exemptions.

**Article 8 (No general monitoring):** No general obligation to monitor content or actively seek illegal activity may be imposed on any intermediary service provider. Only specific, targeted orders are permitted.

---

## Notice-and-Action (Art 16)

Hosting services must provide an easy-to-access electronic mechanism for anyone to report allegedly illegal content. A valid notice must include:

- A substantiated explanation of why the content is allegedly illegal
- The exact URL and any additional locating information
- The notifier's name and email address (except for child sexual abuse material)
- A statement confirming the bona fide belief that the information is accurate

A compliant notice that meets these requirements gives rise to "actual knowledge or awareness" for the purposes of the Article 6 hosting exemption. The provider must acknowledge receipt and notify its decision (with redress options) to the notifier without undue delay.

---

## Statement of Reasons (Art 17)

When a hosting provider restricts content visibility, removes content, suspends or terminates a user's account, suspends or restricts monetisation, or otherwise restricts the provision of the service, it must provide the affected recipient with a clear, specific statement of reasons at the latest when the restriction takes effect. The statement must cover: the factual ground for the decision, whether automated means were used, the legal or contractual ground, and available redress options. This obligation applies regardless of how the provider became aware of the issue (notice, own-initiative investigation or authority order), except that it does not apply to authority orders under Article 9.

---

## Dark Patterns (Art 25)

Online platforms must not design interfaces that deceive, manipulate or materially distort users' ability to make free and informed choices. Prohibited practices include:

- Giving undue prominence to certain choices (e.g. larger or more colourful accept buttons)
- Repeated pop-ups after the user has already made a choice
- Making cancellation or account deletion significantly more cumbersome than sign-up
- Using default settings that are very difficult to change and that unreasonably bias decision-making

This prohibition is separate from, and cumulative with, the Unfair Commercial Practices Directive and GDPR. The Commission may issue guidelines on specific practices.

---

## Advertising Rules (Arts 26-28)

**For all online platforms (Art 26):** For every advertisement, provide each recipient in real time: (a) a clear label that it is an advertisement; (b) the identity of the entity on whose behalf it is presented; (c) the identity of the payer if different; (d) the main targeting parameters. No targeted advertising using GDPR Article 9 special-category data (health, religion, political views, sexual orientation, etc.) is permitted, even with user consent.

**For VLOPs and VLOSEs (Art 39):** Maintain a public, searchable ad repository for one year after last display. The repository must include content, advertiser, payer, display period and targeting criteria. No personal data of users in the repository.

**Prohibition on targeting minors (Art 28):** Online platforms must not present targeted advertisements based on profiling when they are aware with reasonable certainty that the user is a minor.

---

## Recommender Systems (Arts 27 and 38)

**All online platforms (Art 27):** Explain the main parameters of recommender systems in plain language in the terms and conditions. If multiple recommendation options exist, offer a user-controlled selection functionality, directly accessible from the interface.

**VLOPs and VLOSEs (Art 38):** Must offer at least one recommender option that is not based on profiling within the meaning of GDPR Article 4(4).

---

## VLOP/VLOSE Systemic Risk Regime (Arts 34-43)

**Designation:** The Commission designates platforms and search engines exceeding 45 million average monthly active recipients in the Union. The platform must publish its monthly active user figures at least every six months. Designation triggers obligations from four months after notification. VLOPs/VLOSEs pay an annual supervisory fee (capped at 0.05% of worldwide annual net income).

**Systemic risk assessment (Art 34):** At least once per year, identify, analyse and assess systemic risks in four categories:

1. Dissemination of illegal content
2. Negative effects on fundamental rights (dignity, expression, privacy, non-discrimination, children's rights, consumer protection)
3. Negative effects on civic discourse, elections, public security
4. Negative effects on public health, protection of minors, gender-based violence, physical and mental well-being

The assessment must analyse how recommender systems, advertising systems, content moderation, terms enforcement and data practices contribute to each risk, including intentional manipulation and inauthentic use. Documentation preserved for at least three years.

**Risk mitigation (Art 35):** Implement proportionate measures: adapting design, terms, content moderation, algorithmic systems, advertising systems, cooperation with trusted flaggers and other platforms, awareness-raising measures and measures to protect minors.

**Crisis response (Art 36):** The Commission, on Board recommendation, may order VLOPs/VLOSEs to take specific measures for serious threats to public security or public health, for up to three months (renewable).

**Independent annual audit (Art 37):** At own expense. Auditors must be independent (no non-audit services in the preceding 12 months, no continuous audit relationship exceeding 10 years, no contingent fees). Audit covers Chapter III obligations and any code/crisis protocol commitments. A non-positive audit opinion must be addressed within one month.

**Compliance function (Art 41):** Independent compliance function with a head who reports directly to the management body and who may not be removed without management body approval.

## Enforcement

**Digital Services Coordinators (Arts 49-58):** Each Member State designates one DSC as the single national supervisor, independent of government. DSCs supervise all intermediary service providers established in their territory (except for VLOP/VLOSE systemic-risk obligations, which are the Commission's exclusive preserve). DSCs can investigate, impose interim measures, order compliance, and impose fines. They cooperate through the Board and the Commission's information-sharing system.

**European Board for Digital Services (Arts 61-64):** Advisory body composed of all DSCs and chaired by the Commission. Issues opinions, recommends cross-border enforcement actions, publishes annual systemic risk reports, and recommends crisis response measures.

**Commission (Arts 65-85):** Exclusive supervisor of VLOPs/VLOSEs on systemic-risk obligations. Investigatory powers include requests for information, interviews, inspections and data access. Can impose interim measures, non-compliance decisions, fines and periodic penalty payments. VLOP/VLOSE fines: up to 6% of total worldwide annual turnover (Art 74); procedural fines: up to 1% of annual income (Art 74(2)); periodic penalty payments: up to 5% of average daily worldwide annual turnover per day (Art 76). A five-year limitation period applies (Art 77). All Commission decisions subject to review by the Court of Justice of the European Union.

## Relationship to Other Laws

- **Digital Markets Act (Regulation (EU) 2022/1925):** Covers ex ante obligations for gatekeepers (large platforms in core platform services). Different scope and different addressees. Both may apply simultaneously to the same platform.
- **GDPR (Regulation (EU) 2016/679):** Governs all personal data processing. DSA advertising restrictions are without prejudice to GDPR, and both sets of rules apply. Special-category data ban on ad targeting in Art 26(3) DSA is additional to, not a substitute for, GDPR Article 9 requirements.
- **AVMS Directive (2010/13/EU):** Governs video-sharing platforms. The DSA is without prejudice to AVMS provisions; both may apply to the same service.
- **Terrorist Content Regulation (2021/784), Regulation (EU) 2021/1232:** Specific sectoral instruments that apply in parallel and take precedence on their specific subject matter.
- **e-Commerce Directive (2000/31/EC):** The DSA does not formally repeal it but absorbs its liability regime. Remaining e-Commerce Directive provisions on country-of-origin and derogations (Art 3) continue to apply.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does the DSA apply to a small EU start-up running a forum?**
Yes, if the forum qualifies as a hosting service, the DSA applies. However, the most burdensome obligations (online platform tier) do not apply to micro and small enterprises. The basic tier obligations (transparency reporting, notice-and-action, statement of reasons) do apply from 17 February 2024.

**Can a VLOP refuse to take down content based on a user notice?**
Yes. A notice triggers actual knowledge, but the hosting exemption still requires the provider to act expeditiously once it has actual knowledge of illegal content. The platform may assess the notice and decide the content is not illegal, but it must communicate that decision with reasons.

**Is illegal content defined in the DSA?**
The DSA gives a broad definition in Article 3(h): information that, in itself or in relation to an activity, is not in compliance with Union law or Member State law. Examples include child sexual abuse material, hate speech, terrorist content, counterfeit products, unlicensed accommodation services. What is illegal is determined by other laws; the DSA provides the procedural framework for dealing with it.

**What is the difference between a VLOP and an ordinary online platform under the DSA?**
An ordinary online platform (not micro/small) must comply with Arts 19-28 (dark patterns, ad transparency, recommender transparency, minors, trader traceability). A VLOP must do all of that plus: annual systemic risk assessment, independent audit, ad repository, researcher data access, compliance function, enhanced transparency reporting, and supervisory fees. The Commission directly supervises VLOPs/VLOSEs; ordinary platforms are supervised by the DSC in their Member State of establishment.

**Does the DSA cover private messaging services?**
Interpersonal communication services (emails, private messaging to a finite group) are not online platforms because information is not disseminated to "a potentially unlimited number of third parties." However, public groups or open channels on messaging apps may qualify as online platforms if they allow dissemination to a potentially unlimited audience.
