# Trade marks with a reputation and political speech — C-298/23 Inter IKEA Systems

## QUICK FACTS
- **Judgment of the Court of Justice, 8 September 2026**, Case **C-298/23** *Inter IKEA Systems*.
  CURIA press release **No 119/26**
- The question: may a **political party** use a trade mark **with a reputation** to reinforce its
  political message, and does **freedom of expression** give it "due cause"?
- **THE HOLDING: merely invoking freedom of expression is NOT enough to establish due cause.** The
  third party must set out the **specific grounds** for the use and **demonstrate** that they take
  precedence over the proprietor's rights and interests
- The national court must **strike a balance** between the proprietor's **right to property** and the
  third party's **freedom of expression**. **Neither right is absolute**
- **The Court did NOT decide the case.** It held that on these facts it "does not appear" the political
  use prevails, **but that is expressly for the referring court to verify**
- Facts: in 2022 **Vlaams Belang** presented an asylum and immigration plan titled
  *"IKEA-PLAN — Immigratie Kan Echt Anders"* using IKEA marks and assembly-instruction-style figures.
  Inter IKEA sued the **Vrijheidsfonds** association, which ran the campaign
- **Good faith is the pivot**: using a mark to say something **about that mark, its proprietor, its
  commercial practices, goods or services** points to due cause. Borrowing its fame to amplify an
  unrelated message does not
- A proprietor **cannot be required to tolerate** use causing **disproportionate detriment**, or use
  affecting the substance of the exclusive right itself
- Do not write that "IKEA won" or "Vlaams Belang was defeated". The Court gave the framework and a
  strong steer; the Belgian court decides

## Why this matters beyond trade-mark law

This is the Court setting the price of using a famous brand as a **political megaphone**. It is
simultaneously an intellectual-property judgment and a **freedom-of-expression** judgment, and it lands
the burden squarely on the speaker: freedom of expression is not a keyword that unlocks another's mark,
it is a case to be made and evidenced.

## The test the national court must apply

1. **Is there due cause?** EU legislation does not define it, but the recitals require it to be applied
   with full respect for fundamental rights, in particular freedom of expression.
2. **Mere reliance on freedom of expression is insufficient.** Specific grounds must be set out and
   shown to prevail.
3. **Balance property against expression.** Neither is absolute.
4. **Was the mark used in good faith?** Notably where the sign conveys an idea or opinion **about the
   mark itself**, its proprietor, its commercial practices, goods or services.
5. **Does the use contribute to a public-interest debate**, and what are the consequences for the
   proprietor?
6. **Weigh intensity, extent and method of use** — and, critically, whether the use may give the
   impression that **the proprietor agrees with, or supports, the political message**, when that
   proprietor's values are **neutral between parties** or incompatible with the message.

On these facts the Court found the use "may cause significant detriment to the repute of those trade
marks and to the interests of their proprietor", and that use **for the sole purpose of taking
advantage of their reputation** to reinforce and spread a political message does not appear to take
precedence. **Subject to verification by the referring court.**

## How to answer questions about this

- Never state an outcome the Court did not reach. The correct formulation is that the Court set the
  balancing test and indicated that the political use **does not appear** to prevail, leaving the final
  determination to the Belgian court.
- **The distinction that decides these cases** is whether the speech is *about the brand* or merely
  *amplified by the brand*. Parody of a company is far closer to due cause than borrowing its fame for
  an unrelated political programme.
- This concerns marks **with a reputation** and the extended protection they enjoy, not ordinary
  likelihood-of-confusion infringement.
