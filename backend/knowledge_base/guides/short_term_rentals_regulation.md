This guide covers the EU Short-Term Rentals (STR) Transparency Regulation, which is now applying across all 27 Member States from 20 May 2026. The regulation creates a harmonised data-collection and sharing framework for platforms like Airbnb, Booking, Vrbo, and smaller intermediaries, and standardises the host registration procedure.

## What the regulation does

The STR regulation does **not** harmonise the substantive housing rules that Member States, regions, or municipalities apply to short-term rentals (cap on rental nights per year, residence-only restrictions, licensing fees, zoning rules). Substantive STR policy remains a Member State competence. What 2024/1028 harmonises is the **data and registration layer**: how hosts identify themselves, how platforms display and verify identifiers, and how authorities can request and obtain operational data.

### Three pillars

**Pillar 1 — Host registration**
- Member States that operate an STR registration regime must let hosts complete the procedure **fully online** (Article 4)
- Each property receives a **unique registration number** linked to a specific address and to a natural-person or legal-person host
- The registration scheme must be free of charge or proportionate; not a hidden tax
- The registration procedure must accept self-declaration plus identity verification; a "simple authority check" stage is permitted

**Pillar 2 — Platform obligations**
- Online short-term rental platforms ("online STR platforms" — qualified providers of an information society service that allow hosts to offer short-term accommodation) must (Article 5):
  - Display the host registration number prominently on each listing
  - Operate **random checks** to identify listings without a valid registration number
  - On request, remove listings that lack a registration number or carry a clearly fraudulent one
  - Maintain a structured listing index that supports authority-side queries
- Smaller (micro) platforms benefit from lighter reporting obligations (Article 7)

**Pillar 3 — Data sharing via Single Digital Entry Points (SDEPs)**
- Each participating Member State establishes a **Single Digital Entry Point** for receiving platform-side reporting (Article 9)
- Platforms transmit **monthly aggregated data**: number of guest stays per registered property + total nights booked + city-level breakdown
- The SDEP routes the data to the competent authority designated by the Member State (typically a national tourism agency, statistics office, or city authority for major destinations)
- Cross-border consistency is ensured by a common data model defined in the Implementing Acts (Articles 12-14)

## How the opt-in works

A frequent misconception is that 2024/1028 forces every Member State to register every STR host. It does not. The regulation creates a **conditional opt-in**: a Member State (or sub-national authority) is free to keep doing nothing on STR registration. But **if** it operates any registration scheme or any data-collection scheme, that scheme **must** conform to 2024/1028's data model, registration process, and SDEP routing.

This matters for the existing legal patchwork:
- **France**: Articles L. 324-1-1 et seq. of the Code du tourisme (numéro d'enregistrement) — existing regime aligns with 2024/1028 starting 20 May 2026
- **Spain**: Real Decreto 1312/2024 (national STR registration, registry under DGRyN) — operational and aligning
- **Italy**: CIN (Codice Identificativo Nazionale) from 1 January 2025 — alignment in progress
- **Netherlands**: Amsterdam and other large cities operate municipal registration; national framework via Wet Toeristische Verhuur of Woonruimte (2021) — alignment expected by end-2026
- **Germany**: Länder-level regimes (Berlin Zweckentfremdungsverbot, Hamburg, München) — alignment under the Bundesregierung implementation discussion 2026
- **Portugal**: AL (Alojamento Local) regime since 2014 — substantive update Q3 2026 expected

## Platform obligations in detail

### Verification of registration numbers
Platforms must establish a process by which a host claims a registration number and the platform verifies it against the SDEP-routed registry. The verification can be at listing-creation time (recommended) or at first booking. After verification, the platform displays the registration number on the public listing page and in any booking confirmation.

### Random checks
Article 5(3) requires platforms to operate a sampling-based check on active listings to detect:
- Listings without any registration number where one is required by the Member State
- Listings carrying a clearly fraudulent or duplicated registration number (e.g., the same number across multiple addresses)
- Listings where the registered host has been deactivated by authorities

The exact sampling rate is left to platforms but must be proportionate to platform size. Member State authorities may benchmark the sampling rate against a baseline if a platform's reported non-compliance rate diverges from authority-side spot checks.

### Reporting cadence
- **Monthly** data transmission to the SDEP for guest stays + nights booked (Article 9(2))
- **On-request** data on individual listings (Article 9(3)) for enforcement purposes
- Data retention by platforms: minimum 12 months (Article 10)

### Lighter regime for small/micro platforms
- A platform qualifies as "micro" for the purposes of 2024/1028 if it falls below thresholds set in the Implementing Acts (broadly aligned with the GDPR + DSA SMP thresholds)
- Micro platforms file aggregated annual reports instead of monthly transmissions

## Enforcement and penalties

Member States designate competent authorities (typically the tourism ministry, the consumer protection authority, or a city-level enforcement office). Sanctions are set at Member State level under Article 16 but must be "effective, proportionate, and dissuasive". Typical national penalties for STR breaches today range from EUR 1,000 to EUR 30,000 per listing per violation; the regulation requires Member States to apply the same scale to platforms that fail to verify or report.

The **Digital Services Act** (DSA, Reg (EU) 2022/2065) overlays general platform-liability rules on the largest STR platforms (Airbnb is a VLOP since April 2023, Booking from June 2024).

## Interaction with the DSA and the Data Act

- **DSA (Reg (EU) 2022/2065)**: VLOP designation triggers risk-assessment + transparency reporting + crisis-protocol obligations independently of 2024/1028. Compliance with 2024/1028 does not exempt from DSA obligations.
- **Data Act (Reg (EU) 2023/2854)**: The Data Act's B2G data-sharing provisions (Chapter V) apply in cases of public emergency or where data is required to fulfil a public-interest task. Routine STR data exchange runs under 2024/1028 + SDEPs, **not** under Data Act Chapter V.

## Who is affected

- **Hosts**: anyone offering a short-term accommodation rental in any of the 27 Member States — natural persons and SMEs in tourism
- **Platforms**: Airbnb, Booking.com, Vrbo, HomeAway, Tripadvisor Rentals, Hostelworld, Plum Guide, Outdoorsy, and ~50+ smaller niche intermediaries
- **Member States**: 27 national + sub-national tourism authorities
- **Major cities** with active STR caps that will benefit from harmonised data: Paris, Amsterdam, Barcelona, Berlin, Lisbon, Vienna, Prague, Madrid, Rome, Florence, Venice
- **National statistics offices** (Eurostat partner agencies)
- **Tour operator associations**: HOTREC (European hospitality), European Tourism Association (ETOA), European Holiday Home Association (EHHA)

## Practical implications for Brubru users

- **For platform operators**: the technical work is the SDEP integration (one REST endpoint per Member State; data model converges on ISO 19848 / ISO 24613). Budget 6-9 months for full multi-Member-State integration.
- **For property managers and hosts**: register once per property in the Member State where the property is located; the number must appear on the public listing. Failing to display = sanction risk for both you and the platform.
- **For municipalities**: the SDEP provides authoritative occupancy data for STR-policy modelling (urban housing supply impact, overtourism management).
- **For investors**: the regime increases compliance cost for non-EU platforms entering the market but does not cap STR supply directly — substantive caps remain a city-level lever.
