# EU action on antimicrobial resistance (AMR) and the One Health approach

## QUICK FACTS
- **What it is**: the EU's policy framework to slow antimicrobial resistance (AMR), the loss of effectiveness of antibiotics, antivirals, antifungals and antiparasitics. The EU works on it through a "One Health" approach that treats human health, animal health and the environment as one connected system.
- **Why it matters**: AMR is one of the EU's top health-security threats. The Commission's own figures attribute well over 35,000 deaths a year in the EU/EEA to antimicrobial-resistant infections, with a large healthcare and productivity cost. It is a priority for EFPIA's anti-infectives and vaccines members because it shapes both stewardship rules (how existing antibiotics are used) and the incentive debate (how to make new antibiotics commercially viable).
- **Council Recommendation on AMR (2023)**: the central political instrument is the Council Recommendation of 13 June 2023 on stepping up EU actions to combat antimicrobial resistance in a One Health approach (2023/C 220/01). It sets recommended EU-level targets for 2030, measured against a 2019 baseline:
  - reduce total human consumption of antibiotics by 20%;
  - ensure at least 65% of total human antibiotic consumption is "Access" group antibiotics (the WHO AWaRe classification, the first-line narrow-spectrum agents);
  - reduce specified resistant-infection rates for three key bacteria.
- **2017 One Health Action Plan against AMR**: the predecessor framework (still referenced) built around better prevention, surveillance, research, and global cooperation.
- **The AMR One Health Network**: a Commission expert group that brings together Member State representatives from the human-health, animal-health and environment sectors, plus the EU agencies, to coordinate national AMR action plans and share evidence. It meets periodically; its meeting presentations are published by DG SANTE. (When the network "meets" or "publishes presentations" is a date that must be checked against the DG SANTE Latest Updates feed, not assumed.)
- **The EU agencies' roles**:
  - **ECDC** (European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control) runs the human-side surveillance networks EARS-Net (resistance) and ESAC-Net (consumption).
  - **EMA** (European Medicines Agency) covers the medicines side, including veterinary antimicrobial sales monitoring (ESVAC) and scientific advice on new antimicrobials.
  - **EFSA** (European Food Safety Authority) covers resistance in food and animals.
  - The three publish the joint **JIACRA** report (Joint Interagency Antimicrobial Consumption and Resistance Analysis) linking consumption and resistance across human and animal sectors.
- **The incentives debate (pull incentives)**: the revision of the EU pharmaceutical legislation (Regulation 2023/0131(COD) and Directive 2023/0132(COD)) is where the EU is debating how to reward companies that bring genuinely novel antibiotics to market. The headline mechanism proposed is the **transferable exclusivity voucher (TEV)**, a transferable extra period of regulatory data protection that the developer of a priority antimicrobial could use on another product or sell. It is contested: critics warn about cost to health systems, supporters argue the antibiotics market is broken without a "pull" incentive. Watch this as part of the pharmaceutical package negotiations.
- **Stewardship and prevention (push side)**: the Council Recommendation also pushes infection prevention and control, vaccination (to reduce infections that would otherwise be treated with antibiotics), prudent-use rules, and stronger surveillance. Veterinary use is constrained by Regulation (EU) 2019/6 on veterinary medicinal products and Regulation (EU) 2019/4 on medicated feed, which restrict prophylactic and metaphylactic antibiotic use in animals.
- **Global dimension**: the EU pushes AMR at the WHO, the UN (the 2024 UN General Assembly high-level meeting on AMR), the G7 and G20, and through the Transatlantic Taskforce on AMR (TATFAR).
- **Why it matters for industry / EFPIA**: AMR policy sits at the intersection of three EFPIA priorities, the pharmaceutical package incentives (TEV and data protection), the Critical Medicines Act (many antibiotics are on the Union List of Critical Medicines), and health-security coordination (joint procurement, stockpiling). Stewardship targets shape volume; incentive design shapes whether new antibiotics get developed at all.

## Overview

Antimicrobial resistance is the gradual loss of effectiveness of the medicines used to treat infections. It is driven by overuse and misuse of antimicrobials in people, animals and agriculture, which is why the EU frames its response as "One Health": no single sector can solve it alone.

The EU's response has two halves. The first is the "push" or stewardship side, reducing unnecessary use, strengthening infection prevention and vaccination, improving surveillance and setting consumption-reduction targets. The central instrument here is the 2023 Council Recommendation, which sets recommended 2030 targets against a 2019 baseline and asks Member States to update and resource their national AMR action plans. The agencies ECDC, EMA and EFSA supply the surveillance backbone, and the AMR One Health Network is where Member State experts coordinate.

The second half is the "pull" or incentives side: the recognition that the commercial market for new antibiotics is broken because a successful new antibiotic is, by design, used as little as possible. The revision of the EU pharmaceutical legislation is the venue for this debate, and the most-discussed mechanism is the transferable exclusivity voucher. The interaction between AMR policy, the Critical Medicines Act (antibiotics feature heavily on the Union List of Critical Medicines) and the health-security architecture (joint procurement and stockpiling of critical antimicrobials) is what makes this a cross-cutting file for industry.
