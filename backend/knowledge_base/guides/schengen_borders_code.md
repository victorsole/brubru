# Schengen Borders Code (Regulation 2016/399) and the 2024 Amendment

## QUICK FACTS

- **LATEST (Monday 18 May 2026 -- COMMISSION'S ANNUAL STATE OF SCHENGEN REPORT)**: DG HOME's annual Schengen report (IP/26/1015, 2025 reference year) finds the Schengen area "continues to be resilient and ready for future challenges," but confirms that **10 of the then 27 EU Member States** continued to rely on the temporary reintroduction of internal border controls during 2025, mostly citing terrorism, migration, or large-event security threats. The report frames the tightened 2024 rules on reintroduction (below) as the benchmark against which these national measures are assessed, and links Schengen operational readiness -- including the Schengen Information System and the Entry/Exit System, live since November 2025 -- to the Migration Pact's 12 June 2026 implementation deadline. Source: `home-affairs.ec.europa.eu/news/commission-report-shows-schengen-area-continues-be-resilient-and-ready-future-challenges-2026-05-18_en` + presscorner IP/26/1015. Repeated, long-running national controls (Germany, France, Austria and others) remain a live political flashpoint: critics argue the "temporary" and "last resort" character of Article 25 has become normalised rather than exceptional.
- **Core instrument**: Regulation (EU) 2016/399 of the European Parliament and of the Council of 9 March 2016 on a Union Code on the rules governing the movement of persons across borders (the codified Schengen Borders Code), OJ L 77, 23.3.2016 -- CELEX **32016R0399**. It codifies (replaces, without substantive change at the time) the original Schengen Borders Code, Regulation (EC) No 562/2006.
- **2024 amendment**: Regulation (EU) 2024/1717 of the European Parliament and of the Council of 13 June 2024 amending Regulation (EU) 2016/399, OJ L, 2024/1717, 20.6.2024 -- CELEX **32024R1717**. Entered into force 20 days after publication (around 10 July 2024). This is the amendment most people mean by "the new Schengen Borders Code rules."
- **Legal base**: Article 77(2)(b) and (e) TFEU (rules on checks at external borders; absence of checks on persons at internal borders).
- **What the 2016 Code covers**: no checks on persons at internal borders (Article 22) regardless of nationality; common rules on external-border crossing (entry conditions, border checks, refusal of entry, border surveillance); the conditions and procedure for the temporary reintroduction of internal-border controls (Title III).
- **What the 2024 amendment added or tightened**: (1) a defined, harmonised EU response to the **instrumentalisation of migrants** by a third country or hostile non-state actor; (2) a new, harmonised legal basis for **public-health travel restrictions** at external borders in a cross-border health emergency (building on COVID-19 experience); (3) materially **tighter grounds, time limits, and procedural safeguards** for the temporary reintroduction of internal-border controls, including a mandatory necessity/proportionality assessment and a duty to consider alternative measures (police checks, technology-based checks) before reimposing border checks; (4) a new **internal-border transfer procedure** for irregular migrants apprehended in border areas, returning them under bilateral cooperation to the Member State of last entry -- the most contested provision, criticised by civil-society and some Member States as informal "pushback by another name."
- **Schengen area today**: **29 countries** -- 25 EU Member States plus 4 non-EU states (Iceland, Norway, Switzerland, Liechtenstein). **Bulgaria and Romania became full Schengen members on 1 January 2025** (air and sea border checks had already been lifted from 31 March 2024; land-border checks were lifted from 1 January 2025), the last EU states to complete accession. **Cyprus** applies parts of the Schengen acquis but the Council has not yet abolished its internal-border checks. **Ireland** has a permanent opt-out and runs its own visa/border policy, though it participates selectively in some Schengen instruments (e.g. the Schengen Information System, police cooperation).
- **Reintroduction time limits under the 2024 amendment (Articles 25-28)**: unforeseeable/urgent threats -- up to 1 month without prior notification, renewable to a 3-month maximum; foreseeable threats -- 6-month periods, renewable up to a 2-year standard maximum, extendable in "major exceptional situations" to a 3-year absolute maximum; public-health emergencies (Article 28) -- Council authorisation, up to 6 months, renewable. Reintroduction must remain a measure of "last resort," proportionate and geographically/temporally limited to what is strictly necessary.
- **Evaluation mechanism**: Regulation (EU) 2022/922 (CELEX 32022R0922) establishes the Schengen Evaluation and Monitoring Mechanism (SEMC), which replaced Regulation (EU) No 1053/2013 and verifies Member States' correct application of the Schengen acquis, including the Borders Code, through peer-review visits, questionnaires and (since 2022) unannounced inspections.
- **Lead Commission service**: DG HOME (Migration and Home Affairs); Commissioner Magnus Brunner (Internal Affairs and Migration). **EP lead committee**: LIBE. **Council configuration**: Justice and Home Affairs Council (JHA), plus the informal Schengen Council / Schengen Forum ministerial formats.

## Why this file matters

The Schengen Borders Code is the operational rulebook underneath the political promise of "no internal borders": it sets out who may cross an EU external border and how, and -- separately -- the narrow, time-limited conditions under which a Schengen state may temporarily suspend the no-internal-border-checks rule. Both halves are politically sensitive. External-border rules intersect directly with EU asylum and migration policy (the Migration Pact's Screening Regulation operates at the point the Borders Code defines as "the external border"). Internal-border rules are the legal basis every time Germany, France, Austria, Denmark, or another Schengen state announces a return of passport checks at a land border with a neighbouring Schengen state -- a recurring news story and a recurring source of friction over free movement, cross-border commuting and the single market.

The 2024 amendment (Regulation 2024/1717) was negotiated alongside the wider EU Pact on Migration and Asylum (see `eu_migration_asylum_pact`) and responds to two distinct pressure points from 2021-2023: the weaponisation of migration flows by Belarus against Poland/Lithuania/Latvia (the "instrumentalisation" trigger), and the ad hoc, legally uncertain border closures of the COVID-19 pandemic (the public-health trigger). It also responds to years of Commission and European Parliament criticism that some Member States were treating Article 25 reintroductions as a standing rather than exceptional tool -- hence the tightened necessity test, the alternative-measures-first requirement, and the hard 2-year (extendable to 3-year) ceiling.

## Instrumentalisation of migrants

The amendment introduces a legal definition of instrumentalisation: a situation where a third country or a hostile non-state actor facilitates or encourages the movement of third-country nationals to the EU's external borders, or into a Member State, with the aim of destabilising the Union or a Member State, where such actions are liable to endanger essential State functions, including territorial integrity, or public order or security. This mirrors and cross-references the definition used in the Migration Pact's Crisis and Force Majeure Regulation (Regulation (EU) 2024/1359, CELEX 32024R1359 -- see `eu_migration_asylum_pact`). Where instrumentalisation is found, Member States gain tools including restricting the number of open border-crossing points and reinforcing border surveillance, alongside recourse to the Crisis Regulation's derogated timelines for border and asylum procedures.

## Public-health emergency provisions

A new Article 21a-type mechanism gives the Council a harmonised legal basis to authorise temporary travel restrictions at the external border in the event of a public-health emergency threatening the functioning of the Schengen area as a whole -- explicitly designed to avoid a repeat of the uncoordinated, bilaterally improvised border closures of March-April 2020.

## Temporary reintroduction of internal-border controls

This is the most frequently litigated and most newsworthy part of the Code. Under the pre-2024 rules, "foreseeable" reintroductions could in practice be rolled over close to indefinitely through successive 6-month renewals with limited external check. The 2024 amendment keeps the same basic structure but adds real ceilings and safeguards:

- **Grounds**: serious threat to public policy or internal security (including terrorism and organised crime), persistent serious deficiencies relating to external border control that risk the overall functioning of the Schengen area, and (new) large-scale unauthorised movements of third-country nationals between Member States posing a threat to overall Schengen functioning.
- **Necessity and proportionality test**: the reintroducing Member State must assess whether the objective could be achieved by alternative measures (targeted police checks in border areas, enhanced police and customs cooperation, technology-based surveillance) and must consider the impact on cross-border regions and the internal market before choosing full border checks.
- **Notification and reporting**: prior notification to the Commission and other Member States, and (for longer reintroductions) a risk assessment and, where renewed beyond 6 months, a report on the necessity and proportionality of the extension.
- **Time ceilings**: see QUICK FACTS above -- 1 month/3 months for unforeseeable events; 6-month renewable periods up to a 2-year standard maximum and a 3-year absolute maximum for "major exceptional situations"; up to 6 months (renewable) for a Council-authorised public-health emergency.
- **New internal-border transfer procedure**: allows a Member State carrying out checks near an internal border to transfer an irregularly staying third-country national apprehended there directly to the Member State from which they are presumed to have entered, under a bilateral cooperation framework, without a formal return procedure. This is the amendment's most contested element -- NGOs (ECRE, Amnesty International) argue it risks operating as an informal pushback mechanism outside ordinary return-procedure safeguards; several Member States have used it only sparingly or not at all.

## Institutional landscape

- **Lead DG**: DG HOME (Migration and Home Affairs).
- **Responsible Commissioner**: Magnus Brunner (Internal Affairs and Migration).
- **EP lead committee**: LIBE (Civil Liberties, Justice and Home Affairs).
- **Council configuration**: Justice and Home Affairs Council (JHA); informal Schengen Council / Schengen Forum ministerial meetings track implementation and the annual "State of Schengen" report.
- **Monitoring/evaluation**: the Schengen Evaluation and Monitoring Mechanism (SEMC, Regulation (EU) 2022/922) verifies correct national application; Frontex supports external-border operations; eu-LISA operates the large-scale IT systems (SIS, EES, ETIAS, VIS) that underpin border checks under the Code.

## Answer guidance for Brubru

- Distinguish clearly between the **2016 codification** (Regulation 2016/399, the baseline text) and the **2024 amendment** (Regulation 2024/1717, which tightened reintroduction rules and added the instrumentalisation and public-health regimes). A question about "the new Schengen border rules" almost always means the 2024 amendment.
- When a user asks about a specific country reintroducing border checks (e.g. Germany, France, Austria, Denmark), cite the Article 25-28 framework and note the applicable time ceiling, but do not assert a specific country's current status without checking a live source (the Commission's Schengen area page and the annual State of Schengen report are the primary references) -- national practice changes frequently.
- The Schengen area has **29 members** (25 EU + Iceland, Norway, Switzerland, Liechtenstein), not all 27 EU Member States: Cyprus has not completed accession (Schengen acquis applies partially, internal checks not yet abolished) and Ireland has a permanent opt-out. Bulgaria and Romania completed accession on 1 January 2025.
- Cross-link to `eu_migration_asylum_pact` for the asylum/screening dimension and to `schengen_information_system_sis` / `eu_entry_exit_system_ees_2017_2226` / `eu_etias_travel_authorisation_2018_1240` for the IT-systems layer that operationalises the checks the Code requires.
