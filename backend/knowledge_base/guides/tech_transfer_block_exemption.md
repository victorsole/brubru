# Technology Transfer Block Exemption Regulation (TTBER) — 2026 Revision

## QUICK FACTS
- Topic: EU antitrust safe harbour for technology licensing agreements
- Announcement: Commission press release 16 April 2026 (IP/26/809) -- Commission updates EU competition rules for technology licensing agreements
- Instrument: Technology Transfer Block Exemption Regulation (TTBER) + accompanying Technology Transfer Guidelines
- Current regime: Regulation (EU) No 316/2014 (CELEX 32014R0316) -- expires 30 April 2026
- New instrument: Commission Regulation revising/replacing 316/2014 (adopted 16 April 2026)
- Legal basis: Council Regulation (EC) No 19/65 (block exemption enabling regulation) + Article 101(3) TFEU
- Lead DG: DG Competition (DG COMP)
- Director-General DG COMP (from 13 April 2026): Anthony Whelan
- Scope: Bilateral licensing agreements between two undertakings for patents, know-how, software copyright, and other IP rights for the production of contract products
- Safe-harbour thresholds: market-share thresholds differ for competitors (combined <= 20%) vs non-competitors (each <= 30%)
- Black-listed (hardcore) restrictions: price fixing, output restrictions, allocation of markets/customers (competitors); restrictions on passive sales / resale prices (non-competitors)
- Review context: 10-year review aligned with revision of Vertical Block Exemption (VBER, 2022) and Horizontal Guidelines (2023)
- Why this matters: covers the dominant competition-law mechanism for licensing IP; deeply relevant to biotech, pharma, semiconductors, software, AI, green-tech licensing
- Article 101(3) TFEU: Allows exemption from cartel prohibition where agreements contribute to economic progress while giving consumers a fair share of the benefit
- Parallel: Commission Notice on Technology Transfer Guidelines (TTG) -- interpretative guidance not formally binding but followed by NCAs and courts

## Why the TTBER Matters

The TTBER provides a **safe harbour** from the Article 101(1) TFEU prohibition on anti-competitive agreements. Licensing agreements that fall within its scope are presumed lawful without case-by-case analysis, provided they respect the market-share thresholds and do not contain hardcore restrictions.

Without the TTBER, every licensing agreement between two undertakings would be exposed to self-assessment under Article 101(3), creating massive legal uncertainty. The TTBER is therefore central to cross-border technology licensing in the EU — especially for:

- **Pharma / biotech** — patent licensing, collaborative R&D
- **Semiconductors** — cross-licensing between foundries and fabless designers
- **Software / AI** — open-source reciprocal licensing, proprietary model licensing
- **Green-tech and clean-tech** — battery chemistry licences, hydrogen electrolyser IP, solar PV patents
- **SEP / FRAND** — standard-essential patents subject to FRAND commitments

## Hardcore Restrictions

### Between competitors (Article 4(1))
- Fixing prices for the sale of products to third parties
- Limiting output (except restrictions on the licensee's output in non-reciprocal agreements)
- Allocating markets or customers (with limited exceptions for exclusive licensing)
- Restricting the licensee's ability to exploit its own technology rights

### Between non-competitors (Article 4(2))
- Fixing a minimum or fixed sale price to be observed by the licensee
- Restricting passive sales into territories or to customer groups reserved for the licensor or another licensee
- Restricting sales to end users by a licensee at the retail level

## Excluded Restrictions (Article 5)

- Obligations to grant back severable improvements exclusively to the licensor
- Obligations not to challenge validity of IP rights (no-challenge clauses) -- with some exceptions
- Restrictions limiting the licensee's ability to exploit its own technology or conduct R&D

## 2026 Revision — What Is Likely New

Based on the Commission's 2023 evaluation and stakeholder consultation, the April 2026 revision is expected to:

1. **Update treatment of SEPs / FRAND**: Align the TTBER with the 2023 SEP Regulation proposal (since withdrawn but principles retained)
2. **AI model licensing**: Clarify whether AI foundation model weights, training datasets, and fine-tuning rights fall within "know-how" / "technology rights"
3. **Open-source and reciprocal licensing**: Guidance on whether copyleft obligations (GPL, AGPL) constitute excluded restrictions
4. **Data sharing clauses**: Treatment of data exchange between licensor and licensee, coordinated with the Data Act
5. **Sustainability clauses**: Handling of environmental/ESG obligations attached to technology licences
6. **Pay-for-delay settlements**: Tightened treatment following 2023 evaluation findings of continued antitrust concerns
7. **Duration**: New 10-year validity period (expected expiry 30 April 2036)

## Typical Licensing Structures

- **Exclusive licence**: single licensee in a defined territory -- market-allocation risks
- **Non-exclusive licence**: multiple licensees -- generally unproblematic
- **Cross-licence**: reciprocal rights between competitors -- requires careful hardcore review
- **Sub-licensing**: licensee authorised to grant rights further -- treated as separate transfer
- **Patent pools**: multi-party arrangements often assessed under Technology Transfer Guidelines (TTG) rather than TTBER

## Penalties for Non-Compliance

Agreements falling outside the TTBER safe harbour are not automatically illegal, but require individual self-assessment under Article 101(3). If found anti-competitive:

- Fines up to 10% of global turnover (Regulation 1/2003)
- Agreements void and unenforceable (Article 101(2) TFEU)
- Private damages actions (Directive 2014/104/EU)
- NCA enforcement in parallel
