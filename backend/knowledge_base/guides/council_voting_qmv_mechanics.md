# How the Council of the EU Votes: QMV, Blocking Minorities, Unanimity, Passerelle Clauses

## QUICK FACTS
- **Topic:** Council of the EU voting rules -- qualified majority voting (QMV), blocking minority, simple majority, unanimity, reverse QMV, passerelle clauses
- **Treaty basis (default):** Article 16(3) TEU -- QMV is the **default**; unanimity and simple majority are the exceptions
- **QMV formula (Lisbon, since 1 Dec 2009):** at least **55%** of Member States (15 of 27) AND at least **65%** of total EU population -- both conditions must be met simultaneously
- **Enhanced QMV (Council acts without Commission or High Representative proposal):** at least **72%** of Member States (20 of 27) AND at least **65%** of EU population -- Article 238(2) TFEU
- **Blocking minority:** at least **4** Member States representing more than **35%** of EU population; the four-state floor is mandatory under Article 16(4) TEU
- **Simple majority:** 15 of 27 Member States -- used for procedural votes only, never for legislative acts
- **Unanimity areas (selection):** taxation (Articles 113, 115 TFEU), CFSP (Article 31(1) TEU), own resources (Article 311 TFEU), Multiannual Financial Framework (Article 312 TFEU), EU enlargement (Article 49 TEU), Treaty revision (Article 48 TEU), anti-discrimination legislation (Article 19 TFEU)
- **Abstentions in unanimity votes:** do NOT block adoption -- a Member State may abstain to signal political discomfort without vetoing
- **Reverse QMV (Stability and Growth Pact):** Commission fiscal sanctions are **automatically adopted** unless the Council rejects by QMV within the deadline; inertia equals approval -- introduced by Regulation (EU) No 1173/2011 (SGP six-pack)
- **General passerelle (Article 48(7) TEU):** European Council may unanimously switch any TFEU/Title V TEU area from unanimity to QMV or from special to ordinary legislative procedure; any single national parliament can block within 6 months
- **CFSP passerelle (Article 31(3) TEU):** European Council can extend QMV to other CFSP areas (excluding military and defence decisions); no national parliament veto
- **Family-law passerelle (Article 81(3) TFEU):** Council may unanimously switch cross-border family-law measures to OLP; national parliaments have 6-month veto
- **MFF passerelle (Article 312(2) TFEU):** European Council may authorise Council to adopt the MFF Regulation by QMV; no national parliament veto; never yet activated
- **Ioannina compromise:** if members representing roughly 75% of the blocking-minority threshold oppose a decision, the Council must seek broader consensus before proceeding; source: Council Decision 2009/857/EC
- **Population data:** recalculated annually by Council Decision on Eurostat figures; current EU-27 base: approximately 448 million


## Treaty Architecture

### Article 16 TEU -- the default rule

Article 16(3) TEU states that the Council acts by qualified majority **except where the Treaties provide otherwise**. QMV is therefore the structural default; unanimity and simple majority are Treaty-specified carve-outs.

Article 16(4) TEU sets out the double-majority formula:
- At least **55%** of members of the Council (minimum 15 of 27 Member States)
- Representing at least **65%** of the total EU population

A blocking minority must comprise **at least four** Member States. The four-state floor prevents the three largest Member States from vetoing proposals on population weight alone.

### Article 238 TFEU -- thresholds and enhanced QMV

Article 238(2) TFEU raises the bar when the Council acts on a text **not** originating from the Commission or the High Representative for Foreign Affairs and Security Policy. The threshold becomes:
- At least **72%** of Member States (20 of 27)
- At least **65%** of EU population

This protects the Commission's exclusive right of legislative initiative: a coalition of governments cannot push self-drafted amendments through the Council at the lighter QMV threshold.

Article 238(3) TFEU addresses **partial participation**: where not all members participate (for instance, in enhanced-cooperation configurations), both the QMV and blocking-minority thresholds are recalculated proportionally based only on the participating states.


## The QMV Formula in Practice

### Worked example (2025 population base: approximately 448 million)

QMV requires:
- 15 or more Member States (55% of 27 = 14.85, rounded up to 15)
- At least approximately 291 million residents (65% of 448m)

Blocking minority requires:
- At least 4 Member States
- Together representing more than approximately 157 million residents (35% of 448m)

**Blocking coalition example:** Germany (~84m), France (~68m), Poland (~37m), Romania (~18m) = 4 Member States, approximately 207 million = 46.2% of EU population. A blocking minority is achieved.

**Why the four-state floor matters:** Germany (~84m), France (~68m), Italy (~59m) = 3 Member States, approximately 211 million = 47.1% of EU population. On population grounds alone this trio already exceeds 35%. The four-state minimum in Article 16(4) TEU ensures a blocking coalition must have meaningful geographic breadth rather than being dominated by the three largest economies.

**Both conditions must be met simultaneously.** A group of 15 small Member States may satisfy the 55% state threshold while falling short of 65% of population; their votes would not constitute a QMV. Conversely, Germany and France together account for roughly 34% of EU population -- just below the blocking-minority threshold -- even though they are only two states.

### Abstentions under unanimity

Under Article 16(4) TEU, a Member State that abstains in a vote requiring unanimity is not counted as a no vote; the act may still be adopted. Abstentions are a diplomatic instrument allowing a Member State to distance itself publicly from a decision without preventing it.


## Simple Majority

Article 238(1) TFEU: unless the Treaties provide otherwise, the Council acts by simple majority, meaning 15 of 27 Member States. Simple majority applies exclusively to **procedural** matters:
- Adoption of the Council's Rules of Procedure
- Requesting the Commission to undertake studies or submit proposals (Article 241 TFEU)
- Fixing the date and place of a Council session

Simple majority is never used to adopt legislative acts or political decisions.


## Unanimity Domains

The following policy areas require all 27 Member States to vote in favour (or abstain). A single no vote blocks adoption:

| Policy area | Treaty basis |
|---|---|
| Indirect and direct taxation | Articles 113, 115 TFEU |
| Common Foreign and Security Policy (most decisions) | Article 31(1) TEU |
| Own resources (EU revenue system) | Article 311 TFEU |
| Multiannual Financial Framework | Article 312 TFEU |
| EU enlargement | Article 49 TEU |
| Treaty revision (ordinary or simplified) | Article 48 TEU |
| Anti-discrimination legislation | Article 19(1) TFEU |
| Enhanced cooperation: authorisation | Article 329(1) TFEU |
| Environmental fiscal measures | Article 192(2) TFEU |
| Some social-policy measures | Article 153(2) TFEU |
| Language arrangements for EU intellectual-property titles | Article 118 TFEU |
| International agreements covering areas with unanimity in internal law | Article 218(8) TFEU |

**Practical significance for advocacy:** a single Member State can block a Directive or Council Decision in unanimity domains. High-profile cases include Hungary blocking CFSP conclusions on Russia/Ukraine and Ireland protecting its 12.5% corporate-tax rate under Articles 113 and 115 TFEU. Any advocacy campaign on a unanimity-domain file must identify which Member States are potential vetoes, not merely map the majority.


## Reverse QMV under the Stability and Growth Pact

Reverse QMV inverts the normal voting logic. The Commission proposes a recommendation or sanction, and that proposal is **deemed adopted** unless the Council rejects it by qualified majority within a fixed deadline (typically 10 days).

**Source:** Regulation (EU) No 1173/2011 on the effective enforcement of budgetary surveillance in the euro area (SGP six-pack, October 2011), reinforced by Regulation (EU) No 472/2013 (two-pack, May 2013) for euro-area Member States in financial difficulty.

**Applies to:**
- Financial sanctions (interest-bearing deposits, fines) in the preventive and corrective arms of the excessive deficit procedure (EDP) for euro-area Member States
- Annual financial sanctions under the macroeconomic imbalances procedure (MIP)
- Corrective-action recommendations under Regulation 472/2013

**What it changes:** to block a Commission sanction, a qualified majority of Member States must actively organise and vote against. Inertia, political fatigue, or failure to coordinate equals automatic adoption. This was designed to depoliticise fiscal enforcement and reduce the risk of horse-trading in ECOFIN shielding non-compliant Member States.

Reverse QMV does not apply in the ordinary legislative procedure, CFSP, or any other area beyond its specific SGP instruments.


## Passerelle Clauses

Passerelle (French: bridge) clauses allow the Council or European Council to switch a Treaty-defined policy area from unanimity to QMV, or from special legislative procedure (SLP) to ordinary legislative procedure (OLP), **without triggering the full Treaty-revision process under Article 48 TEU**. In most clauses, a single national parliament holds a hard veto.

For the active procedural file on activating passerelle clauses, see `eu_decision_making_passerelle_clauses.md` (EP own-initiative 2026/2012(INI), AFCO, tabled April 2026).

### General passerelle -- Article 48(7) TEU

**Scope:** Any area of TFEU or Title V TEU (CFSP/CSDP), **excluding** decisions with military or defence implications.

**Procedure:**
1. The European Council proposes the switch by unanimous decision.
2. The decision is transmitted to all national parliaments.
3. If **no national parliament objects within six months**, the European Council may adopt the decision.
4. A single objection from one chamber of one national parliament **kills the initiative permanently** for that term; there is no override mechanism.
5. Where the switch concerns a move from SLP to OLP, the European Parliament must give its **consent** (Article 48(7), second subparagraph). For a switch from unanimity to QMV only, the EP is notified but not required to consent, though the EP consistently argues it should be.

**Status (2026):** The general passerelle has never been activated. It is under active political discussion in the context of CFSP reform and eastern enlargement.

### CFSP passerelle -- Article 31(3) TEU

**Scope:** CFSP decisions other than those with military or defence implications.

**Procedure:** European Council decides unanimously. **No national parliament veto.** The EP is informed but not consulted.

**Status (2026):** Historically unused. Revival proposals are linked to the EU's inability to reach Council unanimity on Russia/Ukraine-related CFSP acts when one Member State abstains or objects. The European Defence Union own-initiative (2025/2212(INI)) explicitly explores Article 31(3) as a route short of full Treaty change.

### Family-law passerelle -- Article 81(3) TFEU

**Scope:** Measures concerning family law with cross-border implications listed in Article 81(3) TFEU.

**Procedure:** Council proposes unanimously; national parliaments have six months to veto. If no objection, the Council may switch those measures to OLP (Commission proposes; EP and Council adopt jointly by QMV).

**Use in practice:** Partially applied in the context of Council Regulation (EU) 2019/1111 (Brussels IIb), though the core jurisdictional rules were adopted under Article 81(3) unanimity directly rather than through the passerelle switch.

### MFF passerelle -- Article 312(2) TFEU

**Scope:** Adoption of the Multiannual Financial Framework Regulation.

**Procedure:** European Council unanimously authorises the Council to adopt the MFF by QMV. **No national parliament veto.**

**Status (2026):** Never activated. Every MFF negotiation has ultimately reached unanimity after intensive European Council summits. The 2028-2034 MFF negotiations (commencing 2026) have revived discussion of this clause, as enlargement to 35-plus Member States would make a single-Member-State veto structurally intolerable.

### Other sectoral passerelles

- **Article 192(2) TFEU (environmental fiscal measures):** switch to OLP for measures of a primarily fiscal nature in the environmental field; national parliament veto within six months.
- **Article 153(2) TFEU (social policy):** switch to OLP for certain social-policy measures listed in Article 153(1)(d), (f), (g); national parliament veto within six months.


## The Ioannina Compromise

**Source:** Council Decision 2009/857/EC, Official Journal L 314, 1.12.2009, accompanying the entry into force of the Lisbon Treaty.

**Origin:** The Ioannina compromise was first agreed in March 1994 when Austria, Finland, and Sweden were about to accede. Spain and the United Kingdom argued their blocking power would be diluted by the larger Council. A political declaration preserved a soft right of minority objection. The post-Lisbon version updated the mechanism to the double-majority system.

**Mechanics:** If members of the Council representing at least **three-quarters** of the population threshold or **three-quarters** of the number of Member States needed to constitute a blocking minority indicate opposition:
- The Council must discuss the issue
- The Council must do all within its power to reach a satisfactory solution within a **reasonable time**
- The Council must not proceed to a formal vote until the opposing members are satisfied that discussions have been exhausted

In numerical terms, three-quarters of the blocking-minority thresholds means:
- Approximately 26% of EU population (75% of 35%), or
- At least 3 Member States (75% of the four-state floor, rounded up)

**What it is and is not:** The Ioannina compromise is a **procedural brake**, not a hard veto. It creates an obligation to negotiate further but does not legally prevent adoption. In the Council's consensus culture, a visible near-blocking minority almost always generates enough political pressure to reopen negotiations. No Member State has formally invoked it to override a QMV vote, but its existence shapes negotiating behaviour throughout the legislative cycle.


## How to Read Council Voting Records

### Council public register

The Council public register is the primary entry point for accessing adopted texts, voting results, and preparatory documents:
`consilium.europa.eu/en/documents-publications/public-register/`

Search by document number, keyword, date range, or institution. Legislative acts are published as ST (Council text) + ADD/COR series documents, and formally in the Official Journal.

### Council voting results

Direct URL: `consilium.europa.eu/en/council-eu/voting-results/`

Filterable by Council configuration (ECOFIN, GAC, ENVI, etc.), policy area, and date. Each entry shows the adopted text reference, the vote breakdown (in favour / against / abstain), and the national positions of each Member State. Since 2009, voting records for legislative acts are mandatorily public under Article 16(8) TEU.

### OEIL -- EP Legislative Observatory

URL: `oeil.secure.europarl.europa.eu/oeil/en/`

Each OEIL procedure file includes the Council's formal positions (General Approach, and Common Position in second-reading cases) with dates, document references, and links to the Council register. OEIL is the most efficient single source to track where a file stands in the full legislative cycle including the Council phase.

### Determining whether QMV or unanimity applies

1. Identify the legal basis cited in the Commission proposal (the recitals and the operative "Having regard to..." paragraph).
2. Read the Treaty article cited: it will state "acting by a qualified majority", "acting unanimously", or simply "acting in accordance with the ordinary legislative procedure" (which means QMV in Council).
3. If the article is silent and the proposal follows the ordinary legislative procedure (OLP/COD), default under Article 16(3) TEU is QMV.
4. If the procedure is consultation (CNS) or consent (APP), check whether that specific article requires unanimity or QMV in the Council.
