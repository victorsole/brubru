# Clean Industrial Deal: the 2025 industrial decarbonisation strategy

## QUICK FACTS
- **What it is**: the **Clean Industrial Deal** (CID), a European Commission Communication setting the umbrella industrial and decarbonisation strategy of the von der Leyen II mandate. It reframes decarbonisation as a driver of growth and competitiveness for European industry, rather than a cost to be managed.
- **Reference**: **COM(2025) 85**. EUR-Lex: `https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=COM:2025:85:FIN`
- **Adopted**: 26 February 2025 (College of Commissioners)
- **Type**: Communication (non-legislative), but it launches a wave of legislative and financing follow-up (Industrial Accelerator Act, Temporary Decarbonisation Fund, CISAF, Critical Raw Materials Centre)
- **Political parent**: builds directly on the **Competitiveness Compass** (COM(2025) 30, adopted 29 January 2025) and operationalises the **Draghi Report on EU Competitiveness** (September 2024, EUR 750-800 billion/year investment gap) and the **Letta Report** (April 2024, single market deepening)
- **Responsible Commissioners**: Stephane Sejourne (EVP for Prosperity and Industrial Strategy, lead) and Teresa Ribera (EVP for Clean, Just and Competitive Transition, co-lead)
- **Lead DGs**: DG GROW (industrial strategy, lead markets) and DG CLIMA/DG ENER (energy, decarbonisation financing)
- **Two flagship engines**: (1) lowering energy costs (affordability) for households and industry; (2) turning decarbonisation itself into a driver of growth, jobs and investment for EU industry
- **Six action areas**:
  1. **Affordable Energy** -- the Affordable Energy Action Plan: accelerating clean energy rollout, completing the internal energy market, cutting bills for households and energy-intensive industries
  2. **Lead Markets and Demand for Clean Products** -- a "Buy European Clean" dimension in public and private procurement, plus the proposed **Industrial Decarbonisation Accelerator Act** (see `industrial_accelerator_act.md`, adopted as COM(2026) 100 a year later)
  3. **Financing the Transition** -- mobilising over EUR 100 billion, including the **Industrial Decarbonisation Bank** (target capacity approx. EUR 100 billion) and the revised state-aid rules under the **Clean Industrial Deal State Aid Framework (CISAF)**
  4. **Circularity and Access to Materials** -- the **Critical Raw Materials Centre** for joint purchasing, and a circular-materials target of 24% by 2030
  5. **Global Markets and Trade** -- **Clean Trade and Investment Partnerships** with third countries (diversifying supply chains) and carbon-leakage protection via CBAM
  6. **Skills and Quality Jobs** -- workforce development, reskilling and upskilling for the clean transition, projected to support around 500,000 new jobs
- **Key financing figures**: over EUR 100 billion mobilisation commitment; Industrial Decarbonisation Bank approx. EUR 100 billion capacity; up to EUR 50 billion via an amended InvestEU; EUR 450 million in Horizon Europe research funding for clean-tech; CISAF adopted 25 June 2025 as the state-aid backbone
- **Legislative and financing follow-up spawned by the CID**:
  - Industrial Accelerator Act, COM(2026) 100, 4 March 2026 (see `industrial_accelerator_act.md`)
  - Temporary Decarbonisation Fund proposal (see `temporary_decarbonisation_fund.md`)
  - Clean Industrial Deal State Aid Framework (CISAF), C(2025) 7600, adopted 25 June 2025
  - Critical Raw Materials Demand Aggregation Platform, launched 13 April 2026 under the Critical Raw Materials Act (see `eu_critical_raw_materials_act.md`)
  - "Delivering on the Clean Industrial Deal I" follow-up Communication, COM(2025) 378, 2 July 2025
- **EP response**: European Parliament resolution on the Clean Industrial Deal, 19 June 2025, procedure 2025/2656(RSP), adopted text P10_TA(2025) 0137, rapporteur Tom Berendsen (EPP, NL, ITRE). Commission formally responded 17 November 2025 (SP(2025) 329). See `industrial_accelerator_act.md` for the full EP-request vs Commission-response comparison table.
- **Council configuration**: Competitiveness Council; discussed at multiple European Council meetings on competitiveness (20-21 March 2025, 19-20 March 2026). See `eu_competitiveness_council_debate.md`.
- **Where it sits in the priorities architecture**: flagship of von der Leyen II Priority 1, "A new plan for Europe's sustainable prosperity and competitiveness" (see `eu_commission_priorities_2024_2029.md`)

## Overview

The Clean Industrial Deal is the umbrella industrial strategy of the second von der Leyen Commission. Adopted on 26 February 2025 as COM(2025) 85, it is a Communication rather than a piece of binding legislation, but it functions as the political roadmap from which nearly every industrial and energy-decarbonisation initiative of the 2024-2029 mandate flows: the Industrial Accelerator Act, the Temporary Decarbonisation Fund, the Clean Industrial Deal State Aid Framework (CISAF), the Critical Raw Materials Centre, and the Affordable Energy Action Plan all trace their political mandate back to this document.

Where the 2019-2024 Commission organised its industrial and climate agenda around the **European Green Deal**, the 2024-2029 Commission reframes the same underlying objective (decarbonisation) as a **competitiveness** and growth strategy. The Clean Industrial Deal follows directly from the **Competitiveness Compass** (COM(2025) 30, 29 January 2025), which set out three transformational imperatives (closing the innovation gap, a joint roadmap for decarbonisation and competitiveness, reducing dependencies and increasing security), and it operationalises the diagnosis of the **Draghi Report** (September 2024) that the EU needs an additional EUR 750-800 billion a year in investment to remain globally competitive.

The Communication rests on two flagship engines. The first is **affordability**: bringing down energy costs for households and for energy-intensive industries, delivered principally through the Affordable Energy Action Plan. The second is treating **decarbonisation itself as a driver of growth**: rather than framing emissions cuts as a compliance cost, the CID positions clean technology manufacturing, low-carbon materials and circular-material flows as new EU industrial strengths to be built and defended, including against unfair third-country competition.

## The Six Action Areas

### 1. Affordable Energy
Delivered mainly through the **Affordable Energy Action Plan**, published alongside the CID. Covers accelerating renewable and clean energy deployment, completing the internal energy market, improving grid interconnection, and using public financing tools to lower electricity bills for households and industry. Complementary and more detailed follow-up: `clean_energy_investment_strategy.md` (COM(2026) 116, adopted a year later, sets out the specific investment instruments: the Strategic Infrastructure Investment Fund, Operator Securitisation Facility, hybrid bonds, and the Energy Transition Investment Council).

### 2. Lead Markets and Demand for Clean Products
Creates a **"Buy European Clean"** dimension across public and private procurement, requiring or incentivising minimum shares of low-carbon and EU-origin content in strategic products (steel, cement, aluminium, vehicles). This action area is given binding legal form a year later in the **Industrial Accelerator Act** (COM(2026) 100, 4 March 2026, formally the Industrial Decarbonisation Accelerator Act), which sets out concrete thresholds (25% low-carbon steel, 5% EU-origin concrete/mortar, 25% EU-origin aluminium) and a carbon-intensity labelling system. See `industrial_accelerator_act.md` for the full article-by-article breakdown.

### 3. Financing the Transition
Commits to mobilising **over EUR 100 billion** for industrial decarbonisation, anchored by two instruments:
- The **Industrial Decarbonisation Bank**, targeting roughly EUR 100 billion in financing capacity, using Innovation Fund revenues and InvestEU guarantees to de-risk private investment in decarbonisation projects, particularly Carbon Contracts for Difference (CCfDs) for hard-to-abate sectors.
- The **Clean Industrial Deal State Aid Framework (CISAF)**, adopted 25 June 2025 (C(2025) 7600), which simplifies and speeds up state-aid approval for renewable energy rollout, industrial decarbonisation and clean-tech manufacturing, replacing the earlier Temporary Crisis and Transition Framework as it phases out.

Up to EUR 50 billion is also earmarked via an amended **InvestEU**, alongside EUR 450 million in dedicated Horizon Europe clean-industry research calls. The **Temporary Decarbonisation Fund** (see `temporary_decarbonisation_fund.md`) is a further financing proposal in this action area, aimed at de-risking industrial decarbonisation capital expenditure for energy-intensive industries.

### 4. Circularity and Access to Materials
Targets **24% circular material use by 2030** and establishes the **Critical Raw Materials Centre**, a joint-purchasing mechanism allowing EU companies to aggregate demand for strategic raw materials (lithium, cobalt, rare earths, magnesium, gallium, germanium, natural graphite) and match with diversified international suppliers, mirroring the AggregateEU model used for gas. Operationally launched as the **Critical Raw Materials Demand Aggregation Platform** on 13 April 2026 under the Critical Raw Materials Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/1252). See `eu_critical_raw_materials_act.md`.

### 5. Global Markets and Trade
Proposes **Clean Trade and Investment Partnerships** with third countries to diversify supply chains away from concentrated dependencies (notably on China for processed critical raw materials and clean-tech components), alongside continued use of the Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (CBAM) to prevent carbon leakage and protect the competitiveness of EU industry facing carbon pricing under the EU ETS. A first partnership was launched with South Africa. See `eu_ets_review_2026.md` for the linked carbon-pricing dimension.

### 6. Skills and Quality Jobs
Commits to reskilling and upskilling programmes to support the industrial workforce through the clean transition, projecting around **500,000 new jobs** and drawing on Erasmus+ funding (EUR 90 million referenced for related skills programmes) and the wider Union of Skills initiative.
