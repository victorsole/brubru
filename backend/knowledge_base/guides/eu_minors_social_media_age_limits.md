# Children, Social Media and Age Limits in the EU - What the Union Is Actually Doing

## QUICK FACTS

- **THE ONE THING TO GET RIGHT**: **the EU has NOT banned social media for children, and has no law that does.** There is no EU minimum age to use a social network. What exists is (a) a **non-binding Parliament resolution** asking for one, (b) a **non-binding Council declaration**, (c) a **non-binding Commission Recommendation** on age-verification technology, and (d) **binding platform duties** under the Digital Services Act that are about risk mitigation and design, not about age bans. Every national ban in the EU is a **Member State** law, not an EU one.
- **Parliament's position (26 November 2025)**: own-initiative resolution on the **protection of minors online**, procedure **2025/2060(INI)**, rapporteur **Christel Schaldemose** (S&D, Denmark), IMCO. Adopted **483 votes to 92, with 86 abstentions**. Calls for a harmonised EU **digital minimum age of 16** for social media, video-sharing platforms and **AI companions**, with **13 as an absolute floor** and parental consent for 13 to 16. Also asks for bans on engagement-based recommender feeds and gambling-like game mechanics for minors, and raises personal liability for senior managers on serious repeated breaches. **Non-legislative: it is a demand on the Commission, not law.**
- **Second Parliament file, live now**: **2025/2081(INI)**, *Impact of social media and the online environment on young people*. **Committee responsible: CULT** (Culture and Education), rapporteur **Sandro RUOTOLO** (S&D, Italy), appointed 14 April 2025. Opinions from **IMCO** (Laura Ballarín Cereza), **LIBE** (Katarina Barley) and **FEMM**. Shadow rapporteurs from all seven groups. **Committee vote 14 July 2026**; **indicative plenary sitting 14 September 2026** (indicative, per OEIL - do not state it as fixed). Committee dossier CULT/10/02657.
- **Council's position**: the **Jutland Declaration**, Danish Presidency, **10 October 2025**. A **non-binding political commitment** to privacy-preserving age verification on social media, protection of minors from addictive design and dark patterns, and work toward a **"digital legal age"**. **25 of the 27 Member States signed. Estonia and Belgium did not.** Czechia and Estonia have publicly opposed what they call social-media bans.
- **Commission's approach - plumbing, not prohibition**: age-verification **blueprint published 14 July 2025**; the solution became **feature-ready on 15 April 2026**; the **Commission Recommendation of 28 April 2026** asks Member States to make robust age verification available **by 31 December 2026**, using **anonymous proof-of-age** rather than identity documents, aligned with the **European Digital Identity Wallet** (Regulation (EU) 2024/1183). A **special expert panel on child safety online**, announced in the **2025 State of the Union**, reported on **13 July 2026**.
- **The Court has drawn the border (16 June 2026)**: joined cases **C-188/24 WebGroup Czech Republic and NKL Associates** and **C-190/24 Coyote System**. Requiring age verification is a requirement concerning the **pursuit of an activity**, so it falls inside the **coordinated field** of the e-Commerce Directive and engages the **country-of-origin principle**. A Member State may impose age verification on a provider established in another Member State **only** through measures that are **individual (not general and abstract)**, necessary and proportionate, and **only where the home Member State has failed to act** (Article 3(4) e-Commerce Directive). **This is the quiet legal reason national bans keep running into trouble.**
- **Enforcement is where the binding action is**: on **29 April 2026** the Commission preliminarily found Instagram and Facebook in breach of the DSA over **under-13s** accessing the services (roughly **10 to 12 per cent** of under-13s in the EU do). On **10 July 2026** it preliminarily found the **addictive design** of both platforms in breach, naming **infinite scroll, autoplay, push notifications and highly personalised recommender systems**. Both are **preliminary findings, not final decisions and not fines.** Maximum exposure on a non-compliance decision is **6 per cent of total worldwide annual turnover**.
- **Public opinion**: a Eurobarometer released **12 May 2026** found protecting children online is a high priority for **92 per cent** of Europeans - the highest cross-Member-State convergence on any digital-policy topic in two years. A further Flash Eurobarometer on **13 July 2026** confirmed the concern.
- **There is a real counter-current**: the Commission has registered a **European citizens' initiative** titled *Stop Killing The Internet: No Digital ID & No Age Verification*. MEPs have tabled questions on the privacy and data-security risks of age-assurance vendors. **France's own highest court struck down its under-15 law in August 2026** on freedom-of-expression grounds.

## The single most common mistake

People conflate four different things. Keep them apart:

| Thing | What it actually is | Binding? |
|---|---|---|
| **A social-media ban for children** | A **national** law (France, Denmark, Spain, Austria, Greece...) | Binding in that Member State only |
| **Parliament's "minimum age 16"** | An own-initiative **resolution**, a political demand | **No** |
| **The Jutland Declaration** | A ministerial **political commitment** | **No** |
| **The Age Verification Recommendation** | A Commission **Recommendation** under Article 292 TFEU | **No** |
| **DSA Article 28 duties** | Binding obligations on platforms to protect minors | **Yes** |

Only the last row is enforceable EU law, and it does **not** set an age.

## GDPR Article 8 is not a social-media age limit

This is the second most common error. **GDPR Article 8** sets the age at which a child can validly
**consent to the processing of their personal data** by an information society service. The default
is **16**, and Member States may lower it to **no less than 13**. That is why the "digital age of
consent" varies across the Union.

It is **not** a minimum age to use a platform, and it only bites where the platform relies on
**consent** as its legal basis. A platform processing on another basis is outside it entirely.
Answering "children under 16 cannot use social media in the EU because of the GDPR" is wrong.

## The five EU tracks, and what each can actually do

1. **Parliament** - can demand, cannot legislate alone on its own initiative. Its instrument is the
   INI report. It has used it twice: the adopted November 2025 resolution and the live CULT file.
2. **Council / Member States** - can declare politically and can legislate nationally. The Jutland
   Declaration is the collective political signal; the bans are national acts.
3. **Commission** - holds the initiative. Its choice so far has been **infrastructure and
   enforcement** rather than a prohibition: the age-verification solution, the Recommendation, and
   DSA proceedings. The **Digital Fairness Act** is the pending vehicle where a binding EU age rule
   could plausibly land.
4. **Court of Justice** - sets the outer limit on what a Member State may impose on a provider
   established elsewhere. See the 16 June 2026 judgment above.
5. **Enforcement (DSA / DMA / GDPR)** - the only track producing binding consequences today.

## The legal architecture that already exists

| Instrument | CELEX | What it does for minors |
|---|---|---|
| **Digital Services Act** | `32022R2065` | **Article 28**: providers of platforms accessible to minors must put in place appropriate and proportionate measures for a high level of privacy, safety and security of minors. **Article 34/35**: systemic-risk assessment and mitigation for VLOPs, expressly including negative effects on minors' physical and mental well-being |
| **Audiovisual Media Services Directive** | `32010L0013`, amended by `32018L1808` | **Article 28b**: video-sharing platforms must protect minors from content that may impair their development |
| **GDPR** | `32016R0679` | **Article 8**: digital age of consent, default 16, floor 13. Not a usage ban |
| **European Digital Identity** | `32024R1183` | The wallet the age-verification solution is aligned with |
| **e-Commerce Directive** | `32000L0031` | **Article 3**: country-of-origin principle and the narrow Article 3(4) derogation the Court applied in June 2026 |
| **Child Sexual Abuse Directive** | `32011L0093` | Being recast under procedure **2024/0035(COD)** |
| **Interim ePrivacy derogation** | `32021R1232` | Voluntary CSAM detection, extended to 3 April 2028 |

**The DSA Guidelines on the protection of minors (2025)** are the benchmark the Commission uses when
assessing whether an Article 28 measure is "appropriate and proportionate".

## Member State map (as at 27 August 2026)

Use this for context, and always say a national measure is national.

- **France** - law restricting access for under-15s passed July 2026; **struck down by the
  Conseil constitutionnel in August 2026** on freedom-of-expression grounds. The President has said a
  new version will be brought forward.
- **Denmark** - government plan to bar under-15s from certain platforms; the Presidency that drove
  the Jutland Declaration.
- **Spain** - Prime Minister announced a ban for under-16s in February 2026, **subject to
  parliamentary approval**.
- **Austria** - announced plans in March 2026 for an under-14 ban.
- **Greece, Italy** - among those pushing for mandatory age gates on social platforms.
- **Estonia, Belgium** - **did not sign** the Jutland Declaration. **Czechia and Estonia** have
  publicly opposed bans.

Outside the EU, for comparison: **Australia** (under-16 ban in force since December 2025, and by
spring roughly **70 per cent** of parents of under-16s with pre-existing accounts said their child
still had access), **United Kingdom** (under-16 ban announced June 2026, effect early 2027),
**Indonesia**, **Malaysia**, **Turkey**, **Brazil** (guardian-linked accounts and a prohibition on
addictive features such as infinite scroll), **United Arab Emirates**, **New Zealand** (proposed).
**UNICEF reported in March 2026 that nearly 40 countries are discussing, proposing, adopting or
implementing age-based restrictions.**

## Enforcement against Meta, in order

| Date | Action | Status |
|---|---|---|
| 16 May 2024 | Formal DSA proceedings opened against Facebook and Instagram on protection of minors | Open |
| 29 April 2026 | **Preliminary finding**: failure to diligently assess and mitigate risks of **under-13s** accessing. Birth-date self-declaration with no controls; reporting tool up to seven clicks; risk assessment "incomplete and arbitrary" | Preliminary |
| 3 June 2026 | General Court judgment in **T-1078/23 Meta Platforms v Commission** | Decided |
| 10 July 2026 | **Preliminary finding**: **addictive design** of Instagram and Facebook in breach - infinite scroll, autoplay, push notifications, personalised recommender systems | Preliminary |

**Never describe these as fines.** They are preliminary findings; Meta has rights of defence, the
Board for Digital Services is consulted, and only a non-compliance decision can impose a penalty, up
to **6 per cent of total worldwide annual turnover** plus periodic penalty payments.

Separately, Meta has been fined **200 million euro** under the **Digital Markets Act** over its
pay-or-consent model. That is a competition-and-choice case, **not** a child-protection case, and
should not be presented as one.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- Saying **"the EU has banned social media for under-16s"**. It has not. Parliament asked for it.
- Calling the **Age Verification Recommendation** binding. It is a Recommendation under Article 292 TFEU.
- Saying Member States **"must"** have age verification by the end of 2026. They are **asked** to.
- Presenting the **preliminary findings against Meta as fines**.
- Presenting the **200 million euro DMA fine** as a child-protection penalty. It is pay-or-consent.
- Using **GDPR Article 8** as a social-media minimum age.
- Stating **14 September 2026** as a fixed plenary date. OEIL marks it **indicative**.
- Naming **IMCO** as the lead committee on 2025/2081(INI). It is **CULT**; IMCO gives an opinion.
- Forgetting the counter-current: a registered citizens' initiative against age verification, MEP
  questions on age-assurance privacy risk, and a national law struck down on free-expression grounds.
