# Adequate Minimum Wages Directive (Directive (EU) 2022/2041)

## QUICK FACTS
- Instrument: Directive (EU) 2022/2041 of the European Parliament and of the Council of 19 October 2022 on adequate minimum wages in the European Union (CELEX 32022L2041)
- Legal basis: Article 153(1)(b) and 153(2) TFEU (working conditions), NOT Article 153(5) TFEU (pay is explicitly excluded from EU competence)
- Adopted: 19 October 2022; entered into force 14 November 2022
- Transposition deadline: **15 November 2024** (Member States had 2 years to transpose)
- Does NOT: set a single EU minimum wage, force any country to introduce a statutory minimum wage, or fix wage levels directly
- Does: (1) set a procedural adequacy framework for countries that already have statutory minimum wages, and (2) require all Member States to promote collective bargaining on wage-setting
- Reference values (Article 5): indicative "double decency threshold" of **60% of the gross median wage and/or 50% of the gross mean (average) wage**, used only to guide -- not mandate -- assessment of adequacy
- Collective bargaining coverage threshold (Article 4): Member States where coverage falls below **80%** must establish, after consulting social partners, an action plan with a timeline and concrete measures to progressively increase coverage
- CJEU challenge: Denmark v European Parliament and Council, **Case C-19/23**, action for annulment filed January 2023, supported by Sweden; Grand Chamber judgment delivered **11 November 2025**
- CJEU outcome: the Court **partially annulled** parts of Article 5 -- specifically the words "including the elements referred to in paragraph 2" in Article 5(1), all of Article 5(2), and the phrase "provided that the application of that mechanism does not lead to a decrease of the statutory minimum wage" in Article 5(3) -- but **upheld the Directive in all other respects**, confirming the EU's competence to legislate on working conditions and promote collective bargaining while drawing a line against binding, substantive wage-level requirements
- Monitoring layer: Eurofound's Minimum Wage database tracks statutory and collectively-agreed minimum wage levels and updates across Member States
- Coverage gap it targets: as of the Directive's adoption, roughly half of EU workers covered by collective bargaining agreements had below-80% coverage in several Member States; minimum wage adequacy (relative to median/average wage) varied widely across the bloc

## What the Directive Actually Does

The Adequate Minimum Wages Directive is a framework directive, not a harmonisation instrument. It respects the diversity of national wage-setting systems and explicitly preserves the autonomy of social partners, including in the Nordic and other collective-bargaining-based systems where statutory minimum wages do not exist (Denmark, Sweden, and to varying extents Italy, Austria, Finland and Cyprus).

It has two separate tracks that apply differently depending on a Member State's existing system.

### Track 1: Adequacy of statutory minimum wages (only where they exist)

For the majority of Member States that already operate a statutory minimum wage, the Directive requires:

- Clear, stable criteria for setting and updating the minimum wage, established according to national practice
- Regular and timely updates, at least every two years (four years where an automatic indexation mechanism is used)
- Use of indicative reference values -- commonly the 60% gross median / 50% gross mean "double decency threshold" -- to guide (not dictate) assessment of adequacy
- Limiting and justifying the use of variations and deductions from the minimum wage
- Effective involvement of social partners in the setting and updating of statutory minimum wages, including via consultative bodies

### Track 2: Promoting collective bargaining (applies to ALL Member States)

Every Member State, regardless of whether it has a statutory minimum wage, must promote collective bargaining on wage-setting. Where collective bargaining coverage falls below 80% of the workforce, the Member State must:

- Provide a framework of enabling conditions for collective bargaining, either in law after consulting social partners or by agreement with social partners
- Establish an action plan to progressively increase collective bargaining coverage, with a clear timeline and concrete measures
- Review and, where necessary, update the action plan at least every five years

### Monitoring, data and enforcement

Member States must improve data collection on minimum wage coverage and adequacy, report to the Commission, and ensure workers have effective access to dispute resolution and protection against victimisation for asserting their rights under the Directive. Public authorities' procurement and concession contracts must comply with applicable wage-setting obligations, including collective agreements.

## The CJEU Challenge: Case C-19/23

Denmark, supported by Sweden, brought an action for annulment before the Court of Justice in January 2023, arguing the Directive as a whole exceeded EU competence. Article 153(5) TFEU explicitly excludes "pay" from the areas in which the EU may legislate under the social policy title. Denmark argued that setting adequacy criteria and reference values for minimum wages amounted to indirect regulation of pay, disguised as regulation of "working conditions."

The Grand Chamber ruled on 11 November 2025. The Court:

- **Rejected** the argument that the Directive as a whole was ultra vires; the general framework, the promotion of collective bargaining, and the procedural adequacy criteria fall within the EU's competence on working conditions
- **Annulled** the specific substantive elements of Article 5 that came closest to prescribing a wage-setting mechanism or outcome (the "double decency threshold" cross-reference in Article 5(1), all of Article 5(2), and the anti-decrease clause in Article 5(3))
- Confirmed that the EU may promote collective bargaining and set procedural adequacy safeguards, but may not impose binding, substantive requirements on the composition or level of wages themselves

The practical effect: Member States retain full discretion over the actual level of their statutory minimum wage and are no longer bound even indirectly by the specific reference-value mechanism in the annulled parts of Article 5. The collective bargaining coverage provisions (Article 4, the 80% threshold and action plan requirement) were not affected by the judgment and remain fully in force.

## Why It Matters for Advocacy

- Transposition deadline (15 November 2024) has passed; infringement proceedings against Member States that failed to transpose, or transposed incompletely, are a live monitoring area
- The partial annulment narrows what the Commission can enforce under Article 5 but leaves the collective bargaining coverage obligations (Article 4) untouched -- stakeholders in low-coverage Member States (below 80%) should track national action plans
- Employers' organisations and trade unions both have a direct stake: the Directive channels engagement through social partner consultation at every stage (adequacy criteria, reference values, action plans)
- The case is a significant precedent for the limits of EU competence on pay-adjacent social policy under Article 153(5) TFEU, relevant to any future EU initiative that touches wage-setting, even indirectly
