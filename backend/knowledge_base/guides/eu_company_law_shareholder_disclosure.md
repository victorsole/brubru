# Shareholder disclosure, company law and the GDPR (Case C-798/24)

## QUICK FACTS
- **LATEST (Thursday 3 September 2026 -- THE COURT RULES ON PUBLIC SHAREHOLDER REGISTERS)**: in **Case C-798/24 [Jautiva]**, *A and Others v Latvijas Republikas Saeima*, the Court of Justice (**First Chamber**) held that **EU company law does not require member states to publish the identities of all shareholders**, and that **the GDPR read with the Charter precludes national law that makes every shareholder's personal data publicly available without any condition such as demonstrating a legitimate interest**. Judgment of **3 September 2026**, on a reference from the **Satversmes tiesa** (Constitutional Court, Latvia).
- **CELEX:** 62024CJ0798 -- https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:62024CJ0798
- **Court press release:** No 115/2026, 3 September 2026 -- https://curia.europa.eu/site/upload/docs/application/pdf/2026-09/cp260115en.pdf
- **Case name:** [Jautiva] is a **fictitious name** assigned by the Court; it is not a party's real name.
- **Chamber:** First Chamber. **Rapporteur:** T. von Danwitz (Vice-President of the Court). **Advocate General:** R. Norkus, opinion delivered **18 December 2025**.
- **Language of the case:** Latvian. **Referring court:** Satversmes tiesa, order of 14 November 2024, received 19 November 2024.
- **Acts interpreted:** **Directive (EU) 2017/1132** on certain aspects of company law, Article 14(d)(ii), as amended by Directive (EU) 2019/1151 (CELEX 32017L1132, 32019L1151) - and **Regulation (EU) 2016/679 (GDPR)** Articles 5 and 6 (CELEX 32016R0679), read with **Articles 7 and 8 of the Charter**.
- **Who intervened:** the Latvian, Polish, Finnish, Swedish and Norwegian governments, the European Parliament, the Council and the Commission. Five governments plus all three institutions is unusually heavy participation, and marks the case as one the member states expected to change national registers.

## What the Court actually ruled

The operative part has **two limbs**. Quote them apart; they answer different questions.

**Limb 1 -- company law imposes no such duty.** Article 14(d) of Directive (EU) 2017/1132 "must be
interpreted as **not requiring the disclosure of information relating to all the shareholders** of the
companies covered by that provision, **including the minority shareholders** of such companies."

The referring court had asked whether the concept of *"persons who take part in the administration,
supervision or control of a company"* in Article 14(d)(ii) reaches ordinary shareholders. It does not.
So a member state publishing a full shareholder list is not doing so because EU company law told it to.

**Limb 2 -- and data protection forbids doing it unconditionally.** GDPR Articles 5 and 6, read in the
light of Charter Articles 7 and 8, **preclude** national legislation that requires the personal data of
**all** shareholders of public limited liability companies to be **made available to the public** --
specifically each shareholder's **identity and contact details, the class, number and nominal value of
the shares held, and the number of votes attaching to them** -- **where access to that data is not
subject to any conditions, such as demonstrating a legitimate interest**.

Note what the Court did **not** do. It did not strike down the objectives Latvia invoked: a transparent
business environment, protecting third parties, preventing money laundering and terrorist financing and
the proliferation of weapons of mass destruction, and supplying information needed to implement national,
international and EU sanctions. Those aims are accepted. What fails is **unconditional public access** as
the means. A legitimate-interest gate is the example the Court itself gives.

## Why this matters

**It extends the Luxembourg Business Registers line to shareholder registers.** In Joined Cases C-37/20
and C-601/20 (22 November 2022) the Court invalidated the provision of the anti-money-laundering
directive that opened **beneficial ownership** registers to the general public. C-798/24 applies the same
proportionality reasoning one step further out, to **shareholder** data held under company law, and again
lands on conditioned rather than open access. Anyone advising on either register should now read the two
together. See `eu_anti_money_laundering`.

**It constrains "transparency by publication" as a legislative reflex.** Sanctions implementation and
AML were both argued here as justifications and both were insufficient to carry unconditional publication.
A proposal that makes personal data public in order to make enforcement easier now has to explain why an
access condition would not do the same job.

**It lands in the middle of the EU Inc. negotiation.** The 28th-regime corporate framework
(`2026/0074(COD)`) is being amended in JURI and EMPL in September 2026 and creates a new EU-level company
form with its own registration and disclosure architecture. Any disclosure rule drafted into that
instrument now has to survive limb 2. See `28th_regime_innovation_act`.

## How to answer questions about this case

- If asked **"must shareholders be published?"** -- no, EU company law does not require it (limb 1).
- If asked **"can a member state publish them anyway?"** -- not unconditionally; access must be gated,
  for example on a demonstrated legitimate interest (limb 2). The judgment does not prescribe one single
  permissible model.
- If asked **"does this abolish company transparency?"** -- no. It regulates **who may see what, on what
  showing**. The listed public-interest objectives are all accepted as legitimate.
- The judgment binds as an interpretation of EU law from the date of the ruling. Member states with
  open shareholder registers, Latvia included, must revisit them; the judgment does not name a deadline.
