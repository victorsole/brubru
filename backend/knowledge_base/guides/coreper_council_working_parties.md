# Coreper I, Coreper II, and the Council Working Party Machinery

## QUICK FACTS
- Topic: Coreper, Council working parties -- how the Council of the EU pre-cooks its decisions
- Treaty basis: Article 240 TFEU (Coreper) + Article 16(7) TEU (Council configurations)
- Coreper II (Permanent Representatives, i.e. ambassadors): covers GAC, FAC, ECOFIN, JHA -- the heavy political files
- Coreper I (Deputy Permanent Representatives): covers AGRIFISH, COMPET, ENV, EYCS, EPSCO, TTE -- the sectoral/technical files
- Working parties: approximately 150 standing Council working parties and committees at the technical expert level
- I-item (A-point): item placed on the Council ministerial agenda for formal adoption WITHOUT debate -- fully settled below
- II-item (B-point): item placed on the Council ministerial agenda FOR debate -- Coreper could not achieve consensus
- Antici group: senior officials from each Permanent Representation, prepares Coreper II agendas and tracks the political temperature
- Mertens group: equivalent preparatory body for Coreper I agendas
- PSC (Political and Security Committee): Brussels-based ambassadorial body for CFSP/CSDP files; runs in parallel to Coreper II
- GSC Legal Service: General Secretariat of the Council lawyers who draft compromise texts and give binding legal opinions on treaty basis
- Key insight: approximately 85 per cent of Council decisions are fully pre-agreed at working party or Coreper level before ministers meet
- Source: consilium.europa.eu/en/council-eu/preparatory-bodies/ (browser; returns 403 to automated crawlers)

Roughly 85 per cent of Council decisions never receive a genuine ministerial debate. A file is line-by-line negotiated among national officials in a working party, filtered through Coreper, and placed on the ministerial table as a formality. Understanding this machinery is essential for anyone seeking to influence the Council. See also `council_guide.md` for Council structure fundamentals and `eu_legislative_procedures_explained.md` for the full co-decision process.

## Coreper: Structure and Legal Basis

Article 240(1) TFEU establishes the Committee of Permanent Representatives (Coreper) as responsible for preparing the work of the Council and carrying out tasks assigned to it by the Council. Each member state maintains a Permanent Representation to the EU in Brussels, staffed by diplomats and national civil servants seconded from capitals.

Coreper operates at two levels:

**Coreper II** consists of the Permanent Representatives (ambassadors). These are the most senior diplomats in each Permanent Representation, typically with the rank of ambassador to the EU. Coreper II handles the politically sensitive and horizontally significant files: General Affairs Council (GAC), Foreign Affairs Council (FAC), Economic and Financial Affairs Council (ECOFIN), and Justice and Home Affairs Council (JHA). Because these configurations cut across domestic political fault lines -- fiscal surveillance, asylum, foreign policy, rule of law -- Coreper II requires the highest-ranking national representative.

**Coreper I** consists of the Deputy Permanent Representatives. They handle the more sectoral, technically complex configurations: Agriculture and Fisheries (AGRIFISH), Competitiveness (COMPET), Environment (ENV), Education, Youth, Culture and Sport (EYCS), Employment and Social Policy (EPSCO), and Transport, Telecommunications and Energy (TTE). This does not mean these files are less contested -- environment, transport and competitiveness produce some of the most difficult negotiations -- but they tend to be governed more by technical than geopolitical considerations.

Both formations meet weekly (usually Wednesdays for Coreper II, Thursdays for Coreper I) in Brussels except during Council recess. Meetings are chaired by the Permanent Representative or Deputy from the rotating Presidency member state.

For the current Permanent Representatives and their deputies by member state, see `european_council_and_council_personnel.md`.

## I-Items and II-Items: The Agenda Mechanism

The most practical concept for anyone monitoring Council negotiations is the distinction between I-items (also written as "A-points") and II-items ("B-points") on a ministerial Council agenda.

**I-item (A-point):** The file has been fully agreed at working party level or, if not there, at Coreper. All 27 delegations have signalled acceptance of the text. The item is placed on the Council agenda purely for formal adoption, without any ministerial discussion. In a typical Council meeting, the I-items are approved en bloc at the start, often within a few minutes. A minister can request an item be removed from the I-item list and referred back to Coreper -- this is rare but possible.

**II-item (B-point):** Coreper could not reach consensus, or the Presidency has judged that the political visibility of a genuine ministerial exchange is desirable (political orientations, progress reports, public debates). Ministers debate the item. The outcome may be a general approach (the Council's negotiating mandate for trilogues), a partial general approach, a set of Council conclusions, or simply an exchange of views that guides further negotiations at lower levels.

A file can move back and forth. A II-item that produces a political steer is sent back down to the working party for further technical work, then re-examined by Coreper, potentially becoming a I-item at a future Council meeting. This iterative cycle is the rhythm of Council decision-making.

## The Approximately 150 Working Parties

Working parties are the engine room. There are approximately 150 standing Council working parties and committees, plus ad hoc groups convened for specific files. They are composed of national civil servants, either from the Permanent Representations in Brussels or flying in from capitals as subject-matter specialists.

Working parties meet as often as a file demands: a major regulation under active trilogue may meet two or three times a week; a dormant file may not meet for months. The rotating Presidency chairs all working party meetings, giving the Presidency team significant control over pacing, framing, and compromise architecture.

### Key working parties by policy area (illustrative, not exhaustive)

**Internal market and competitiveness:** Working Party on Internal Market (Horizontal), Working Party on Technical Harmonisation, Working Party on Intellectual Property.

**Environment and climate:** Working Party on Environment, Working Party on International Environmental Issues.

**Justice and home affairs:** Working Party on Asylum and Migration, Working Party on Police Cooperation, Working Party on Data Protection.

**Economic and financial affairs:** Working Party on Tax Questions (Direct Taxation; Indirect Taxation/VAT), Working Party on Financial Services, Economic Policy Committee, Budget Committee.

**Foreign affairs and defence:** Working Party on Foreign Relations (RELEX), Working Party on Public International Law (COJUR), PSC-related Politico-Military Group (PMG), Military Committee.

**Agriculture:** Special Committee on Agriculture (SCA), which functions as the working party level for AGRIFISH files and reports directly to Coreper I.

**General affairs and horizontal:** Working Party of Legal/Linguistic Experts (JURILINGUIST), which conducts final legal-linguistic review of all adopted texts across all configurations.

When a new legislative proposal arrives from the Commission, the General Secretariat of the Council allocates it to the relevant working party within days. The working party secretariat prepares a commentary ("note") identifying open issues, and delegations begin submitting their positions in writing.

## Antici and Mertens Groups

Both Antici and Mertens groups are composed of senior officials from each Permanent Representation, one step below the level of Permanent Representative or Deputy. Their function is to prepare Coreper meetings.

**Antici group:** Named after Paolo Massimo Antici, the Italian diplomat who created the format. Prepares Coreper II meetings. The Antici group meets in the week before Coreper II to consolidate working party reports, identify which items are ready for I-item treatment, flag political sensitivities, and draft the provisional Coreper II agenda. Antici officials also physically attend Coreper II meetings as note-takers and information-passers, transmitting agreed positions back to capitals in near-real time via the Coreper II channel.

**Mertens group:** The equivalent for Coreper I, named after Fernand Mertens. Performs the same preparatory and logistical function for the Deputies.

Both groups are significant beyond their administrative role: the debate within Antici/Mertens is itself an informal political filtering process where a great deal of soft signalling and pre-agreement happens before the formal Coreper meeting.

## PSC: Political and Security Committee for CSDP

The Political and Security Committee (PSC) is an ambassadorial-level body established under Article 38 TEU. It monitors the international situation in areas covered by the Common Foreign and Security Policy (CFSP) and Common Security and Defence Policy (CSDP), and contributes to the definition of policies.

PSC is composed of Brussels-based ambassadors from each member state, distinct from the Permanent Representatives to the EU. It meets at least twice per week. PSC does not report through Coreper II for CSDP-specific files: it has a direct channel to the FAC (Defence). However, for matters with both CFSP and general legal implications, coordination with Coreper II does occur.

PSC is supported by the European Union Military Committee (EUMC), composed of Chiefs of Defence or their representatives, and by the EU Military Staff (EUMS) within the European External Action Service (EEAS). It also coordinates with the Politico-Military Group (PMG) and the Committee for Civilian Aspects of Crisis Management (CIVCOM).

For Brubru users tracking CSDP files: PSC agendas are not systematically published in advance. The best public signal is the FAC (Defence) ministerial agenda on the Council website and press releases after PSC meetings on consilium.europa.eu.

## How a File Travels from Working Party to Council

A standard legislative file follows this sequence:

1. **Commission proposal adopted** by the College, transmitted to the Council and European Parliament simultaneously.

2. **Council Secretariat routing:** The GSC allocates the proposal to the relevant working party. A note is circulated identifying the legal basis, applicable voting rule, and outstanding issues from a preliminary legal review.

3. **Working party scrutiny:** National experts examine the proposal article by article. The Presidency tables compromise texts. Delegations record their positions in square-bracketed texts. This phase can take weeks to years depending on complexity.

4. **Reporting to Coreper:** When the working party has exhausted its room for manoeuvre or has reached provisional agreement on a substantial portion, the Presidency reports to Coreper with a progress note or a request for political guidance. Items fully agreed are listed as I-items on the Coreper agenda (for endorsement as I-items on the Council agenda). Items needing political steer are listed as II-items.

5. **Coreper examination:** Coreper can endorse the working party outcome (sending it forward as a I-item to Council), send it back for further work, or -- for politically charged questions -- have a substantive debate at ambassadorial level. A Coreper agreement at this stage may be recorded as a "Coreper I-item" on the Council agenda.

6. **Council adoption:** I-items are adopted en bloc without debate. II-items receive ministerial exchange, which may produce a general approach, partial agreement, or mandate for further work.

7. **Trilogue (for ordinary legislative procedure):** Once the Council has a general approach, informal negotiations with the European Parliament begin. Presidency representatives (often working party chairs or senior Coreper officials) lead the Council side of trilogues. Results are submitted back to Coreper for formal endorsement before the Council adopts the final text.

8. **Legal-linguistic review:** The JURILINGUIST working party carries out the final legal-linguistic verification across all 24 official languages before the act is signed and published in the Official Journal.

For the full procedure map including co-decision, consultation, consent, and delegated/implementing acts, see `eu_legislative_procedures_explained.md`.

## The Role of the GSC Legal Service

The General Secretariat of the Council (GSC) Legal Service is one of the most influential bodies in the EU legislative process, yet it is rarely discussed in advocacy guides. Its functions include:

**Legal basis opinions:** The GSC Legal Service gives formal opinions on whether a Commission proposal has the correct treaty basis. An incorrect legal basis affects the voting rule (QMV vs unanimity) and whether the European Parliament has co-decision rights. These opinions are not always public but are highly influential in working party debates.

**Compromise text drafting:** For complex negotiations, GSC lawyers work alongside the Presidency to draft compromise texts that are legally consistent and technically precise. This is particularly significant in JHA and ECOFIN files where financial and criminal-law language must be exactly right.

**Council positions in Court proceedings:** The GSC Legal Service represents the Council before the Court of Justice of the EU. Its internal positions on legality often shape what the Council is willing to accept in negotiations.

**Interinstitutional coordination:** GSC lawyers liaise directly with the European Parliament's Legal Service and the Commission's Legal Service on questions of treaty compliance and institutional balance.

For Brubru users: GSC Legal Service opinions do not appear in standard Council document repositories, but where available they are released via the Council's document register at register.consilium.europa.eu. Searching for the dossier number with "Legal Service" in the title will surface them.

## Practical Advice for Brubru Users Tracking Coreper Agendas

**Where to find Coreper and working party documents:**
Council document register: register.consilium.europa.eu. Use the advanced search with the interinstitutional number (e.g. 2023/0012(COD)) or the document series (ST = Council doc). Working party notes, Presidency compromise texts, and progress reports are released with varying delays (some immediately, some after Council adoption, some classified).

**Presidency provisional agendas:**
Coreper I and Coreper II provisional agendas are published on consilium.europa.eu under the "Meetings" section. These appear typically 7-10 days before the meeting. The final agenda is circulated 2-3 days before. Checking these agendas reveals whether a file has been placed as a I-item (i.e. text agreed) or II-item (i.e. still contested).

**Presidency compromise texts:**
These are the single most important working documents in Council negotiations. They appear as ST (Council document) notes with "Presidency compromise" in the title. Subscribing to notifications via the Council register for a specific interinstitutional reference will flag new Presidency texts as they are published.

**Capital contacts vs Brussels contacts:**
For files with strong technical content at working party level, the relevant national expert in the capital ministry is often more influential than the Permanent Representation official. For files with high political salience (the Coreper II configurations: FAC, ECOFIN, JHA, GAC), the Permanent Representation in Brussels is the key entry point. See `permanent_representations_brussels_engagement.md` for engagement strategy with PermReps.

**Tracking the Presidency trio programme:**
The Presidency rotates every six months, but the 18-month Trio Programme sets out shared legislative priorities for three consecutive Presidencies. A file prioritised by the Trio is likely to receive intensive working party treatment and a general approach before the Trio period ends. See `council_presidency_trio_programme.md` for current Trio priorities and legislative calendar.

**Signals that a file is close to general approach:**
The strongest signals are: (a) the Presidency tables a "revised Presidency compromise" for the first time as a "clean text" (square brackets removed); (b) a Coreper agenda item is listed as "examination with a view to reaching a general approach"; (c) the Presidency formally schedules a political discussion as a II-item at the next Council configuration meeting.
