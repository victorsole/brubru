- **NOT the same act as CSDDD.** CSDDD is a Directive on corporate due diligence duties. The FLR is a Regulation that bans products. A question about one is not a question about the other.

## What the Regulation actually does

It bans products made with forced labour from the EU market. Not companies, not sectors, not countries
— **products**. A product that falls foul of it cannot be placed or made available on the EU market, and
cannot be exported from it.

The definition comes from Article 2 of ILO Forced Labour Convention No 29 (1930): work or service
exacted from a person under coercion, for which the person has not offered themselves voluntarily. Three
elements — work or service, lack of voluntary consent, coercion — and **involuntariness and coercion must
both be present at the same point in the analysed period**.

## Scope, stated precisely

- **All products, regardless of origin, type or sector**, placed or made available on the EU market or
  exported from it.
- **"In whole or in part"** — forced labour at any stage of extraction, harvesting, production,
  manufacturing, working or processing, anywhere in the supply chain, inside or outside the EU.
  **The size of the tainted share is irrelevant.** There is no de minimis.
- The FLR's definition of "product" is **wider than the New Legislative Framework**: it also covers
  agricultural products within Article 38 TFEU and extracted products such as minerals and raw materials.
- **Services and domestic work are NOT covered**, even when performed with forced labour, because the
  Regulation reaches products only.
- Excluded from the forced-labour definition, following ILO Convention No 29: purely military compulsory
  service, normal civic obligations, work following a court conviction under public authority
  supervision, and work in emergency situations.

**The retroactivity point most readers miss:** the Regulation applies to all products placed or made
available on the EU market **from 14 December 2027, even if those products or their components were
produced or imported into the EU before that date.** Stock manufactured in 2026 is in scope if it is
still being sold in 2028.

## The three types of forced labour

1. **Privately imposed** — by individuals, groups or companies. Most cases worldwide, concentrated in
   industry, services, agriculture and domestic work.
2. **State-imposed (SIFL)** — by state authorities, agents acting for the state, or entities exercising
   state-like authority. Arises from national laws, policies or practices. ILO Convention No 105
   supplements No 29 by barring states from using forced labour as political coercion or punishment for
   holding or expressing political views, among other grounds.
3. **Forced child labour** — treated separately in the guidelines.

## Risk indicators (Notice Section 3.4)

**Fourteen general indicators:** forced recruitment · deception · exploitation of debt · hazardous or
degrading working conditions · onerous working hours or schedule · degrading work-related living
conditions · abusive additional obligations · physical or sexual violence · abuse of isolation ·
restrictions on workers' movements · retention of cash, assets or identity documents · withholding of
wages · threats or intimidation · abuse of vulnerability.

**Ten structural indicators specific to state-imposed forced labour:** imprisonment and detention ·
manipulation of custodial conditions · financial penalty imposed by the state · withdrawal or denial of
state services, legal status and social entitlements · expropriation or confiscation of property, land,
housing or means of production · employment sanctions imposed by the state or state enterprises · legal
prohibition on employment termination · surveillance and social monitoring · state-induced psychological
coercion and ideological pressure · collective and third-party penalties.

## How enforcement will work

**Who investigates.** A lead competent authority in a Member State, or the Commission itself where the
suspected forced labour is outside the EU.

**Prioritisation (Notice 4.3.1) — three criteria assessed together:** the scale and severity of the
suspected forced labour (including SIFL), and the two further criteria the Notice sets alongside it. Scale
rises with the number of people affected, in absolute terms and by prevalence; severity is judged on
elements including how many forced-labour indicators are present.

**Information sources the authority draws on:** the single information submission point · the **EU forced
labour risk database**, which will flag risks by product and geographical area · the risk indicators
above · the **ICSMS Forced Labour Module** (past ban-violation decisions and inter-authority information)
· other authorities · civil society, trade unions and independent experts.

**Two phases.** A preliminary phase (requests for information from the operator and from other product
suppliers, further fact-finding) and, if substantiated, a full investigation with the operator's right to
be informed and to be heard.

**Non-cooperation has a defined consequence (Notice 4.8.2).** If an operator or public authority fails to
cooperate and thereby impedes evidence-gathering, the authority **must establish the violation on the
basis of any other facts available**. Examples the Notice gives: refusing requested information without
valid justification; missing the prescribed time limit without valid justification; providing incomplete,
misleading or incorrect information, or a large volume of irrelevant information, to block the
investigation.

**Outcome.** A ban-violation decision prohibits placing the products on the EU market and orders
withdrawal and disposal. **All decisions are published on the Forced Labour Single Portal.** Customs
enforce at the border: suspension of release, then refusal, seizure and disposal.

**The strategic-supply-chain carve-out (Notice 4.8.4).** Where products form part of a supply chain of
strategic or critical importance for the EU, the authority may, to avoid disrupting the single market,
decline to order disposal and instead require the operator to **withhold the products at its own expense**
for a defined, proportionate period while eliminating the forced labour. If the operator demonstrates it
has done so in time, the decision is withdrawn for the future.

**Penalties.** Article 37(2) FLR sets the framework; Member States retain discretion on methodology but
**must take utmost account of these guidelines**. The Notice sets out five logical steps, beginning with
identifying the factual circumstances and the nature and extent of the non-compliance, then assessing
gravity and duration to calculate the basic amount.

## What companies are expected to do

The Notice's Chapter 6 is a due-diligence manual built on the **OECD six-step framework**:

1. Integrate forced-labour due diligence into company policies and risk management systems.
2. Identify and assess forced-labour risks in operations, supply chains and business relationships.
3. Prevent, mitigate and bring to an end the risks found.
4. Monitor and assess implementation and results.
5. and 6. Communicate, and provide for or cooperate in remediation.

**SMEs are treated proportionately.** They are not expected to conduct broad consultations or engagement
activities, but can engage directly with key suppliers, customers or employees where relevant — for
instance when the EU forced-labour risk database flags their sector or geographical area. The Commission
must provide support measures under Article 10 FLR, and the Single Portal is to carry resources for them.

## Submitting information (Notice Chapter 7)

Required by Article 11, first paragraph, point (h) FLR. **The single information submission point will be
accessible via the Forced Labour Single Portal from 14 December 2027.** Any natural or legal person, or
any association without legal personality, may submit — victims, businesses, business organisations,
NGOs, consumer organisations, trade unions — directly or through an intermediary. Submissions may be
anonymous, though that limits follow-up.

Petitioners must act in good faith and give reasons and evidence; manifestly incomplete, unfounded or
bad-faith submissions are discarded. **Petitioners who qualify as whistleblowers** under Directive (EU)
2019/1937 and acquired the information in a work-related context **must receive that Directive's
protection against retaliation** — the FLR amends the Whistleblower Directive to this end.

## How this differs from CSDDD (asked constantly, answered wrongly)

| | Forced Labour Regulation 2024/3015 | CSDDD 2024/1760 |
|---|---|---|
| Instrument | Regulation, directly applicable | Directive, needs transposition |
| Target | **A product** | **A company's conduct** |
| Duty | None until a ban decision; then withdrawal | Ongoing due-diligence obligations |
| Who is caught | Any economic operator of any size | Only large companies above thresholds |
| Consequence | Market ban, seizure, disposal | Supervisory action, civil liability |
| Applies from | **14 December 2027** | Wave 1 from 26 July 2028 (post Stop-the-clock) |

An answer about one is not an answer about the other. Say which act you are describing.

## Open questions as of 3 September 2026

- The **EU forced labour risk database** and the **Forced Labour Single Portal** are provided for but not
  yet live; the Notice describes what they will contain.
- The **ICSMS Forced Labour Module and the Customs Risk Management System (CRMS) are not yet integrated.**
  Until they are, competent and customs authorities share information under **national protocols to be put
  in place before the Regulation starts to apply**.
- Member States must designate competent authorities and set penalty rules before 14 December 2027.
