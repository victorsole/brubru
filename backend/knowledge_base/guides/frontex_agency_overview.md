# Frontex: European Border and Coast Guard Agency

These are preliminary detection counts and not a count of individual people: Frontex states that the same person may be detected several times, at different locations or on different occasions. August alone recorded some **10 700** detections, 38% fewer than August 2025. - Agency: Frontex, formally the European Border and Coast Guard Agency
- Headquarters: Warsaw, Poland (Article 93(5) of the founding Regulation, per a formal Headquarters Agreement with Poland)
- Legal basis: Regulation (EU) 2019/1896 of the European Parliament and of the Council of 13 November 2019 on the European Border and Coast Guard (OJ L 295, 14.11.2019, p. 1), repealing Regulations (EU) No 1052/2013 and (EU) 2016/1624
- Structure: Frontex is one half of the "European Border and Coast Guard" -- the other half is the border and coast guard authorities of the Member States and Schengen-associated countries. The two act as a shared responsibility, not a substitute for national border guards
- Mandate history: Council Regulation (EC) 2007/2004 (2004, original agency) -> Regulation (EU) 2016/1624 (2016, rebrand + expanded mandate as the European Border and Coast Guard Agency) -> Regulation (EU) 2019/1896 (2019, current mandate, creates the Standing Corps)
- Standing Corps: described by Frontex as "the European Union's first uniformed law enforcement service"; target of 10,000 operational staff by 2027 (an earlier interim target of 5,000 by 2021 was part of the original 2019 design)
- Standing Corps categories (as run today): Category 1 -- border guards and other staff directly employed by Frontex; Category 2 -- staff seconded from Member States for long-term deployment; Category 3 -- staff deployed from Member States for short-term missions. The 2019 Regulation also created a transitional Category 4, a Member State rapid-reaction reserve for unexpected needs while the Corps built up to critical mass; its 2023 target of 1,500 nominated staff was reached and it is being phased out as Categories 1-3 mature, with reallocation capacity in place from 2025
- Standing Corps target breakdown for 2027: roughly 3,000 Category 1 (Frontex-employed) officers plus around 7,000 officers seconded or deployed from Member States
- Executive powers: Standing Corps officers can carry out border checks and may carry a firearm, but always operate under the authority and instructions of the host Member State's border guards -- they do not act as an autonomous EU police force independent of national control
- Fundamental rights architecture: an independent Fundamental Rights Officer (FRO) monitors Frontex's compliance with EU and international fundamental rights law and advises the Executive Director; a team of Fundamental Rights Monitors assesses individual operations, including returns; a Consultative Forum on Fundamental Rights (established 2012, bringing together EU institutions, international bodies and civil society organisations) gives independent advice and publishes an annual report
- ETIAS Central Unit: Frontex hosts and operates the ETIAS Central Unit (created under Regulation (EU) 2018/1240), a 24/7 operation of 200-plus staff that verifies ETIAS applications flagged by EU database hits, sets risk indicators and screening rules, and supports Member States, carriers and travellers
- EUROSUR: Frontex runs the European Border Surveillance System (EUROSUR) framework, which pools situational awareness and risk analysis across Member States and the Agency at the external borders
- Core tasks: joint operations at land, sea and air external borders; risk analysis and vulnerability assessments; technical and operational assistance to Member States and, via status agreements or working arrangements, to non-EU countries; support for return operations and a return coordination role; training standards for European integrated border management (IBM)
- Executive Director: Hans Leijtens, in post since 1 March 2023 (previously Commander of the Royal Netherlands Marechaussee); verify currency before citing, as the mandate is time-limited and subject to Management Board renewal or replacement
- Accountability debates: Frontex has faced sustained scrutiny over alleged involvement in or awareness of pushbacks at external borders (notably the Aegean and Balkan routes), the adequacy of its fundamental rights safeguards, and the balance between its search-and-rescue notification duties and its border-control mandate. Frontex supports EU Member States and Schengen-associated countries in managing the external borders of the Schengen area and in tackling cross-border crime, while national authorities retain primary responsibility for their own stretches of border.

## Irregular Crossing Detections, January to August 2026

Frontex publishes monthly preliminary figures on irregular crossings at the external borders. The release of 11 September 2026 covers the first eight months of 2026.

The unit of measurement matters when citing these numbers. Frontex counts **detections of irregular border crossings**, not persons: the Agency's own note states that the same person may cross the external border several times, in different locations or on different occasions, and each detection is counted. A Brubru answer should therefore say "detections of irregular crossings" rather than treating the figure as a headcount of arrivals. The data are preliminary, are compiled from what national authorities report, and are subject to later revision.

| Eastern Mediterranean | More than 24 200 | Down 25% |
| Central Mediterranean | Around 19 400 | Down 55% |
| Western Mediterranean | Around 15 900 | Up 34% |

| The Channel, on exit towards the UK (not in the EU entry total) | Some 26 600 | Down 43% |

Route detail from the same release:

- **Eastern Mediterranean**, the busiest route. The corridor from Libya to the Greek island of Crete was the most active over the period as a whole, but by July the land border with Türkiye had become the busiest section, ahead of the Aegean islands. Some 3 000 detections were recorded in August. Main nationalities detected: Afghan, Sudanese and Bangladeshi.
- **Central Mediterranean**, the second-steepest fall of all routes. Libya remained the main departure hub at around two thirds of arrivals on the route, ahead of the Algerian and Tunisian corridors, with enforcement operations, detention campaigns and returns by the Libyan and Tunisian authorities suppressing departures. Main nationalities detected: Bangladeshi, Somali and Algerian.
- **Western Mediterranean**, the only major route to increase. Algeria remained the main departure country, with the Balearic Islands and the Spanish mainland the principal destinations. Pressure surged in July to a three-year monthly high before easing in August, reflecting a shift of smuggling routes towards Algerian shores as controls tightened in Morocco and on neighbouring routes.
- **Western African**, the steepest decline of all routes. Preventive measures by Mauritania, and more recently by Senegal in cooperation with Spain and the EU, have significantly reduced departures, though the flow remains volatile and an increasing share of boats set out from The Gambia, Senegal and Guinea-Bissau on longer and more dangerous journeys towards the Canary Islands.

Three caveats belong with any citation of these figures. First, the totals **exclude** the large-scale movement of people from Morocco into Ceuta at the end of July 2026: Frontex expects no definitive total, because most of those who crossed returned to Morocco without being registered. Second, the Channel figure counts attempts detected on exit towards the United Kingdom, including both those who reached the UK and those prevented from leaving, so it does not form part of the EU entry total. Third, falling detections do not mean falling risk: the International Organization for Migration recorded more than 1 800 deaths in the Mediterranean so far in 2026.

The period covered the first full summer under the Pact on Migration and Asylum, which introduces a single standardised screening process at the external borders. Frontex officers support Member States in establishing the nationality of arrivals, collecting biometric data and verifying documents. Some 3 800 officers were present at the external borders.

Executive Director Hans Leijtens framed the trend as fragile rather than settled, noting that a quiet border can change within hours and that smuggling networks move and wait for an opening rather than disappearing when numbers fall.

## What Frontex Is and Is Not

Frontex is an EU decentralised agency, not a supranational border police force that replaces national border guards. Regulation (EU) 2019/1896 frames the "European Border and Coast Guard" as a shared system: Member States remain primarily responsible for managing their own sections of the external border, while Frontex provides technical and operational support, coordination, risk analysis, training standards and, through the Standing Corps, additional deployable personnel.

The Agency's own framing is that it works "together with EU Member States" to keep external borders "safe and well-functioning," rather than acting independently of them.

## The Standing Corps

The Standing Corps is the single most significant innovation of the 2019 Regulation. Before 2019, Frontex depended entirely on ad hoc pooling of Member State officers for joint operations. The Standing Corps instead builds a body of officers who are either directly employed by the Agency or made available on a structured, predictable basis by Member States.

- **Category 1** officers are Frontex's own statutory staff, regularly deployed to border-guarding missions and other operational activities.
- **Category 2** officers are seconded from Member States for long-term postings.
- **Category 3** officers are deployed from Member States for short-term missions.
- **Category 4**, provided for in the original 2019 design, was a Member State rapid-reaction reserve intended to cover unexpected surges before the Corps reached critical mass; its 2023 target (1,500 nominated staff) was met and it is being wound down as the other categories scale up.

Training for Standing Corps officers combines classroom instruction with practical exercises over up to 12 months, covering EU border management standards, fundamental rights, operational safety, communications, first aid and equipment use. Officers deploy at land, sea and air borders across the EU and Schengen area, and can also be deployed outside the EU where a status agreement or working arrangement exists with the host third country.

Standing Corps officers may exercise executive powers, including carrying a firearm, but only under the authority and instructions of the host Member State's border guards -- oversight and ultimate command remain national.

## Fundamental Rights Safeguards

Regulation (EU) 2019/1896 built a fundamental rights architecture directly into the Agency:

- The **Fundamental Rights Officer (FRO)** has an independent role monitoring the Agency's compliance with EU and international fundamental rights law and advising the Executive Director.
- **Fundamental Rights Monitors**, part of the Fundamental Rights Office, assess whether operational activities -- including return operations -- comply with the fundamental rights framework, and contribute to training.
- The **Consultative Forum on Fundamental Rights**, running since 2012, brings together EU institutions (including the EU Agency for Fundamental Rights), international organisations and civil society to advise the Agency and its Management Board, and publishes an annual report with observations and recommendations.

These structures exist precisely because Frontex has been a recurring subject of scrutiny over alleged pushbacks and fundamental rights compliance at the external border, particularly on the Aegean and Western Balkan routes. Any Brubru answer touching these controversies should present them as contested and evolving rather than resolved, and should distinguish documented findings (for example from OLAF, the European Ombudsman, or the European Court of Auditors) from unproven allegations.

## Beyond Border Control: ETIAS and EUROSUR

Frontex's mandate extends beyond physical border operations into two digital systems:

- It hosts and runs the **ETIAS Central Unit**, the 24/7 operational core of the European Travel Information and Authorisation System established by Regulation (EU) 2018/1240. The Central Unit verifies applications flagged by hits against EU databases, sets risk indicators and screening rules, and forwards contested cases to national ETIAS Units for a final decision.
- It operates the **EUROSUR** framework, the EU's border surveillance and situational-awareness system, which pools information between Member States and the Agency to support faster detection and response at the external border.

## Integrated Border Management (IBM)

Frontex is the operational arm of the EU's Integrated Border Management strategy, which links border checks, surveillance, risk analysis, cooperation with third countries, return policy, and use of modern technology into a single multi-annual strategic policy cycle. The Agency contributes technical strategy documents, training curricula and joint operational planning that feed into this cycle alongside Member State and Commission input.
