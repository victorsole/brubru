# The Rapporteur and Shadow Rapporteur System: How the European Parliament Assigns and Negotiates Files

## QUICK FACTS
- **What this is**: the internal European Parliament (EP) mechanism for allocating ownership of a legislative or non-legislative file to individual MEPs, and for building the Parliament's negotiating position across political groups. The **rapporteur** steers the file; **shadow rapporteurs** represent every other group; **coordinators** run the allocation.
- **Legal basis**: European Parliament Rules of Procedure (RoP), 10th parliamentary term consolidated text (in force from 16 July 2024). Key rules: **Rule 51** (legislative reports), **Rule 54** (co-rapporteurship), **Rule 55** (own-initiative reports), **Rule 56** (drafting of reports), **Rule 57** (opinions of committees, replacing the pre-2024 "associated committee procedure"), **Rule 58** (budgetary assessment, BUDG committee), **Rule 59** (joint committee procedure), **Rule 220** (committee coordinators) and **Rule 221** (shadow rapporteurs). Full consolidated text: https://www.europarl.europa.eu/doceo/document/RULES-10-2024-07-16-TOC_EN.html (PDF: https://www.europarl.europa.eu/rules/rules20240716/Rules20240716_EN.pdf).
- **The points/auction system that decides WHO gets the rapporteurship is informal, not written into the Rules of Procedure.** It is a practice developed by committee coordinators, applied with variations committee by committee (see "How rapporteurships are allocated" below).
- **Who runs it**: each political group appoints one **coordinator** per committee (Rule 220); coordinators from all groups meet to bid for and assign rapporteurships, shadow rapporteurships, and to prepare committee decisions.
- **Where to verify who holds a file today**: the **Legislative Observatory (OEIL)**, `oeil.europarl.europa.eu`, procedure file page, "Key players" section. This is the single authoritative source, see `oeil_legislative_observatory`. Press coverage and secondary sources lag or get it wrong.
- **Known data gap**: OEIL reliably lists the **rapporteur** and **rapporteur(s) for opinion**, but **shadow rapporteurs are inconsistently populated**, some procedure files show one shadow, some show several, some show none even when shadows exist in practice. Always cross-check against the committee's own webpage or press release if a shadow name matters for outreach.
- **Why it matters for advocacy**: the rapporteur drafts the report and the compromise amendments; shadows negotiate on behalf of every other group; both sit on Parliament's trilogue negotiating team. Identifying them within days of a file's committee referral is the single highest-leverage step in any EU advocacy campaign, because early contact happens before positions harden.

## What a rapporteur does
The rapporteur is the Member of the European Parliament formally appointed by the **lead (responsible) committee** to manage a specific file on Parliament's behalf, from referral through to the final vote and, for co-decision files, through interinstitutional negotiations. Concretely, the rapporteur:
- drafts the **committee report**, including a draft legislative resolution and, where the committee wishes to amend the Commission's text, proposed amendments (Rule 51, legislative reports; Rule 55, own-initiative reports; Rule 56 sets out how reports are drafted);
- steers debate inside the committee, tries to build a majority for a coherent Parliament position, and negotiates **compromise amendments** that merge overlapping amendments tabled by MEPs and shadows into a single text the committee can adopt;
- presents the report to the committee for a vote, then defends it in plenary;
- once the committee mandates Parliament to enter interinstitutional negotiations, **leads Parliament's negotiating team in trilogues** with the Council presidency and the Commission, alongside the shadow rapporteurs and under the chairmanship of the committee chair (see `trilogue_interinstitutional_negotiations`);
- reports back to committee (or, when a committee meeting cannot be convened in time, to the committee coordinators) after each trilogue round, and signs off the provisional political agreement on Parliament's behalf once negotiations conclude.

A rapporteur may be appointed early, "on the basis of the Commission's annual legislative programme," so as to follow a proposal during its preparatory phase before it is formally referred. On some major files two political groups negotiate a **co-rapporteurship** (Rule 54, new in the 2024 reform), sharing the drafting role rather than one group holding it alone; this is more common on cross-cutting or highly technical dossiers where committee coordinators cannot agree on a single winning bid.

## How rapporteurships are allocated: the points/auction system
The Rules of Procedure do not specify how a committee chooses which political group gets to hold a rapporteurship. In practice, EP committees run an informal, self-regulating **points-based auction**, developed and refined by group coordinators over successive terms:
1. Each political group represented on a committee is allotted a number of **points**, roughly proportional to its number of seats on that committee (a common formula, used e.g. in ECON, is points equal to three times the group's numerical strength on the committee).
2. Each incoming file (a report, an own-initiative text, an opinion) is given a **"price" in points** by the committee secretariat or the coordinators. A legislative or own-initiative **report** typically costs around 2-3 points; an **opinion** typically costs 1 point; procedures handled in the name of the committee chair or a vice-chair (usually low-interest files) can cost 0 points.
3. At a coordinators' meeting, group coordinators **bid** their group's points for files they want. Where two groups compete for the same file, tie-break conventions generally favour the group that has spent fewer points so far that term, to keep the overall distribution proportional. Groups also trade informally, letting one take a given file today in exchange for a future file.
4. The winning coordinator then assigns the rapporteurship to a specific MEP inside their group's delegation on that committee, weighing national-delegation balance and individual members' policy interests and workload.
5. On especially salient or contested files, the same mechanism can produce a **co-rapporteurship** between two groups (Rule 54) rather than a single winner.

This system is not written into the Rules of Procedure; it is committee practice, confirmed by successive editions of the EP's own procedural literature (Corbett, Jacobs and Shackleton, *The European Parliament*) and by European Parliamentary Research Service (EPRS) analysis of the 2024 reform.

## Shadow rapporteurs
Once a group has won a file, **every other political group represented on the committee may designate a shadow rapporteur** for that report (Rule 221). Shadow rapporteurs:
- follow the progress of the file inside the committee and keep their own group informed;
- **negotiate compromise amendments** with the rapporteur and the other shadows, forming what is often described as an informal "sub-committee" that does the real drafting work behind a report before it reaches a full committee vote;
- **table their group's own amendments** where compromise cannot be reached, and mobilise their group's votes in committee and plenary;
- sit on **Parliament's negotiating team in trilogues** alongside the rapporteur, under the chairmanship of the committee chair, and can strike deals directly with the Council presidency if the rapporteur is unavailable or the file requires it;
- act as a check on the rapporteur: because rapporteurs hold significant delegated authority over the text and the negotiation, shadows are the mechanism by which the other groups retain oversight and influence.

Shadow rapporteur appointment is largely **self-selection**: MEPs on a committee volunteer for a shadow role based on their own policy interest and portfolio, and the group coordinator arbitrates only when two members of the same group want the same file. Non-attached Members do not constitute a political group under Rule 33 and therefore cannot designate coordinators or, in the ordinary sense, shadow rapporteurs, though they retain a right to information.

**Data caveat for Brubru users**: unlike the rapporteur, shadow rapporteur identity is **not consistently published on OEIL**. Some procedure files show a full slate of shadows (one per group), others show only one or two, and some show none at all even on active files with known shadow negotiators. When a shadow name is mission-critical for an advocacy plan, verify with the committee secretariat, the group's own press office, or the MEP's own website, in addition to OEIL.

## Committee coordinators
Each political group may designate one of its members in each committee as **coordinator** (Rule 220). Coordinators:
- meet, convened by the committee chair when necessary, to prepare procedural decisions for the committee, in particular **rapporteur appointments**;
- may be delegated power by the committee to take certain decisions on its behalf, **except** decisions on the actual adoption of reports, resolutions, opinions or amendments (those require a full committee vote);
- decide the committee's points-based allocation of rapporteurships and opinions (see above);
- set the committee's agenda alongside the chair.
Where coordinators cannot reach consensus, they act only by a majority that clearly reflects the political weight of the groups on the committee.

## Opinion-giving committees and shared/associated competence (Rules 57-59)
Not every file is handled by a single committee. When a proposal touches the competence of more than one policy area, Parliament's President refers it to a **lead (responsible) committee** and may simultaneously refer it to one or more **opinion-giving committees**:
- **Rule 57 (Opinions of committees)**: an opinion-giving committee appoints its own **rapporteur for opinion**, who drafts an opinion adopted by that committee and forwarded to the lead committee. Rule 57(2) binds the chairs and rapporteurs of the lead and opinion-giving committees to a **principle of good and sincere cooperation**: they must keep each other informed and try to agree on shared texts and positions, including inviting each other into meetings between rapporteurs and shadows. This rewritten Rule 57, in force since the 16 July 2024 Rules of Procedure reform, **absorbed and replaced** the older, separately named "associated committee procedure" (formerly its own Rule, under which an associated committee could have amendments accepted without a vote on matters within its exclusive competence, or table amendments directly in plenary if the lead committee ignored them). OEIL procedure files opened before the reform can still display the legacy label **"(Associated committee)"** against a committee name; functionally this now operates through Rule 57's cooperation duty rather than a standalone procedure.
- **Rule 58 (Budgetary assessment)**: where a proposal has budget implications, the President refers it in parallel to the **Budgets (BUDG) committee**, which can issue a budgetary assessment; BUDG's rapporteur also joins Parliament's trilogue negotiating team on such files.
- **Rule 59 (Joint committee procedure)**: used when a proposal falls so evenly across **two or three committees'** competences (never more than three) that responsibility cannot be split into a lead-plus-opinion structure. The committees involved are treated as a single committee for votes, majorities and amendment tabling, and typically appoint one rapporteur per committee involved (co-rapporteurs), or the Conference of Presidents decides on a shared drafting arrangement.

For advocacy purposes, the practical distinction that matters is: the **lead committee's rapporteur and shadows** control the substance and the trilogue mandate; **opinion-giving committee** rapporteurs influence a narrower slice of the text and typically have less leverage over the final compromise; a **joint committee** procedure means two (or three) rapporteurs each carry real weight and both/all delegations must be engaged.

## Why identifying the rapporteur and shadows early matters
- **Positions harden fast.** The rapporteur's first draft report, and the shadows' first tranche of amendments, set the terrain for the rest of the procedure. Advocacy input delivered before the draft report is tabled has materially more influence than input delivered after committee amendments have already been submitted.
- **The rapporteur is the single point of accountability** for the Parliament's negotiating mandate in trilogues; the shadows are the check on that mandate and the fastest route to a group's internal position if you do not have access to the rapporteur.
- **The coordinator is the right contact when the rapporteurship itself is still being decided** (before OEIL shows a named rapporteur), since coordinators run the points-based allocation.
- **Committee choice drives everything downstream.** Knowing whether a file sits with a single lead committee, has opinion-giving committees under Rule 57, or is jointly handled under Rule 59, tells you how many MEP offices to engage and which one holds the pen.
- **OEIL first, always.** Because OEIL is the EP's own system of record, it should be the first and final check for who currently holds a file, even though shadow rapporteur fields are the weak point in that data (see Data caveat above).
