# Insurance Protection Gap (Natural Catastrophes): EIOPA Dashboard and Policy Response

## QUICK FACTS
- Definition: the insurance protection gap is the share of economic losses from natural catastrophes (floods, storms, wildfires, droughts, earthquakes) that is NOT covered by insurance
- Headline figure: only around a quarter (~25%) of natural-catastrophe losses in the EU were insured between 1980 and 2024, and EIOPA notes the insured share has been declining even as losses rise
- Consumer uptake: only around 17% of European households hold property insurance covering natural-catastrophe damage (EIOPA, April 2026)
- Scale: the cost of natural catastrophes in EU Member States totalled around EUR 900 billion between 1981 and 2023, with roughly a fifth of that total incurred in just the last three years of the period
- Body: EIOPA -- European Insurance and Occupational Pensions Authority, the EU supervisory authority for insurance and occupational pensions (Frankfurt-based, part of the European System of Financial Supervision alongside EBA and ESMA)
- Key tool: EIOPA Dashboard on the Insurance Protection Gap for Natural Catastrophes -- covers 30 European countries, combines economic losses, insured losses, risk estimations and insurance coverage, with a historical view (1980-2024) and a current modelled view; most recently updated 5 December 2025
- Perils tracked: flood and earthquake are the explicitly modelled perils in the dashboard's risk estimation; the wider protection-gap narrative also covers storm, wildfire and drought losses
- Drivers of the gap: affordability of premiums, availability of cover in high-risk areas (insurer withdrawal/non-renewal), low consumer awareness and risk perception, and rising climate-driven loss frequency and severity
- EU-level policy proposal (EIOPA-ECB, joint work, referenced in EIOPA's April 2026 remarks "Insurance protection gaps in a changing climate"): a two-pillar approach combining (1) a public-private reinsurance scheme pooling private risk capacity with broader distribution across Member States, and (2) an EU disaster fund to strengthen public disaster-risk management and EU-wide coordination -- proposal status: under discussion, not yet legislated; verify current status before citing as adopted policy
- National schemes already in place: France's regime Cat Nat (mandatory extension of property/motor policies, state reinsurance backstop via Caisse Centrale de Reassurance); Spain's Consorcio de Compensacion de Seguros (public reinsurer of last resort, extraordinary-risks cover bundled into standard policies); Italy's new mandatory natural-catastrophe insurance for businesses (2024 Budget Law, implementing decree in force from 31 March 2025 for large/medium enterprises with staggered deadlines to 1 January 2026 for small and micro enterprises; covers flood, overflow, inundation, earthquake and landslide; SACE reinsures up to 50% of indemnities paid by insurers)
- Regulatory interaction: Solvency II (natural-catastrophe risk module of the Standard Formula, reinsurance treatment, see `solvency_ii_directive`); ECB and ESRB systemic-risk monitoring of climate-related financial risk (see `esrb_macroprudential_framework`); the EU Taxonomy's climate-adaptation objective (see `eu_taxonomy_sustainable_finance`); the European Climate Law's adaptation strand (see `european_climate_law`)
- Source: EIOPA dashboard and publications -- verify the latest insured-share figure and any EU reinsurance-scheme proposal status live before citing as current fact

The insurance protection gap for natural catastrophes has moved from a technical actuarial concept to a live EU financial-stability and climate-resilience question. EIOPA and the ECB now treat it as a systemic issue: uninsured losses fall on households, businesses and ultimately public budgets, which increasingly act as insurer of last resort after major floods, storms and wildfires.

## Why the Gap Exists

Four factors are consistently cited by EIOPA and the European Central Bank:

1. **Affordability**: premiums for natural-catastrophe cover in high-risk areas can be prohibitively expensive relative to household or SME income, especially as insurers reprice risk upward in response to more frequent and severe events.
2. **Availability**: in the highest-risk locations (flood plains, wildfire-prone regions, coastal areas exposed to storm surge), insurers may restrict, non-renew or withdraw cover altogether, leaving properties effectively uninsurable through the private market.
3. **Awareness**: many households and small businesses underestimate their exposure to climate perils or assume state disaster relief will cover losses, reducing voluntary take-up where cover is available and affordable.
4. **Rising climate risk**: the frequency and severity of floods, wildfires, droughts and storms is increasing across Europe, widening the gap between the pace of loss growth and the pace of insurance-market adaptation.

## The EIOPA Dashboard

EIOPA's Dashboard on the Insurance Protection Gap for Natural Catastrophes is the EU's principal public data tool on this issue. It brings together, for 30 European countries:

- **Economic losses**: modelled and observed total losses from natural catastrophes
- **Insured losses**: the portion of those losses actually covered by insurance payouts
- **Risk estimations**: forward-looking modelled exposure, with flood and earthquake as the explicitly covered perils
- **Insurance coverage indicators**: household and business take-up rates by country

The dashboard offers both a historical view (losses and insurance coverage from 1980 to 2024) and a current, model-based view of exposure. It is updated periodically; the most recent refresh was 5 December 2025. Coverage and insured-share figures vary significantly by country and by peril, so any country-specific claim should be checked against the live dashboard rather than the EU-wide average.

## National Schemes: Three Models

EU Member States have developed distinct approaches to closing the gap domestically, each combining a degree of mandation or bundling with a public reinsurance backstop:

| Country | Scheme | Mechanism |
|---------|--------|-----------|
| France | Regime Cat Nat | Natural-catastrophe cover is bundled as a mandatory extension into standard property and motor policies; the state-owned Caisse Centrale de Reassurance provides an unlimited state guarantee as reinsurer of last resort; a ministerial decree declares "state of natural catastrophe" per event to trigger payouts |
| Spain | Consorcio de Compensacion de Seguros | A public reinsurer covering "extraordinary risks" (including natural catastrophes and terrorism), funded by a surcharge automatically added to most property and motor policies; steps in when private insurers cannot or will not cover a risk |
| Italy | Mandatory business nat-cat insurance (2024 Budget Law) | Businesses registered in Italy (excluding agricultural companies) must purchase property cover against flood, overflow, inundation, earthquake and landslide; implementing decree in force from 31 March 2025 for large enterprises, with deadlines staggered to 1 October 2025 (medium enterprises) and 1 January 2026 (small and micro enterprises); non-compliant businesses lose eligibility for public disaster relief and subsidies rather than facing direct fines; the state export-credit agency SACE reinsures up to 50% of insurer payouts |

These three models illustrate the range of EU approaches: full bundling into standard policies (France, Spain) versus a targeted mandate on one segment (Italy, businesses only). Neither model has yet been replicated at EU level.

## The EU-Level Debate

EIOPA and the ECB have jointly explored whether a purely national patchwork of schemes is sufficient given the cross-border and systemic nature of climate risk. In public remarks during 2026 ("Insurance protection gaps in a changing climate", April 2026), EIOPA set out a two-pillar direction of travel:

1. **A public-private reinsurance scheme**: pooling private insurance-market risk capacity with a broader, EU-wide distribution mechanism, so that risk concentrated in the highest-exposure Member States can be shared more widely across the Union.
2. **An EU disaster fund**: dedicated to strengthening public disaster-risk management and ensuring coordination across Member States, complementing (not replacing) the EU Solidarity Fund's existing post-disaster relief role.

EIOPA has stressed that private insurance solutions alone cannot absorb the impact of rare, extremely severe events, and that closing the gap requires combining insurer underwriting reform, risk-based pricing linked to prevention measures, and coordinated EU-level frameworks. As of mid-2026 this remains a policy direction under discussion between EIOPA, the ECB and the Commission, not an adopted legislative proposal -- verify the latest status (any Commission communication, legislative proposal number, or Council conclusions) before asserting that an EU reinsurance scheme or disaster fund has been formally proposed or adopted.

## Interaction with Existing EU Frameworks

- **Solvency II**: insurers' natural-catastrophe exposure is captured in the non-life underwriting risk module of the Solvency Capital Requirement standard formula, and reinsurance arrangements (including public backstops such as France's CCR or Italy's SACE) affect insurers' capital treatment of catastrophe risk. See `solvency_ii_directive`.
- **Systemic risk monitoring**: the ESRB and the ECB monitor climate-related financial risk, including the protection gap, as part of their macroprudential remit; climate risk is flagged as a cross-cutting systemic concern alongside cyber risk and geoeconomic fragmentation. See `esrb_macroprudential_framework`.
- **EU Taxonomy for sustainable finance**: climate change adaptation is one of the Taxonomy's six environmental objectives, and insurance products (including parametric and index-based nat-cat cover) can qualify as Taxonomy-aligned adaptation activities. See `eu_taxonomy_sustainable_finance`.
- **European Climate Law**: the Union's climate-adaptation objective under the Climate Law provides the policy umbrella under which the protection-gap debate sits, alongside the EU Adaptation Strategy. See `european_climate_law`.
- **Wildfire and disaster-risk policy**: wildfire losses are one of the perils driving the widening gap, addressed operationally through the EU's wildfire risk management and civil protection work. See `wildfire_risk_management`.
