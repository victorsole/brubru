# EU Transparency Register (Joint Lobbying Register)

## QUICK FACTS

- **What it is:** the joint mandatory register of interest representatives (lobbyists) operated together by the European Parliament, the Council of the European Union and the European Commission. It lists organisations, associations, groups and self-employed individuals who carry out activities aimed at influencing EU policy formulation, legislation or decision-making.
- **Legal basis:** Interinstitutional Agreement of 20 May 2021 between the European Parliament, the Council of the European Union and the European Commission on a mandatory transparency register (the "2021 IIA"). Entered into force 1 July 2021. Published OJ L 207, 11.6.2021, p. 1; EUR-Lex CELEX 32021Q0611(01): https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32021Q0611(01)
- **History:** the European Parliament set up its own register in 1995, the Commission followed in 2008, and the two merged into a joint voluntary register in 2011. The 2021 IIA was the first time the Council became a full signatory (it had only observer status before), and it introduced the "conditionality" principle that makes registration a de facto precondition for defined interactions rather than a purely voluntary listing.
- **Who runs it operationally:** the Joint Transparency Register Secretariat (JTRS), a joint operational unit staffed by officials from the three signatory institutions, responsible for processing applications, running eligibility and data-quality checks, handling complaints and investigations, and enforcing the Code of Conduct. Oversight sits with a Management Board composed of the Secretaries-General of the three institutions.
- **Registration is de facto mandatory, not a general legal obligation.** The 2021 IIA is an interinstitutional instrument, not a regulation with direct legal effect on private parties, so it cannot itself impose sanctions on lobbyists. Instead it works through "conditionality measures": each institution makes registration a precondition for specific privileges (EP access badges, certain senior-official meetings, speaking at committee hearings, expert-group membership). The Commission proposed a genuinely mandatory Transparency Register regulation in 2021 (COM(2021)/none finalised); this remains a live policy debate, not adopted law.
- **Database URL:** https://transparency-register.europa.eu/index_en
- **Guidelines / how to register:** https://transparency-register.europa.eu/guidance/guidelines_en
- **Scale (most recent published figures):** the register passed 12,000 registrants in 2021; roughly 12,500 by February 2024; over 16,000 organisations by late 2025. Companies and NGOs are consistently the two largest groups. Check the live statistics page for the current count: https://transparency-register.europa.eu/about-register/data-portal/register-statistics_en
- **Not the same as the Documents Register.** The Transparency Register lists *who is lobbying whom*. The separate Public Register of Documents (Regulation (EC) 1049/2001) lists *what documents an institution holds* and lets citizens request access to them. Do not conflate the two when a user asks "how do I find an EU document" versus "who is lobbying on file X" -- see `public_access_to_documents_1049_2001`.

## 1. What the register covers

The Transparency Register lists "interest representatives": companies, trade and business associations, trade unions, NGOs, think tanks, professional consultancies, law firms engaged in advocacy, self-employed consultants, religious organisations, and subnational public authorities (regions, cities) when they lobby in their own commercial or associative interest rather than as public bodies exercising EU-recognised competences.

Covered activities include:

- direct or indirect contact with EU decision-makers to influence policy, legislation or implementation
- organising or participating in meetings, conferences and policy events aimed at influencing decision-making
- participating in public consultations and hearings
- organising communication campaigns, platforms, networks or grassroots initiatives directed at EU institutions
- preparing, circulating and communicating position papers, letters and information material to MEPs, Commission officials or Council staff

**Excluded from the scope:** lawyers acting in the exercise of the fundamental right to a fair trial for a client; social partners acting as formal actors in the EU social dialogue; activities carried out purely in response to a direct, individual request from an institution (e.g. answering a Commission consultation question when asked, rather than proactively campaigning); and public authorities acting in their institutional/constitutional capacity (a national ministry replying in its official role is not "lobbying").

## 2. The six sections of registrants

The register's Annex I structure groups registrants into named sections used for both classification and the searchable filter on the database:

- **Section I** -- Professional consultancies, law firms and self-employed consultants offering lobbying services to clients
- **Section II** -- In-house lobbyists and trade, business and professional associations (companies representing their own interests; trade associations, trade unions and professional bodies representing members' interests)
- **Section III** -- Non-governmental organisations (NGOs, platforms, networks and similar)
- **Section IV** -- Think tanks, research and academic institutions
- **Section V** -- Organisations representing churches and religious communities
- **Section VI** -- Organisations representing local, regional and municipal authorities, other public or mixed entities, and networks of subnational public bodies

For financial-disclosure purposes the 2021 IIA guidelines also group registrants into three simplified categories that determine which disclosure fields apply: (1) organisations advancing their own interests or those of their members, (2) organisations advancing the interests of paying clients (mainly consultancies and law firms), and (3) organisations that do not represent commercial interests (mainly NGOs, think tanks, and religious/subnational bodies). Roughly two-thirds of registrants fall into category 1 (own-interest, dominated by companies and associations), around a quarter into category 3, and a small remainder into category 2 (paid client representation).

## 3. What registrants must disclose

Every registrant creates and maintains a public profile that must be kept up to date and is updated annually at minimum. Required disclosures include:

- **Identity and structure:** legal name, head office location, legal form, and (since a recent register feature) links between a registrant and any intermediaries or clients it represents.
- **EU files and policy interests:** the specific legislative proposals, policy areas and initiatives the registrant is working on, tagged to help users filter the database by topic.
- **Meetings and consultation contributions:** the register links out to published records of meetings the registrant has had with Commissioners, cabinet members and Directors-General (published by the relevant Commission service within 14 days), and to any roadmap or public-consultation contributions made since July 2018.
- **Financial data, banded not exact:** annual costs relating to covered lobbying activities (staff costs, office and administrative expenses, representation costs, outsourced activity costs, membership fees) reported in ranges rather than precise figures. Section I registrants (consultancies, law firms) must additionally disclose their clients by name with revenue bands attributable to each client and the specific files worked on for that client. Section III/IV/V/VI (non-commercial) registrants disclose their main sources of funding by category (EU funding, national public funding, grants, donations, membership contributions) and must itemise any single contribution exceeding both 10% of total budget and EUR 10,000, naming the contributor.
- **Code of Conduct acceptance:** every registrant signs the Code of Conduct annexed to the 2021 IIA, governing standards of behaviour, prohibiting misrepresentation and requiring accuracy of the declared information. Breach can trigger suspension or removal (up to two years) from the register.

## 4. The 2021 conditionality principle

The defining feature of the 2021 IIA is "conditionality": each signatory institution undertakes to make registration a precondition for specific defined interactions, rather than leaving registration purely optional. In practice the three institutions apply conditionality unevenly:

- **European Parliament:** registration and an active status are required to obtain a long-term EP building access badge, to speak at most committee hearings as an invited stakeholder, and (as of a Bureau decision applying from 1 September 2025) to hold a scheduled meeting on Parliament business with EP staff in a management role -- specifically the Secretary-General, Deputy Secretaries-General, Directors-General, Directors, Heads of Unit and team leaders within the Cabinets of the President and the Secretary-General. Those managers must declare such meetings, published on the EP staff-meetings portal within 30 calendar days: https://www.europarl.europa.eu/staff-meetings/en/home. This sits alongside the separate, longer-standing Rule 11(2) obligation on MEPs acting as rapporteur, shadow rapporteur or committee chair to publish their own meetings -- see `ep_lobby_transparency_rules` for the full MEP-level regime (Rule 11(2)/11(3), legislative footprint, intergroup disclosures).
- **European Commission:** registration is required for meetings with Commissioners, members of their private offices (cabinets) and Directors-General, a rule dating from 2014 guidelines and formalised in the 2021 IIA. Meetings are published on the DG/cabinet's own pages and aggregated in the Commission's lobby-meeting register -- see `eu_commission_transparency_and_college_agenda` for how to navigate that register and the related College-agenda and corporate-body-code surfaces.
- **Council of the European Union:** the weakest implementation of the three. Only the highest-level decision-makers at the Council General Secretariat are subject to the conditionality precondition; working-party chairs and COREPER members are not currently required to publish individual meetings with interest representatives. The Council adopted its own decision regulating contacts between its General Secretariat and interest representatives as part of the 2021 package, but civil-society groups (notably Transparency International) continue to press for the Council to match Parliament and Commission practice.

A European Court of Auditors special report (Special Report 05/2024) found the register broadly consistent with international lobbying-transparency principles but flagged weak enforcement: because the IIA is not a legislative act, the JTRS cannot impose direct sanctions on non-compliant lobbyists beyond suspension or removal, and the Secretariat's own working arrangements between the three institutions are not fully formalised. The 2021 IIA was due for institutional review by July 2025.

## 5. The Joint Transparency Register Secretariat (JTRS)

The JTRS is the joint administrative body, staffed by officials seconded from Parliament, Council and Commission, that keeps the register running day to day:

- processes new registration applications and runs an eligibility and data-quality check before any new entry is published
- carries out periodic and complaint-triggered investigations into alleged breaches of the Code of Conduct
- can suspend or remove non-compliant registrants (removal can last up to two years, and a re-activation feature allows a removed registrant to later re-apply under the same registration (REG) number)
- produces aggregate statistics on registrant numbers and categories, published on the register's statistics page
- reports to a Management Board of the three institutions' Secretaries-General, which has the final say on interinstitutional policy questions the Secretariat cannot resolve alone

The JTRS's working arrangements are not governed by a single formal rulebook shared across all three institutions, which the 2024 Court of Auditors audit identified as an operational efficiency risk (unclear workload distribution and case handover between institutions).

## 6. The searchable database

The public-facing database at https://transparency-register.europa.eu/index_en lets any user:

- search by organisation name, registration (REG) number, country, or Section (I-VI)
- filter by policy area / EU file to see who has declared an interest in a specific proposal
- view a registrant's declared clients (Section I), financial data bands, and linked meetings with Commissioners/cabinets/DGs and with MEPs
- see a registrant's declared contributions to public consultations and roadmaps since July 2018
- check the list of currently suspended registrants
- view the register's live statistics (total organisations, breakdown by section and by country)

Every registration number follows the format `NNNNNNNNNN-NN` (10 digits, hyphen, 2 digits) -- useful when cross-referencing a lobbying organisation across Brubru's Stakeholder Mapping and Position Analysis surfaces.

## 7. Why it matters to a policy professional

- **Due diligence on counterparties.** Before a coalition meeting or joint position paper, check a partner organisation's Transparency Register profile: declared clients, funding sources and other files they work show whether their stated interest aligns with yours or conceals a conflict.
- **Competitive intelligence.** A competitor's or opposing coalition's declared EU files, meeting history with Commissioners/DGs, and consultation contributions are all publicly visible -- a legitimate way to map who else is active on a file before you engage.
- **Access planning.** If your organisation wants EP building access, a scheduled meeting with a Director-General, or to be invited to speak at a committee hearing, registering (and keeping the profile active and accurate) is an operational precondition, not paperwork you can skip until later.
- **Legislative footprint tracing.** Combined with a rapporteur's Rule 11(3) declaration (see `ep_lobby_transparency_rules`) and the Commission's published DG/cabinet meetings (see `eu_commission_transparency_and_college_agenda`), the register lets you reconstruct which organisations had documented input into a specific file -- useful for both offensive advocacy planning and defensive monitoring of what competitors have already told decision-makers.
