# EU Chips Act

## Overview

The European Chips Act (Regulation (EU) 2023/1781) is the EU's flagship industrial policy response to the 2021-2022 global semiconductor shortage that disrupted automotive, medical device, and consumer electronics supply chains across Europe. The Act establishes a tripartite framework: cutting-edge research and innovation via the Chips for Europe Initiative (managed through the Chips Joint Undertaking); incentives to attract and scale up manufacturing capacity; and a coordinated monitoring and crisis response mechanism.

The regulation sits at the heart of the EU's open strategic autonomy agenda. It is explicitly linked to the Competitiveness Compass (January 2026), which identifies semiconductor self-sufficiency as a prerequisite for EU technological sovereignty. The Draghi Report (September 2024) further elevated chips as a strategic priority, pointing to the EU's dependency on Taiwan (TSMC) and South Korea (Samsung) for advanced logic chips as a systemic risk.

The Chips Act uses state aid flexibility (recital 43; Chips Act Communication) to allow Member States to grant aid for "first-of-a-kind" facilities outside existing aid maps -- a significant departure from EU state aid orthodoxy, designed to attract large-scale investments like the Intel Ohio and Magdeburg fabs (though the latter was put on hold in September 2024 following Intel's global restructuring).

## Three Pillars

### Pillar 1: Chips for Europe Initiative (Research and Innovation)

The Chips for Europe Initiative channels EU budget resources into pre-competitive research, design, and pilot line access. It is governed through the Chips Joint Undertaking (Chips JU), which succeeded the Key Digital Technologies Joint Undertaking (KDT JU).

Key actions under Pillar 1:
- Virtual design platform: EU-wide network of design tools, IP libraries, and electronic design automation (EDA) access for fabless start-ups and SMEs
- Pilot lines: shared open-access fabrication lines for prototyping at sub-2nm node and advanced packaging -- removing the cost barrier for SMEs to test designs at leading-edge process nodes
- Advanced technology and engineering capacities: quantum chips, neuromorphic computing, wide-bandgap semiconductors (SiC, GaN), photonic chips
- Skills: European Semiconductor Education and Training (RESET) network to address talent shortage
- Technology and Innovation Roadmap: Commission-led forward-looking assessment updated every two years

The Chips JU operates under the EU Joint Undertakings framework (Council Regulation (EU) 2021/2085). It is co-funded by Member States and industry, with the EU contributing via Horizon Europe and the Digital Europe Programme.

### Pillar 2: EU Chips Supply Security (Manufacturing Capacity)

Pillar 2 creates the legal and financial framework for "Integrated Production Facilities" (IPFs) and "Open EU Foundries" (OEFs) -- two new categories that unlock bespoke state aid treatment.

| Category | Description | State Aid Treatment |
|----------|-------------|---------------------|
| Integrated Production Facility (IPF) | Vertically integrated fab producing chips for own use and/or third parties; "first-of-a-kind" in the EU | Can benefit from derogation from regional aid maps; notified to Commission |
| Open EU Foundry (OEF) | Fab offering manufacturing services to unaffiliated chip designers (fabless model) | Same derogation; must guarantee open-access to EU customers on non-discriminatory terms |

Both categories require Commission approval and must be "first-of-a-kind" in the EU -- meaning they introduce technology or production capacity not yet present at scale in the EU. This ensures the state aid derogation targets genuine additionality rather than subsidising incumbent activities.

The Chips Act Communication (adopted alongside the Regulation) set out the state aid framework allowing Member States to support IPFs and OEFs. The Commission has approved multiple schemes under this framework, including Germany's IPCEI ME/CT (Microelectronics and Communication Technologies) successor schemes.

### Pillar 3: Monitoring, Crisis Response, and Coordination

Pillar 3 establishes a real-time monitoring and early warning system for semiconductor supply chains, and a crisis response mechanism.

Key elements:
- Member States designate national competent authorities (NCAs) for chips monitoring
- European Semiconductor Board (ESB): coordination body of NCAs, chaired by Commission; advises on priority-rated orders and crisis measures
- Monitoring scope: wafer fabrication capacity, substrates, chemicals, equipment; key chokepoints in the supply chain
- Crisis stages: "alert" (early warning) and "crisis" (Article 20 -- Commission activates crisis measures)
- Crisis measures (Article 21): Commission can mandate that IPFs and OEFs prioritise orders from "crisis actors" (entities producing critical goods); time-limited, proportionate, with compensation
- Priority-rated orders: Commission coordination of voluntary or mandatory reallocation of production capacity across Member States
- Mapping of supply chain vulnerabilities: updated every two years and linked to the strategic dependencies analysis under the Single Market Emergency Instrument (SMEI/IMERA)

## Budget

| Source | Amount | Notes |
|--------|--------|-------|
| EU public (Horizon Europe, Digital Europe, EDF) | EUR 3.3 billion | Redirected from existing programmes; no new budget line at adoption |
| Member State public contributions | EUR ~8.4 billion estimated | National schemes, IPCEI, regional funds; varies by country |
| Private sector investment (leveraged) | EUR ~31 billion estimated | Private co-investment by chip manufacturers and ecosystem companies |
| Total target | EUR 43 billion | By 2030; public + private |

The EUR 43 billion target is a political commitment, not a binding appropriation. The Commission's 2026 Competitiveness Compass review noted that private investment mobilisation has been slower than projected, partly due to Intel's fab pause and global semiconductor demand cycles.

The STEP platform (established by Regulation (EU) 2024/795, CELEX 32024R0795) created a EUR 29 billion pan-EU investment framework directing Cohesion Policy, Innovation Fund, and Digital Europe resources to strategic technologies including semiconductors. STEP serves as a predecessor/pilot for the European Competitiveness Fund (ECF, COM(2025)555) under the MFF 2028-2034.

## European Semiconductor Alliance

The European Semiconductor Alliance (ESA) is an industry-led stakeholder body established alongside the Chips Act to coordinate industry input into the Chips for Europe Initiative, the Technology and Innovation Roadmap, and monitoring activities. It brings together chip manufacturers, equipment makers, chemical suppliers, fabless design houses, EDA vendors, and end-user industries (automotive, industrial, health).

Key participants include ASML (the Netherlands), Infineon, STMicroelectronics, NXP, Bosch, IMEC (Belgium), Fraunhofer, and major automotive OEMs. The Alliance feeds into the European Semiconductor Board's advisory functions and shapes the strategic priorities of the Chips JU work programmes.

## Key Provisions

### Articles 1-4 -- Scope, Definitions, and Objectives
The Act defines "semiconductor" broadly, covering integrated circuits, photonic chips, quantum chips, and advanced packaging. It sets the 2030 goal of a 20% global market share for EU-manufactured chips by volume, with an emphasis on resilience rather than full self-sufficiency.

### Articles 5-14 -- Chips for Europe Initiative (Pillar 1)
Establishes the Initiative's governance under the Chips JU; sets out the virtual design platform, pilot lines, advanced technology capacities, and skills actions. Article 10 mandates the Technology and Innovation Roadmap.

### Articles 15-19 -- Supply Security (Pillar 2)
Defines IPF and OEF status; application procedures; Commission approval criteria; state aid derogation activation. Article 19 requires IPFs and OEFs to provide the Commission with information on production capacity and order books on request.

### Articles 20-26 -- Monitoring and Crisis Response (Pillar 3)
Article 20: Commission may declare a "semiconductor crisis" following ESB assessment. Article 21: crisis measures including priority-rated orders (voluntary, then mandatory). Article 22: establishment of the European Semiconductor Board. Article 23: NCAs and their obligations. Article 24: monitoring of supply chain indicators. Article 25: international cooperation provisions.

### Article 27 -- International Partnerships
The Commission may conclude international agreements on semiconductor supply chain cooperation. Active bilateral dialogues underway with the US (EU-US Trade and Technology Council), Japan, South Korea, India, and Taiwan (informal).

The Intel Magdeburg pause is the most significant setback for the Act's implementation. The Commission has since signalled it will continue to pursue attraction of other large-scale investors, including TSMC's Dresden joint venture (ESMC -- European Semiconductor Manufacturing Company, with Infineon, NXP, and Bosch), which broke ground in August 2024 and targets production of 300,000 wafers/year at 28/22nm by 2029.
