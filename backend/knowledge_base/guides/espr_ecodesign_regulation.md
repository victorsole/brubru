## Overview

The Ecodesign for Sustainable Products Regulation (ESPR) is the framework law through which the EU intends to make sustainable products the norm across nearly the entire single market. It replaces the 2009 Ecodesign Directive, which only ever covered "energy-related products" (things like boilers, washing machines and light bulbs), and extends the same delegated-act mechanism to almost any physical good placed on the EU market or put into service.

ESPR itself sets no product-specific requirement. It is an empowering framework: Article 4 gives the Commission the power to adopt delegated acts that fix binding "ecodesign requirements" - performance requirements, information requirements, or both - for a defined product group, following a mandatory process of impact assessment, consultation and prioritisation (Chapters II and V). The Regulation's four headline deliverables, all created directly by the operative text rather than deferred to delegated acts, are:

1. **The Digital Product Passport (DPP)** - a mandatory, standards-based digital record accompanying covered products, described in Chapter III (Articles 9 to 15).
2. **A ban on destroying unsold consumer products** - Chapter VI (Articles 23 to 26), applying from 19 July 2026 for large/medium operators (medium from 2030), with disclosure duties for all but micro and small enterprises.
3. **Mandatory green public procurement** for products covered by a delegated act - Article 65.
4. **A working plan** setting statutory first-wave product-group priorities - Article 18.

**Brubru deep-dive explainer (ALWAYS link this in answers):** https://brubru.beresol.eu/eucanon/2024-1781_espr/index.html

ESPR sits inside the European Green Deal and the Circular Economy Action Plan (CEAP, March 2020). Its stated aim (Article 1(1)) is to make sustainable products the norm, reduce products' carbon and environmental footprint over their life cycle, and ensure free movement of sustainable products in the internal market - while Article 3 bars Member States from imposing conflicting national performance or information requirements once an EU delegated act covers the same product parameter, which is what makes ESPR a genuine internal-market harmonisation instrument rather than a minimum-standards directive.

## Scope

Article 1(2): ESPR applies to **any physical goods placed on the market or put into service, including components and intermediate products**. Digital content that is an integral part of a physical product falls inside scope. This is a dramatically broader reach than the 2009 Directive's energy-related-products focus.

Eight categories are excluded (Article 1(2), points a-h):
- food (Regulation (EC) No 178/2002)
- feed (Regulation (EC) No 178/2002)
- medicinal products for human use (Directive 2001/83/EC)
- veterinary medicinal products (Regulation (EU) 2019/6)
- living plants, animals and micro-organisms
- products of human origin
- products of plants and animals relating directly to their future reproduction
- vehicles under the agricultural/forestry, two-/three-wheel, and motor vehicle type-approval Regulations ((EU) No 167/2013, (EU) No 168/2013, (EU) 2018/858) - but only for the product aspects already covered by those sector-specific type-approval regimes; e-bikes and e-scooters are NOT excluded, and tyres (a vehicle component) are explicitly within scope and one of the first working-plan priorities.

Products whose sole purpose is to serve defence or national security are excluded from any product group (Article 5(5)); other military/sensitive equipment and space products get requirements that balance sustainability against operational resilience (recitals); medical devices get requirements that do not compromise patient/user health and safety.

Second-hand products already circulating in the Union are not treated as new products and do not need to comply with delegated acts adopted after they were first placed on the market - but remanufactured products count as new and must comply if they fall inside scope (recital 17).

## Ecodesign requirements: the delegated-act architecture

ESPR is a framework, not a requirements catalogue. Article 4 empowers the Commission to adopt delegated acts under Article 290 TFEU that supplement the Regulation with binding "ecodesign requirements" for a specific product group (or, where technical similarities allow, "horizontal" requirements spanning several product groups - Article 5(7)). Key mechanics:

- **Product aspects** (Article 5(1)): durability, reliability, reusability, upgradability, repairability, possibility of maintenance/refurbishment, presence of substances of concern, energy use/efficiency, water use/efficiency, resource use/efficiency, recycled content, remanufacturing potential, recyclability, possibility of material recovery, environmental impacts (carbon and environmental footprint), expected waste generation.
- **Two requirement types** (Article 5(9)): **performance requirements** (Article 6) set minimum/maximum levels or non-quantitative improvement obligations for a product parameter, and cannot restrict a chemical substance's presence for reasons of chemical safety alone; **information requirements** (Article 7) mandate that products be accompanied by information - always including, as a minimum, DPP data and substances-of-concern data.
- **Lead time**: the date of application of a delegated act cannot be earlier than 18 months from its entry into force, except in duly justified cases (Article 4(4)).
- **Criteria a requirement must meet** (Article 5(11)): no significant negative impact on product functionality or on consumer affordability, no adverse health/safety effect, no disproportionate negative impact on economic-operator competitiveness (including SMEs), no imposed proprietary technology, no disproportionate administrative burden.
- **Impact assessment mandatory** before any delegated act (Article 5(10)).
- **Article 8** lists what every delegated act must contain at minimum: the product group definition and CN commodity codes, the requirements themselves, test/measurement/calculation methods, the conformity assessment module, the manufacturer information duties, the transitional period, and a scheduled review date.

## The working plan (Article 18)

The working plan is the Commission's rolling prioritisation document, covering at least three years and updated regularly, presented in draft to Parliament first. **The first working plan was statutorily due by 19 April 2025** and had to prioritise these product groups (Article 18(5), points a-k):

(a) iron and steel; (b) aluminium; (c) textiles, in particular garments and footwear; (d) furniture, including mattresses; (e) tyres; (f) detergents; (g) paints; (h) lubricants; (i) chemicals; (j) energy-related products (new requirements, or review of existing measures carried over from Directive 2009/125/EC); (k) information and communication technology products and other electronics.

If the Commission omits one of these or adds another product group instead, it must justify the deviation in the working plan itself. A separate, later-dated obligation applies to **cement**: if the Construction Products Regulation still lacks adequate carbon/environmental footprint requirements for cement, the Commission must set ecodesign requirements for it by delegated act **not earlier than 31 December 2028 and not later than 1 January 2030** (Article 18(6)).

Consultation runs through the **Ecodesign Forum** (Article 19, a balanced expert group covering Member State representatives and interested parties) and its **Member States Expert Group** subgroup (Article 20).

## Self-regulation measures (Article 21)

As an alternative to a Commission delegated act, at least two economic operators together can submit a self-regulation measure (a voluntary agreement or code of conduct) covering products not yet in scope of a delegated act or the working plan. To be accepted and listed by the Commission it must, among other conditions, cover signatories representing **at least 80% of the market by volume** of the products concerned, include a transparent monitoring plan with an independent inspector, and demonstrably achieve ESPR's objectives faster or more cheaply than regulation would. Non-compliant signatories must be removed after three months without corrective action; the Commission can delist the whole measure if the criteria stop being met.

## The Digital Product Passport (Articles 9 to 15)

The DPP is the most structurally novel element of ESPR: a persistent digital record, connected via a data carrier to a unique product identifier, that accompanies a covered product through its life. As with ecodesign requirements generally, **the DPP is not self-executing** - Article 9(1) makes the passport a market-access condition ("products can only be placed on the market or put into service if a digital product passport is available"), but the substantive content, data carrier choice, layout, model/batch/item level, access rights and update rules for each product group are all fixed by the relevant delegated act under Article 4 (Article 9(2), points a-i).

**Essential requirements (Article 10):** the data carrier must be physically present on the product, its packaging, or accompanying documentation; the unique product identifier and data carrier must comply with recognised standards (Annex III); data must be open-standard, machine-readable, structured, searchable and transferable without vendor lock-in; no customer personal data without explicit GDPR consent; access is governed by the access rights set in the delegated act. The economic operator placing the product on the market must give dealers/online marketplaces a digital copy of the data carrier within five working days of a request, and must keep a **back-up copy of the DPP through a digital product passport service provider** (Article 10(3)-(4)).

**Technical design and operation (Article 11):** full interoperability between DPPs; free-of-charge, easy access for customers, manufacturers, importers, distributors, dealers, repairers, independent operators, refurbishers, remanufacturers, recyclers, market surveillance and customs authorities, civil society and trade unions, according to their respective access rights; the DPP must remain accessible even after the responsible operator's insolvency, liquidation or cessation of EU activity; data authentication, reliability, integrity, security and fraud-prevention must be assured. DPP service providers cannot sell, reuse or process the data beyond what storage/processing requires, unless separately agreed.

**Unique identifiers (Article 12):** unique operator identifiers and unique facility identifiers must comply with the standards in Annex III; where one does not yet exist, the economic operator creating/updating the DPP must request it on the relevant actor's behalf.

**The DPP registry (Article 13):** the Commission had to set up the registry **by 19 July 2026**, storing at least the unique identifiers (plus commodity codes for products entering free circulation, and the unique identifiers for batteries under Article 77(3) of the Batteries Regulation). The Commission is the data controller (Regulation (EU) 2018/1725). Economic operators upload the required data and receive an automatically generated **unique registration identifier** - explicitly **not proof of compliance** with ESPR or any other Union law (Article 13(5)).

**Public web portal (Article 14):** the Commission must run a publicly accessible web portal letting stakeholders search and compare DPP data, subject to the applicable access rights - a separate surface from the registry itself.

**Customs controls (Article 15):** from the moment the registry is operational, anyone releasing a covered product for free circulation must provide the unique registration identifier to customs; customs can only release the product after verifying the identifier and commodity code against the registry. That verification becomes automatic once the registry is interconnected with the **EU Customs Single Window Certificates Exchange System (EU CSW-CERTEX)**, an interconnection due **within four years** of the entry into force of the relevant implementing act.

For deeper, continuously refreshed DPP implementation tracking (the registry's actual go-live date, the harmonised standards adopted for it, and the Commission's indicative sector rollout dates for batteries, textiles, furniture and more) see the companion guide `ecodesign_digital_product_passport.md`.

## Labels (Articles 16-17)

Where an information requirement calls for a label, the relevant delegated act fixes the label's content, layout, display method (including for distance selling) and, where relevant, electronic generation. Labels showing a class of performance must let customers easily compare products and pick the better performer. For energy-related products already carrying an energy label under Regulation (EU) 2017/1369, the Commission may substitute an ESPR label only where the extra information is more relevant and comprehensive than what the energy label already covers. Mimicking an Article 16 label, or otherwise misleading customers about it, is prohibited outright (Article 17) - this is a market-access bar, not merely an enforcement matter.

## Self-regulation, SME support and the destruction ban's carve-outs

Article 22 requires the Commission to support SME (and especially microenterprise) participation with digital tools and tailored guidance accompanying delegated acts, and requires Member States to run at least a one-stop-shop-type mechanism to help SMEs adapt, alongside optional financial and technical support measures.

## The ban on destroying unsold consumer products (Chapter VI, Articles 23-26)

This is one of ESPR's most publicly visible provisions and applies directly from the Regulation's own text, with no delegated act required to trigger the core prohibition.

- **Article 23** - a general principle: economic operators must take reasonably expected measures to prevent the need to destroy unsold consumer products.
- **Article 24 - disclosure.** Economic operators that discard (or have discarded on their behalf) unsold consumer products must publicly disclose, at least on their website, annually: the number and weight of products discarded per year by type/category; the reasons for discarding (including any derogation relied on); the proportion sent to reuse/refurbishment/remanufacturing, recycling, other recovery, or disposal, following the waste hierarchy; and measures taken/planned to prevent destruction. **This disclosure duty does not apply to micro and small enterprises, and applies to medium-sized enterprises only from 19 July 2030.** The first implementing act on disclosure format was due by 19 July 2025.
- **Article 25 - the ban itself.** **From 19 July 2026, destroying unsold consumer products listed in Annex VII is prohibited.** As with disclosure, **the ban does not apply to micro and small enterprises, and applies to medium-sized enterprises only from 19 July 2030.** Operators outside the ban's scope cannot accept supplied products for the purpose of circumventing it. The Commission can extend Annex VII by delegated act (to add new products or align commodity codes), and must adopt delegated-act derogations (health/hygiene/safety, unrepairable damage, unfitness for purpose, refused donations, unsuitability for reuse/remanufacture, IP-infringing/counterfeit products, or where destruction has the least negative environmental impact) - the first such derogation act was due by 19 July 2025. Annex VII's exact initial product list is not in Brubru's reading copy of the operative text; verify directly against EUR-Lex before quoting specific categories. Article 18(3) records that the Commission will specifically consider adding electrical and electronic equipment to the destruction-ban list once it first identifies further candidate products.
- **Article 26 - consolidated reporting.** By **19 July 2027, and every 36 months thereafter**, the Commission must publish consolidated information on the prevalence and comparative environmental impact of unsold-goods destruction by product group, based on operators' Article 24 disclosures.

## Green public procurement (Article 65)

Once a product group is covered by a delegated act, contracting authorities and entities (under Directives 2014/24/EU and 2014/25/EU) must award public contracts complying with Commission-set minimum requirements - technical specifications, award criteria, contract performance conditions or targets, set by implementing act. Award criteria must carry a minimum weighting of **15% to 30%** in the tender evaluation; procurement targets must reach a minimum of **50%** of relevant procurement (by contracting authority, entity, or aggregated national level, annually or multiannually) going to the most environmentally sustainable products, benchmarked against the two highest performance classes or best available performance levels. Article 64 separately lets Member States offer incentives for top-performing products, aimed at the top two populated performance classes or EU Ecolabel holders.

## Obligations of economic operators (Chapter VII)

Once a delegated act covers a product, ESPR imposes New Legislative Framework-style duties across the value chain: manufacturers must design to the performance requirements, ensure the accompanying information and DPP exist, run the specified conformity assessment procedure, draw up the EU declaration of conformity, affix CE marking, keep technical documentation for 10 years (unless the delegated act sets a different period), and provide digital instructions accessible online for at least 10 years after placing on the market. Importers must verify manufacturer compliance before placing products on the market and cannot do so if they doubt conformity. Distributors must exercise due care and check for CE marking, required documentation and labelling before making a product available. Dealers must ensure customers can access DPP and label information, including in distance selling. Fulfilment service providers must not let warehousing/dispatch conditions jeopardise compliance. Online marketplaces cooperate with market surveillance authorities and must have a single contact point. Article 40 bars any behaviour - contractual, commercial or technical - that circumvents compliance, explicitly including "defeat device"-style test-detection and post-sale software updates that worsen performance without explicit customer consent.

## Conformity, market surveillance and enforcement (Chapters VIII-XII)

Conformity assessment follows the EU's New Legislative Framework: presumption of conformity for products meeting harmonised standards, common specifications where standards are missing, module-based conformity assessment (Article 4(5), tied to Decision No 768/2008/EC modules or Annex IV of ESPR itself), the EU declaration of conformity (Article 44) and CE marking (Articles 45-46). Chapter IX sets out the full notified-body regime (notification, independence, competence, subcontracting, monitoring) for product groups where third-party conformity assessment is required. Market surveillance (Chapter XI) runs through national strategies coordinated via the ADCO group, with a Commission benchmarking report due every four years (first by 19 July 2028) and cost-recovery rights for authorities that test non-compliant products. The safeguard procedure (Chapter XII) follows the standard EU pattern: national corrective action, notification, and - where objections arise - a binding Commission decision via implementing act on whether the national measure is justified.

## Delegated and implementing powers

The Article 4 (ecodesign requirements), Article 10(1) second subparagraph, Article 11 third paragraph, Article 12(4) and Article 25(3)/(5) delegations run for **five years from 18 July 2024**, tacitly renewable unless Parliament or Council object at least three months before expiry; either institution can revoke at any time, and either can object within two months (extendable by two more) of notification of any individual delegated act. Comitology implementing acts (registry arrangements, common specifications, disclosure format, green public procurement minimum requirements, digital credential procedures) run through the standard examination procedure (Article 73(3)) or, for self-regulation-measure listing/delisting, the advisory procedure (Article 73(2)).

## Penalties, evaluation and consumer redress

Member States must set effective, proportionate, dissuasive penalties (at minimum: fines and time-limited exclusion from public procurement - Article 74), calibrated to factors including gravity, intent, financial situation, economic benefit gained and environmental damage caused. The Commission must evaluate the whole Regulation **by 19 July 2030 and every six years thereafter** (Article 75(2)), and separately evaluate whether to extend ESPR to **social sustainability requirements by 19 July 2028** (Article 75(4)). Article 76 makes the manufacturer (or, failing EU establishment, the importer/authorised representative, or failing that the fulfilment service provider) liable for consumer damage caused by non-compliance with ecodesign requirements, without prejudice to other consumer remedies.

## Repeal and transition from the 2009 Ecodesign Directive

Directive 2009/125/EC is repealed with effect from 18 July 2024, but narrow parts of it (definitions and specific annexes) continue to apply in parallel: **until 31 December 2026** for a named list of energy-related product categories still governed by existing implementing measures (photovoltaic panels, space/combination/water heaters, solid fuel local space heaters, air conditioners and comfort fans, solid fuel boilers, air heating/cooling products, ventilation units, vacuum cleaners, cooking appliances, water pumps, industrial fans, circulators, external power supplies, computers/servers/data storage, power transformers, professional refrigeration equipment, imaging equipment); and **until 31 December 2030** for products under other existing implementing measures, but only insofar as amendments address technical issues with those measures. Market surveillance, anti-circumvention and safeguard provisions of the new Regulation (Articles 3, 40, 66-71) already apply to products still regulated under the old Directive's implementing measures.

## Glossary

- **Ecodesign requirement** - a performance requirement or information requirement (or both) aimed at making a product, including its value-chain processes, more environmentally sustainable (Article 2(7)).
- **Performance requirement** - a quantitative or non-quantitative requirement for a product to reach a given performance level on a product parameter listed in Annex I (Article 2(8)).
- **Information requirement** - the obligation for a product to be accompanied by specified information, as detailed in Article 7(2) (Article 2(9)).
- **Digital Product Passport (DPP)** - a set of product-specific data, defined by the applicable delegated act, accessible electronically via a data carrier under Chapter III (Article 2(28)).
- **Product group** - a set of products serving similar purposes, similar in use or functional properties, and similar in consumer perception (Article 2(5)).
- **Delegated act** - the Article 290 TFEU instrument through which the Commission supplements ESPR with binding, product-group-specific ecodesign requirements (Article 4).
- **Working plan** - the Commission's rolling, at-least-three-year plan listing prioritised product groups and their estimated requirement-setting timelines (Article 18(3)).
- **Destruction of unsold consumer products** - intentional damaging or discarding of an unsold consumer product as waste, excluding discarding solely for preparing-for-reuse, refurbishment or remanufacturing (Article 2(34)).
- **Substance of concern** - a substance meeting REACH SVHC criteria, certain CLP hazard classes, POPs Regulation coverage, or that negatively affects reuse/recycling of the product's materials (Article 2(27)).
- **Self-regulation measure** - a voluntary agreement or code of conduct that economic operators submit as an alternative to a delegated act, subject to Commission vetting and an 80% market-share threshold (Article 21).
- **Economic operator** - collective term covering the manufacturer, authorised representative, importer, distributor, dealer and fulfilment service provider (Article 2(46)).
- **Premature obsolescence** - a design feature, action or omission that makes a product non-functional or worse-performing other than through normal wear and tear (Article 2(21)).

## Lineage

- **2005** - Directive 2005/32/EC, the original Ecodesign Framework Directive for energy-using products
- **2009** - Directive 2009/125/EC recasts and extends to energy-related products (the direct predecessor ESPR repeals)
- **2010** - EU Ecolabel Regulation (EC) No 66/2010; presumption of conformity later carried into ESPR (Article 41(4))
- **2017** - Energy Labelling Regulation (EU) 2017/1369, the sibling instrument for energy-related product labels and the EPREL database
- **December 2019** - European Green Deal communication sets the overarching sustainability strategy
- **March 2020** - Circular Economy Action Plan (CEAP) commits to a "sustainable products" policy framework, the direct policy trigger for ESPR
- **30 March 2022** - Commission proposal COM(2022) 142
- **23 April 2024 / 27 May 2024** - Parliament position / Council decision
- **13 June 2024** - ESPR signed
- **28 June 2024 / 18 July 2024** - OJ publication / entry into force
- **12 July 2023** - Batteries Regulation (EU) 2023/1542 adopted (later amended by ESPR Article 78 to link the battery unique identifier into the ESPR registry)
- **16 April 2025** - first ESPR and Energy Labelling Working Plan adopted (SWD(2025) 130), covering the Article 18(5) statutory priority list
