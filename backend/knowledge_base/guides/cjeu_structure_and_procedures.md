# Court of Justice of the European Union -- Structure, Procedures and Case Types

## QUICK FACTS
- **Full name**: Court of Justice of the European Union (CJEU), comprising two courts: the Court of Justice and the General Court
- **Seat**: Luxembourg (Grand Duchy of Luxembourg)
- **Treaty basis**: Articles 251-281 TFEU; Statute annexed as Protocol No 3 to the Treaties
- **Two-court structure**: Court of Justice (appeals, preliminary references, infringement actions) + General Court (direct actions at first instance). The Civil Service Tribunal was absorbed into the General Court in September 2016.
- **Court of Justice composition**: 27 Judges (one per Member State) + 11 Advocates General; sit in full court, grand chamber (15 judges), or chambers of 5 or 3
- **General Court composition**: 54 Judges (2 per Member State since the 2019 reform)
- **Working language**: French (internal deliberations and drafting); output published in all 24 official EU languages
- **Jurisdictional categories**: preliminary references (Art 267 TFEU); infringement actions (Arts 258-260 TFEU); annulment actions (Art 263 TFEU); failure-to-act actions (Art 265 TFEU); damages actions (Arts 268 + 340 TFEU); staff cases (Art 270 TFEU); appeals from the General Court; opinions on draft international agreements (Art 218(11) TFEU)
- **Average duration**: 16-18 months (Court of Justice reference cases); 3-4 years (General Court direct actions)
- **Where output is published**: EUR-Lex (case-law section, CELEX sector 6); Reports of Cases; InfoCuria / Curia case-law database
- **Key URLs**: General info: https://curia.europa.eu/jcms/jcms/Jo2_6999/en/ | Members: https://curia.europa.eu/jcms/jcms/Jo2_7026/en/ | Procedures: https://curia.europa.eu/jcms/jcms/Jo2_7024/en/

The CJEU is the supreme judicial authority of the EU legal order. Its rulings on preliminary references bind all national courts of the Member States. Its annulment judgments can strike down EU legislation. Public-affairs professionals encounter the CJEU primarily when a regulation or directive is challenged by a Member State or industry body, when a national court seeks guidance on EU law, or when the Commission pursues a Member State for non-compliance.

## The Two-Court Structure

The CJEU as an institution contains two separate courts.

**Court of Justice** (commonly called "the Court" or simply "CJEU" in shorthand) sits at the apex. It handles: (a) all preliminary references from national courts; (b) infringement proceedings brought by the Commission or another Member State; (c) annulment actions brought by EU institutions against each other, or by Member States against EU institutions; (d) appeals on points of law from the General Court; and (e) opinions on proposed international agreements.

**General Court** (formerly the Court of First Instance until 2009) handles: (a) direct actions for annulment brought by natural or legal persons (companies, NGOs, individuals) against EU acts; (b) direct actions by Member States against the Commission (in certain cases, principally state-aid and anti-dumping); (c) actions in the area of EU intellectual property (EUIPO trade-mark and design rulings); (d) staff cases since 2016.

The **Civil Service Tribunal**, which existed from 2004 to 2016 as a specialised panel for EU staff disputes, was dissolved in September 2016. Its pending caseload and all future staff cases transferred to the General Court. Historical decisions carry the ECLI court code `F` (see `ecli_european_case_law_identifier.md`).

## Jurisdictional Types

### Preliminary References -- Article 267 TFEU

The most-used procedure. A national court or tribunal, when it has doubts about the interpretation or validity of EU law, may (and in final-instance courts, must) refer a question to the Court of Justice. The Court gives a ruling on the EU law question; the national court then applies that ruling to decide the case. Preliminary rulings are binding on all national courts in the EU, not just the referring court.

Businesses and public-affairs practitioners are often interested in preliminary references because they are the main channel through which EU secondary legislation is interpreted authoritatively and, where necessary, declared invalid. Notable examples: the Google Spain right-to-be-forgotten ruling (ECLI:EU:C:2014:317), the Schrems I and II decisions on data transfers.

### Infringement Actions -- Articles 258-260 TFEU

The Commission (Art 258) or another Member State (Art 259) brings an action against a Member State for failing to fulfil its obligations under EU law. The procedure has two stages: a pre-litigation administrative phase (letter of formal notice, reasoned opinion) before the Commission refers to the Court. If the Court finds an infringement, the Member State must comply. If it does not, the Commission may return under Art 260 TFEU and request financial penalties (lump sum and/or periodic penalty payments).

A recent landmark example is Commission v Hungary on the anti-LGBTI law (see `cjeu_hungary_lgbti_article2_judgment.md`), the first ruling to find a Member State in breach of Article 2 TEU values as a free-standing legal basis.

### Annulment Actions -- Article 263 TFEU

Actions to annul an EU act (regulation, directive, decision, etc.) on grounds of lack of competence, infringement of essential procedural requirements, infringement of the Treaties or a rule of law relating to their application, or misuse of powers. Privileged applicants (EU institutions, Member States) have automatic standing. Private parties (individuals, companies) must demonstrate that the act is of direct and individual concern to them -- a notoriously strict test.

Annulment actions by private parties go to the General Court at first instance. Those between EU institutions or between Member States and institutions go to the Court of Justice directly.

### Failure to Act -- Article 265 TFEU

An action against an EU institution, body, office or agency that has failed to act in breach of the Treaties. Before bringing the action, the applicant must first call on the institution to act; the institution then has two months to respond. If it fails to define its position, the action may proceed.

### Damages Actions -- Articles 268 and 340 TFEU

Actions for non-contractual liability of the Union. The Union must make good, in accordance with the general principles common to the laws of the Member States, damage caused by its institutions or by its servants in the performance of their duties. Art 268 confers jurisdiction on the Court of Justice; Art 340 provides the substantive standard (Bergaderm conditions: sufficient breach of a rule conferring rights on individuals; causation; actual damage).

### Staff Cases -- Article 270 TFEU

Disputes between the EU and its servants (officials, temporary staff, contract agents) are brought before the General Court under Art 270. Before the 2016 reform, these went to the Civil Service Tribunal.

### Appeals from the General Court

Decisions of the General Court may be appealed to the Court of Justice, but only on points of law (not facts). Appeals may challenge lack of jurisdiction, procedural irregularities, or error of law in the General Court's judgment.

### Opinions on International Agreements -- Article 218(11) TFEU

Any EU institution or Member State may request a prior opinion from the Court of Justice on whether a proposed international agreement is compatible with the Treaties. If the Court gives a negative opinion, the agreement may only enter into force after amendment of the Treaties. This procedure has been used for the EU's accession to the ECHR (Opinion 2/13, 2014 -- negative).

## Procedural Stages

All CJEU procedures follow a common architecture, with variations by case type.

1. **Lodging**: the originating document (reference, application, appeal) is lodged with the Registry. The Registry assigns a case number (e.g. C-xxx/yy for Court of Justice, T-xxx/yy for General Court).
2. **Written procedure**: exchange of pleadings (application, defence, reply, rejoinder). In preliminary references, the Court invites the parties to the main proceedings, the Member States, the Commission, and relevant EU institutions to submit written observations.
3. **Preparatory report**: the Judge-Rapporteur drafts a report for the hearing, summarising the facts and arguments.
4. **Oral procedure**: a hearing before the assigned formation. In practice, many cases proceed without a hearing at the Court's request.
5. **Advocate General opinion**: in cases assigned to an Advocate General, a non-binding but highly influential reasoned opinion is delivered before deliberation. The Court follows AG opinions in the majority of cases.
6. **Deliberation**: the formation deliberates in private in French.
7. **Judgment**: the final ruling, published in all 24 official languages. Judgments of the Court of Justice are collegiate (no dissents published).

## Special Procedures

### Simplified Procedure

Where a preliminary reference is identical or closely related to a question already decided, or where the answer admits of no reasonable doubt, the Court may rule by reasoned order without full procedure (Art 99 of the Rules of Procedure). This accelerates routine or repetitive references.

### Expedited Procedure

Either court may grant expedited treatment on application, compressing the written phase and moving rapidly to hearing. Used in cases of particular urgency, such as constitutional crises or matters with immediate economic impact.

### Urgent Preliminary Ruling (PPU -- Procedure Prejudicielle d'Urgence)

Introduced in 2008 under Art 267(4) TFEU for questions in the Area of Freedom, Security and Justice (AFSJ): criminal law, border control, asylum, family law with cross-border implications. A preliminary reference under PPU can be determined in weeks rather than months. The referring court must demonstrate genuine urgency (e.g. person in detention, child custody across borders). PPU cases are decided by a chamber of five judges and skip the standard AG opinion in most instances, though an AG may be called on for a brief recommendation.

## Court of Justice -- Composition and Formations

**Judges**: 27 in total, one per Member State, appointed by common accord of Member State governments for a renewable term of six years. Partial renewal every three years (alternating between 13 and 14 positions). Judges elect their President and Vice-Presidents from among themselves for renewable three-year terms.

**Advocates General**: 11, appointed on the same terms as judges. Each AG is assigned to individual cases by the First Advocate General and delivers an independent, non-binding opinion on the legal issues before the Court deliberates. The AG is neither an advocate for a party nor a public prosecutor but an impartial officer of the Court.

**Formations**:
- **Full Court**: Reserved for cases of exceptional importance (Art 218(11) opinions; proceedings against members of EU institutions; cases of particular complexity). 15 judges sit as quorum.
- **Grand Chamber**: 15 judges (President + Vice-Presidents + 13 judges). Used for important or novel questions of law referred by Member States, EU institutions, or cases raising significant constitutional issues.
- **Chambers of Five**: Standard formation for most preliminary references and direct actions.
- **Chambers of Three**: Used for simpler or routine cases.

## General Court -- Composition and Formations

**Judges**: 54 (2 per Member State following the reform completed in 2019). The General Court does not have permanent Advocates General; in complex cases, a judge may be called upon to act as AG.

**Formations**: Grand Chamber (15 judges), chambers of 5 or 3, single judge (for straightforward cases, particularly staff matters and EUIPO appeals). The President of the General Court is elected from among the judges for a renewable three-year term.

## Advocates General

The institution of the Advocate General is distinctive to the CJEU and has no direct parallel in national court systems. AGs are full members of the Court but their role is advisory. Their opinions are published before the Court's judgment and often provide the most detailed and reasoned analysis of the law, making them influential reading for practitioners even when the Court departs from the AG's conclusion.

Not every Court of Justice case receives an AG opinion. Cases of insufficient difficulty or novelty may be decided without one (Art 20(5) of the Statute).

## Languages Regime

The CJEU has 24 authentic languages corresponding to the EU's official languages. However, internal working language is French. This means:
- Judges deliberate in French.
- The judgment is drafted in French and then translated.
- The authentic version is that in the language of the case (in preliminary references, the language of the referring court; in direct actions, the applicant's language choice, subject to constraints for Member State respondents).

Practitioners should note that where subtleties of translation matter for interpretation, the French text is the primary internal text, and comparing language versions across EUR-Lex can clarify interpretive ambiguities.

## Average Duration and Caseload

The Court of Justice handles approximately 900-1,000 new cases per year. Average duration for preliminary references is 16-18 months from lodging to judgment; expedited procedures can reduce this to under 6 months; PPU cases are often decided within 2-4 months.

The General Court handles around 800-900 new cases annually. Direct actions for annulment in complex areas (competition, state aid, intellectual property) regularly take 3-5 years. The 2019 doubling of the General Court bench was introduced specifically to address the backlog.

## Where CJEU Output is Published

**EUR-Lex case law** (CELEX sector 6): every judgment, order, and opinion since 1954 is available at https://eur-lex.europa.eu/collection/eu-law/eu-case-law.html. CELEX identifiers in sector 6 take the form `6{year}{court-code}R{case-number-zero-padded}` (e.g. `62017TJ0160` = General Court case T-160/17, entered in 2017). See `ecli_european_case_law_identifier.md` for the ECLI system.

**Reports of Cases** (formerly European Court Reports, ECR): the official series of reported judgments, now published electronically in EUR-Lex.

**Curia / InfoCuria** (https://curia.europa.eu/jcms/jcms/Jo2_6999/en/): the Court's own case-law database, searchable by case number, ECLI, date, formation, subject matter or applicant. InfoCuria also provides the procedural history (lodging date, admissibility decisions, hearing dates) which EUR-Lex does not always surface. For most practitioner searches, InfoCuria is the faster route; EUR-Lex is the canonical archive for full-text machine-readable versions. See the planned guide `curia_infocuria_case_law_search.md` for a step-by-step search walkthrough.

**ECLI**: the preferred citation form for chat answers in Brubru (e.g. `ECLI:EU:C:2014:317` for Google Spain). Never fabricate an ECLI; if the identifier cannot be verified, direct the user to the InfoCuria search or EUR-Lex case-law search. Full ECLI reference: `ecli_european_case_law_identifier.md`.

## Practical Relevance for Public-Affairs Users

Public-affairs and regulatory-affairs professionals encounter the CJEU in three main contexts:

1. **Challenging EU legislation**: if an EU act disproportionately affects your organisation or sector, Art 263 TFEU annulment before the General Court may be an option (direct and individual concern test is strict; specialist legal advice is essential).
2. **Monitoring infringement proceedings**: if a Member State is being pursued by the Commission for non-implementation of a directive that your organisation relies on, Art 260 TFEU penalty proceedings can accelerate compliance and create leverage for advocacy.
3. **Tracking preliminary references**: national courts regularly refer questions about the interpretation of sector-specific EU legislation (competition, data protection, customs, environmental law). A pending reference can signal legal uncertainty that affects strategy; a judgment can clarify or expand obligations.

For monitoring legislative implementation and infringement status, see `eu_law_application_monitoring.md` and `finding_and_citing_eu_law.md`. For understanding the legislative procedures that produce the acts the CJEU reviews, see `eu_legislative_procedures_explained.md`.
