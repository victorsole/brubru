## Context and Purpose

The GDPR represents the most significant overhaul of EU data protection law since the 1995 Data Protection Directive. Its two-part objective is to protect the fundamental right of natural persons to the protection of their personal data (Art 1(2)) while simultaneously ensuring the free movement of personal data within the Union is not restricted or prohibited for data protection reasons (Art 1(3)).

The Commission proposed the GDPR in January 2012 to address the fragmentation that had resulted from divergent national implementations of the 1995 Directive, the explosion of digital data processing, and the emergence of global tech platforms for which no effective harmonised enforcement existed. A four-year legislative process followed, culminating in the trilogue agreement and adoption in April 2016.

The two-year application delay (May 2016 to May 2018) was deliberately granted to allow businesses, public authorities and civil society time to adapt.

## Scope

### Material Scope (Art 2)

The GDPR applies to the processing of personal data wholly or partly by automated means, and to non-automated processing where the data form part of, or are intended to form part of, a filing system. It covers both manual and digital processing.

It does not apply to: activities outside Union law (e.g. national security); Member State activities under CFSP (Chapter 2 Title V TEU); purely personal or household activities; or processing by competent authorities for criminal law enforcement purposes (covered by Directive (EU) 2016/680 instead).

### Territorial Scope (Art 3) -- the extraterritorial reach

The GDPR's territorial reach goes well beyond EU borders. It applies to:
1. Any controller or processor established in the EU, regardless of where the processing takes place.
2. Non-EU controllers or processors whose activities are related to: (a) offering goods or services to data subjects in the Union (whether paid or free); or (b) monitoring the behaviour of data subjects as far as their behaviour takes place within the Union.
3. Processing in a place where Member State law applies by virtue of public international law (e.g. diplomatic missions).

This means a business based in the United States, India or Australia that targets EU consumers online or tracks their behaviour is subject to the GDPR in full.

## Key Definitions (Art 4)

- **Personal data:** any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person (the data subject). IP addresses, cookie identifiers and location data are personal data where they can be linked to an individual.
- **Processing:** any operation performed on personal data -- collection, recording, organisation, structuring, storage, adaptation, retrieval, use, disclosure, erasure, destruction.
- **Controller:** the natural or legal person, public authority, agency or body that determines the purposes and means of processing.
- **Processor:** the person or body that processes personal data on behalf of the controller.
- **Consent:** freely given, specific, informed and unambiguous indication of the data subject's wishes by a statement or clear affirmative action.
- **Personal data breach:** a breach of security leading to accidental or unlawful destruction, loss, alteration, unauthorised disclosure of, or access to, personal data.
- **Pseudonymisation:** processing such that data can no longer be attributed to a specific data subject without additional information that is kept separately.
- **Profiling:** any form of automated processing to evaluate personal aspects relating to a natural person, in particular to analyse or predict performance at work, economic situation, health, preferences, interests, behaviour, location or movements.

## The Seven Principles (Art 5)

1. **Lawfulness, fairness and transparency** -- there must be a legal basis; the data subject must be informed.
2. **Purpose limitation** -- data is collected for specified, explicit and legitimate purposes and not further processed incompatibly.
3. **Data minimisation** -- only the data that is adequate, relevant and limited to what is necessary.
4. **Accuracy** -- data must be accurate and kept up to date; inaccurate data must be rectified or erased without delay.
5. **Storage limitation** -- kept for no longer than necessary for the purposes.
6. **Integrity and confidentiality** -- appropriate technical and organisational security measures must be in place.
7. **Accountability** -- the controller is responsible for and must be able to demonstrate compliance with all of the above.

Failure to comply with the principles in Art 5 attracts Tier 2 fines (up to EUR 20 million or 4% of global turnover).

---

## Legal Bases for Processing (Art 6)

There are exactly six legal bases; processing is unlawful unless at least one applies:
- **(a) Consent** (also see Art 7 for conditions; Art 8 for children)
- **(b) Contract** -- necessary for contract performance or pre-contractual steps at the subject's request
- **(c) Legal obligation** -- necessary for compliance with a legal obligation of the controller
- **(d) Vital interests** -- necessary to protect the vital interests of the data subject or another person
- **(e) Public task** -- necessary for performance of a task in the public interest or the exercise of official authority
- **(f) Legitimate interests** -- necessary for the controller's or a third party's legitimate interests, provided not overridden by the subject's interests or rights (unavailable to public authorities acting in their tasks)

The choice of legal basis has practical consequences: if consent is chosen, withdrawal must be as easy as giving it, and the controller must be able to prove consent was given (Art 7(1)).

---

## Special Categories (Art 9)

Processing of the following is prohibited unless one of ten specific exceptions applies: racial or ethnic origin; political opinions; religious or philosophical beliefs; trade union membership; genetic data; biometric data (for unique identification); health data; sex life or sexual orientation data.

Exceptions include: explicit consent; employment/social security law; vital interests (when the subject cannot consent); legitimate not-for-profit body activities; publicly made data; legal claims; substantial public interest; preventive/occupational medicine; public health; and archiving/research/statistics.

## Data Subject Rights (Chapter III, Arts 12-22)

The controller must respond to rights requests within **one month**, free of charge, extendable by two months for complex/numerous requests:

| Right | Article | Core content |
|---|---|---|
| Transparency and information | Arts 12-14 | Privacy information at collection (or within 1 month if indirect) |
| Right of access | Art 15 | Confirmation of processing, copy of data, supplementary information |
| Right to rectification | Art 16 | Correction of inaccurate data without undue delay |
| Right to erasure (right to be forgotten) | Art 17 | Erasure where data no longer necessary, consent withdrawn, unlawfully processed, subject objects |
| Right to restriction | Art 18 | Limit processing in specified circumstances (e.g. whilst accuracy is contested) |
| Right to data portability | Art 20 | Structured, machine-readable copy; direct controller-to-controller transfer where technically feasible; applies only where processing is based on consent or contract and is automated |
| Right to object | Art 21 | Object to processing based on public task or legitimate interests, including profiling; absolute right for direct marketing |
| Automated decision-making and profiling | Art 22 | Not to be subject to decisions based solely on automated processing that produce legal/significant effects; right to human review, to express a point of view and to contest the decision |

## Controller and Processor Obligations (Chapter IV, Arts 24-43)

### Accountability (Art 24)
Controllers must implement appropriate technical and organisational measures (TOMs) and be able to demonstrate compliance.

### Data Protection by Design and by Default (Art 25)
Data protection principles must be embedded in processing systems from design stage (privacy by design) and by default only the minimum data necessary is processed.

### Records of Processing Activities (Art 30)
Both controllers and processors must maintain written records. The SME exemption (fewer than 250 employees) does not apply where processing is not occasional, likely to risk individuals' rights, or involves special categories.

### Processor Contracts (Art 28)
Every controller-processor relationship must be governed by a binding written contract specifying: the subject matter and duration; the nature and purposes of processing; the types of data; the controller's documented instructions; security obligations; sub-processor rules; and deletion/return obligations.

### Security (Art 32)
Appropriate TOMs to address risks from accidental or unlawful destruction, loss, alteration, or unauthorised disclosure/access. This includes (where appropriate): pseudonymisation/encryption; ongoing resilience; timely restoration after incidents; and regular testing/evaluation.

### Data Breach Notification (Arts 33-34)
- **To the DPA (Art 33):** Without undue delay, and where feasible within 72 hours of awareness, unless the breach is unlikely to result in a risk to individuals' rights and freedoms.
- **To the data subject (Art 34):** Without undue delay where the breach is likely to result in a high risk to individuals' rights and freedoms (not required if encryption or other mitigating measures are in place).

### Data Protection Impact Assessment (Art 35)
A DPIA is mandatory before high-risk processing, in particular: systematic and extensive profiling with legal/significant effects; large-scale special-category processing; systematic large-scale monitoring of publicly accessible areas. Where the DPIA shows residual high risk, the controller must consult the DPA before proceeding (Art 36).

### Data Protection Officer (Arts 37-39)
Mandatory for: all public authorities (except courts in their judicial capacity); organisations whose core activities involve large-scale regular and systematic monitoring of data subjects; and those whose core activities involve large-scale special-category or criminal conviction data. The DPO must have expert knowledge, be independent, report to the highest management level, and cannot be dismissed for performing their role. Their contact details must be published and communicated to the supervisory authority.

## International Data Transfers (Chapter V, Arts 44-50)

All transfers of personal data to third countries require one of:
1. **Adequacy decision** (Art 45): Commission has determined the third country provides adequate protection. Current decisions include the EU-US Data Privacy Framework, the UK (IDFR), and others.
2. **Standard contractual clauses** (SCCs): Commission-adopted standard terms binding the parties contractually. Updated SCCs adopted by the Commission in June 2021.
3. **Binding corporate rules** (BCRs) (Art 47): intra-group transfer policies approved by the lead DPA.
4. **Other Art 46 safeguards**: approved codes of conduct, certification, binding administrative instruments.
5. **Art 49 derogations** (for specific cases): explicit consent with risk disclosure, contract necessity, vital interests, public interest, legal claims, or limited public register transfers.

The Schrems II judgment (CJEU, 16 July 2020, Case C-311/18) invalidated the Privacy Shield and established that SCCs require a case-by-case transfer impact assessment (TIA) before use.

---

## Supervisory Authorities and the EDPB (Chapter VI, Arts 51-76)

Each Member State establishes at least one independent Data Protection Authority. DPAs have comprehensive powers: investigative (access to premises, documents, data), corrective (warnings, reprimands, orders, bans on processing, fines) and advisory (opinions, consultations, codes, certifications).

The **one-stop-shop mechanism** (Art 56, 60): for cross-border processing, the DPA of the Member State where the controller has its main establishment acts as lead supervisory authority, coordinating with other concerned DPAs.

The **European Data Protection Board** (EDPB) is an independent EU body composed of the heads of all national DPAs and the European Data Protection Supervisor. It ensures consistent application across the EU, issues binding decisions on cross-border disputes, and publishes guidelines on the interpretation and application of the GDPR.

---

## Remedies, Liability and Fines (Chapter VIII, Arts 77-84)

- Data subjects may lodge complaints with any DPA (Art 77).
- Any person suffering material or non-material damage from a GDPR infringement has the right to compensation from the controller or processor (Art 82). Both material and non-material damage (including distress) are compensable.
- **Tier 1 fines** (Art 83(4)): up to EUR 10 million or 2% of total worldwide annual turnover for infringements of: obligations under Arts 8, 11, 25-39, 42-43 (records, DPO, security, DPIA, processor contracts, certification/monitoring bodies).
- **Tier 2 fines** (Art 83(5)): up to EUR 20 million or 4% of total worldwide annual turnover for infringements of: basic principles (Art 5); legal basis and consent (Arts 6, 7, 9); data subject rights (Arts 12-22); international transfer rules (Arts 44-49); non-compliance with an order or ban by the supervisory authority.

When computing fines, the "undertaking" test applies: for group companies, the relevant turnover is the consolidated worldwide group turnover.

---

## Key Enforcement Cases and EDPB Guidance

The GDPR has produced major enforcement actions:
- Meta/Instagram (Ireland, 2023): EUR 1.2 billion fine for transfers to the US without adequate safeguards.
- Amazon (Luxembourg, 2021): EUR 746 million fine for advertising processing without adequate legal basis.
- WhatsApp Ireland (2021): EUR 225 million fine for transparency failures.

The EDPB has issued extensive guidelines on: consent (2020), data portability (2022), international transfers and TIAs (2021), codes of conduct (2021), DPIA criteria (2017, updated), DPO (2017, updated), right of access (2022), personal data breach notification (2022), and many sector-specific topics.

---

## Relationship to Other EU Data Laws

- **Directive (EU) 2016/680** (Law Enforcement Directive): governs processing by competent authorities for criminal law purposes; the GDPR does not apply to that processing.
- **Regulation (EU) 2018/1725**: applies the same framework to EU institutions and bodies.
- **ePrivacy Directive (Directive 2002/58/EC)**: governs electronic communications privacy (cookies, direct marketing); lex specialis to the GDPR. An ePrivacy Regulation proposal to replace it has been under negotiation since 2017.
- **AI Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689)**: GDPR obligations apply to AI systems that process personal data; the AI Act supplements rather than replaces the GDPR for AI.
- **Data Act (Regulation (EU) 2023/2854)** and **Data Governance Act (Regulation (EU) 2022/868)**: new EU data-sharing frameworks that operate alongside and are without prejudice to the GDPR.
