# Charter of Fundamental Rights of the European Union

## QUICK FACTS
- Full name: Charter of Fundamental Rights of the European Union (often called "the Charter" or "the EU Charter").
- Proclaimed: 7 December 2000, in Nice, by the European Parliament, the Council and the Commission. Re-proclaimed in its current (adapted) form on 12 December 2007, in Strasbourg.
- Legally binding since: 1 December 2009, the date the Treaty of Lisbon entered into force. Article 6(1) TEU gives the Charter "the same legal value as the Treaties", it is EU primary law, not a political declaration.
- Structure: 7 titles, 54 articles, plus a Preamble.
  - Title I -- Dignity (Articles 1-5)
  - Title II -- Freedoms (Articles 6-19)
  - Title III -- Equality (Articles 20-26)
  - Title IV -- Solidarity (Articles 27-38)
  - Title V -- Citizens' Rights (Articles 39-46)
  - Title VI -- Justice (Articles 47-50)
  - Title VII -- General Provisions governing the interpretation and application of the Charter (Articles 51-54)
- Article 51 (Field of application): the Charter binds the EU institutions, bodies, offices and agencies AT ALL TIMES, and binds Member States ONLY when they are "implementing Union law." It does not extend the competences of the EU beyond the Treaties or create new EU powers.
- Article 52 (Scope and interpretation): any limitation on a Charter right must be provided for by law, respect the essence of the right, and be proportionate and genuinely meet objectives of general interest or the need to protect others' rights. Article 52(3) aligns Charter rights that correspond to ECHR rights with the meaning and scope given by the ECHR (the EU can give more extensive protection). Article 52(4)-(5) distinguishes enforceable "rights" from programmatic "principles" (e.g. Article 25 rights of the elderly, Article 37 environmental protection) which are judicially cognisable only in the interpretation of acts implementing them.
- Article 53 (Level of protection): nothing in the Charter may be interpreted as restricting human rights already recognised by Union law, international law, the ECHR, or Member States' constitutions.
- Article 54: prohibition of abuse of rights.
- Not a substitute for the ECHR (European Convention on Human Rights, a Council of Europe treaty, 46 members, enforced by the European Court of Human Rights in Strasbourg). The EU itself is not yet a party to the ECHR; accession is mandated by Article 6(2) TEU and remains an ongoing, stalled process since the CJEU's negative Opinion 2/13 (2014).
- Enforcement: the Court of Justice of the EU (CJEU, Luxembourg) is the ultimate interpreter of the Charter within EU law's scope; national courts apply it when adjudicating matters governed by EU law, often via preliminary references (Article 267 TFEU).
- FRA (EU Agency for Fundamental Rights, Vienna) maintains **Charterpedia**, the reference tool with official explanations, EU and national case law, and comparative constitutional law for each article.
- The European Commission publishes an **Annual Report on the Application of the EU Charter of Fundamental Rights** and maintains a **Charter Strategy** (adopted December 2020) to mainstream Charter compliance across EU law-making, funding and Member State implementation.
- Denmark, Ireland and (until Brexit) the UK negotiated Protocol No 30 ("the UK/Poland opt-out protocol," originally UK and Poland; Poland's declared opt-out was never legally distinct from ordinary Article 51 limits per CJEU case law) clarifying, not disapplying, the Charter's scope for those states -- the CJEU has confirmed the Protocol does not create a genuine opt-out.
- Landmark CJEU jurisprudence: *Åkerberg Fransson* (C-617/10, 2013, defines "implementing Union law" broadly); *Melloni* (C-399/11, 2013, primacy of EU law over higher national fundamental-rights standards in harmonised areas); *Digital Rights Ireland* (C-293/12 and C-594/12, 2014, invalidated the Data Retention Directive on Articles 7-8 grounds); *Schrems* (C-362/14, 2015, and *Schrems II*, C-311/18, 2020, data transfers and Article 47 effective remedy); *Google Spain* (C-131/12, 2014, right to be forgotten under Articles 7-8); *LM/Celmer* (C-216/18 PPU, 2018, European Arrest Warrant and rule-of-law/Article 47 fair-trial risk); the Hungary/Poland rule-of-law conditionality judgments (C-156/21 and C-157/21, 2022) reaffirming Article 2 TEU values underpinning the Charter.

The Charter is the EU's own bill of rights: it is the single most frequently invoked legal text across Brubru's compliance, chat, and legislative-analysis surfaces, because nearly every EU legislative act touching data, migration, criminal justice, consumer protection, non-discrimination or digital rights is required to be interpreted consistently with it.

## What the Charter is (and is not)

The Charter consolidates civil, political, economic and social rights previously scattered across the ECHR, Member States' constitutional traditions, the EU Treaties, and CJEU case law into a single, EU-specific catalogue. Unlike the ECHR, which binds Council of Europe states across virtually all state action, the Charter binds only the EU institutions and, per Article 51(1), Member States "only when they are implementing Union law." This narrower scope is the single most litigated and most commonly misunderstood feature of the Charter.

Two documents are frequently confused with the Charter and should be distinguished in any chat answer:
- **ECHR** (European Convention on Human Rights, 1950, Council of Europe, 46 states, enforced by the European Court of Human Rights). Broader membership, broader material scope, but not an EU legal instrument.
- **Universal Declaration of Human Rights** (UN, 1948): a non-binding declaration, not a treaty and not an EU instrument.

## Article 51: when does the Charter apply?

This is the single most important operative provision for compliance analysis. The Charter applies to:
1. **EU institutions, bodies, offices and agencies**, always, in everything they do (legislating, adjudicating, administering).
2. **Member States**, only "when they are implementing Union law." The CJEU in *Åkerberg Fransson* read this expansively: any national measure that falls "within the scope of EU law" (transposing a directive, applying a regulation, derogating from an EU free-movement rule, or acting under an EU-conferred discretion) triggers Charter scrutiny. Purely internal Member State matters with no EU-law connection remain outside the Charter's reach, the applicable standard there is the national constitution and/or the ECHR.

Article 51(2) is a safeguard clause: the Charter does not establish any new power or task for the EU, nor modify the competences and tasks defined in the Treaties.

## Article 52: limitations, rights vs principles, and ECHR alignment

- **Limitation test** (Article 52(1)): any limitation must be (a) provided for by law, (b) respect the essence of the right, (c) be proportionate, and (d) genuinely meet an EU objective of general interest or protect others' rights and freedoms. This mirrors the CJEU's classic three/four-part proportionality test used throughout EU law.
- **ECHR correspondence** (Article 52(3)): where a Charter right corresponds to an ECHR right, its "meaning and scope" are the same as under the ECHR, though EU law may provide more extensive protection. This is why CJEU rulings on Articles 7 (privacy) and 47 (fair trial) routinely cross-reference ECtHR case law.
- **Rights vs principles** (Article 52(5)): "principles" (e.g. Article 25 rights of the elderly, Article 26 disability integration, Article 37 environmental protection) are not directly enforceable as subjective rights before a court; they are judicially cognisable only in interpreting acts that implement them, and in ruling on their legality. "Rights" (e.g. Article 47 effective remedy, Article 8 data protection) are directly enforceable.

## Charterpedia, the annual report, and the Charter Strategy

- **Charterpedia** (FRA): article-by-article reference tool with the Charter text, official explanations relating to the Charter (2007/C 303/02, itself an interpretive aid under Article 6(1) TEU third subparagraph), relevant CJEU and ECtHR case law, and comparative national constitutional provisions.
- **Commission Annual Report on the Application of the Charter**: published each year (usually spring), reviews how the Charter shaped that year's EU legislative and policy output, flags Member State implementation gaps, and reports on the EU Fundamental Rights Agency's input.
- **EU Charter Strategy** (adopted December 2020): a Commission strategy to (1) ensure effective application of the Charter, (2) empower civil society and rights defenders, and (3) make the Charter more visible and better known, including via mandatory Charter checks in Commission impact assessments and better-regulation procedures.

## Distinguishing the Charter from the ECHR and EU accession to the ECHR

| Feature | EU Charter of Fundamental Rights | ECHR |
|---------|-----------------------------------|------|
| Adopting body | EU (Nice 2000 / binding 2009) | Council of Europe (1950) |
| Membership bound | EU institutions always; Member States only when implementing EU law | 46 Council of Europe states, across virtually all state action |
| Court | CJEU (Luxembourg) | European Court of Human Rights (Strasbourg) |
| EU is a party? | Is the EU's own instrument | Not yet, EU accession mandated by Article 6(2) TEU, stalled since CJEU Opinion 2/13 (2014) found the then-draft accession agreement incompatible with EU law autonomy; negotiations resumed but remain unresolved |

## Key jurisprudence quick-reference

| Case | Year | What it settled |
|------|------|------------------|
| Åkerberg Fransson (C-617/10) | 2013 | Broad reading of "implementing Union law" under Article 51(1) |
| Melloni (C-399/11) | 2013 | EU law primacy over higher national fundamental-rights protection in fully harmonised areas |
| Digital Rights Ireland (C-293/12, C-594/12) | 2014 | Invalidated Data Retention Directive 2006/24/EC on Articles 7-8 grounds (privacy, data protection) |
| Google Spain (C-131/12) | 2014 | "Right to be forgotten" grounded in Articles 7-8 |
| Schrems (C-362/14) | 2015 | Invalidated the Safe Harbour EU-US data-transfer decision |
| Schrems II (C-311/18) | 2020 | Invalidated Privacy Shield; Article 47 effective judicial remedy for EU data subjects |
| LM / Celmer (C-216/18 PPU) | 2018 | European Arrest Warrant execution can be refused where a real risk to Article 47 fair-trial rights exists due to rule-of-law deficiencies |
| Hungary v Parliament and Council; Poland v Parliament and Council (C-156/21, C-157/21) | 2022 | Upheld the rule-of-law conditionality mechanism, reaffirming Article 2 TEU values that underpin Charter enforcement |
