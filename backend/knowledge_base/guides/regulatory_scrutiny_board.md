# Regulatory Scrutiny Board (RSB)

## QUICK FACTS
- What it is: an independent quality-control body inside the European Commission that scrutinises the quality of draft impact assessments, evaluations and fitness checks before a Commission initiative can be adopted by the College of Commissioners
- Legal basis: Decision of the President of the European Commission C(2015) 3263 final of 19 May 2015 (established the Board, replacing the former Impact Assessment Board), updated by Decision P(2020)2 of 23 January 2020 and Decision P(2022)1; current Rules of Procedure adopted 31 January 2023 (Ares(2023)698232)
- Who runs it: nine full-time members serving non-renewable three-year terms (extendable up to one year in exceptional circumstances): a Chair ranked at Director-General level, four Commission officials ("internal members") and four externally recruited temporary agents ("external members"). Composition was widened from seven to nine members by the January 2023 rules revision (previously three internal plus three external plus Chair)
- Current Chair: Leopoldo Rubinacci (Director-General rank); Director: Doriane Givord-Strassel; members include Marek Havrda, Rolf Hoijer, Arianna Vannini, Alexander Gemberg-Wiesike, Emmanuelle Maincent and Rebecca Schindler (verify the full current line-up against the official members page, since membership rotates on three-year terms)
- Reports to: the President of the European Commission and the Vice-President for Interinstitutional Relations and Foresight; acts independently of the policy-making Directorates-General and of any other EU institution, body, office or agency
- Three possible opinions: positive, positive with reservations, or negative
- Impact assessments: a positive or positive-with-reservations opinion is required before an initiative can proceed (normally blocked from moving forward to inter-service consultation and being tabled for adoption until any negative opinion is addressed). A negative opinion means the draft report must be revised and resubmitted to the Board. After a second negative opinion, only the Vice-President for Interinstitutional Relations and Foresight can submit the initiative to the College of Commissioners, which then decides whether to proceed anyway
- Evaluations and fitness checks: also scored positive / positive with reservations / negative, but a negative opinion does not block publication, only a voluntary second submission is possible (no further resubmission after that), and the lead department is expected to reflect the Board's comments regardless
- Negative-opinion rate: has historically run around 30-46% on first submissions of impact assessments year to year (2016-2023), underscoring that the Board is a real quality filter, not a rubber stamp
- Annual report: published every year; the Regulatory Scrutiny Board Annual Report 2025 was published 24 April 2026 by the Secretariat-General
- Official page: https://commission.europa.eu/law/law-making-process/regulatory-scrutiny-board_en
- Members page: https://commission.europa.eu/law/law-making-process/regulatory-scrutiny-board/members-regulatory-scrutiny-board_en
- Not to be confused with: the Fit for Future Platform (F4F), a separate, now-concluded external advisory body (2021-2024) that gave non-binding recommendations on simplifying and modernising EXISTING EU law; the RSB scrutinises the quality of NEW impact assessments and evaluations before adoption (see "RSB vs Fit for Future Platform" below)

## What the Board does

The Regulatory Scrutiny Board is a body embedded within the European Commission, but functionally independent from the policy Directorates-General that draft legislative proposals. Its job sits at the centre of the Commission's Better Regulation system: before a Commissioner-level proposal accompanied by an impact assessment can be tabled for adoption by the College of Commissioners, the underlying impact assessment must first pass through the Board.

The Board examines and issues opinions and recommendations on the quality of draft reports relating to:
- **Impact assessments** for new legislative and non-legislative initiatives with a significant expected economic, social or environmental impact
- **Fitness checks**, comprehensive evaluations of a whole policy area or group of related legal acts (fitness checks are always scrutinised by the Board, unlike ordinary evaluations)
- **Major evaluations** of existing legislation and policies, selected each year by relevance and political priority (not every Commission evaluation goes to the Board)

Crucially, the Board does not judge the political merits or objectives of a proposal. That is the sole preserve of the College of Commissioners, exercising the Commission's Treaty right of initiative. The Board's remit is narrower and more technical: is the problem definition sound, are the policy options genuinely compared, is the evidence base robust, are the expected costs and benefits properly quantified, and is proportionality (including impact on SMEs) adequately addressed. Since 2023 the Board has also begun scrutinising a newly introduced "competitiveness check" and a "consumer impacts" check as part of impact-assessment quality standards.

## How the opinion process works

### Impact assessments (new initiatives)
1. The lead Directorate-General drafts the impact assessment following the Better Regulation Guidelines and Toolbox.
2. The draft report is submitted to the Board, which reviews it against a quality checklist and issues one of three opinions: **positive**, **positive with reservations**, or **negative**.
3. A positive or positive-with-reservations opinion allows the lead department to finalise the impact assessment and table the accompanying initiative for the College's decision.
4. A **negative opinion** means the report is not considered fit for purpose. It must be substantially revised and resubmitted to the Board before the initiative can proceed further through the Commission's internal process (normally before it can move into or complete inter-service consultation and go to the College).
5. If the Board issues a **second negative opinion** on the same file, the initiative can still go forward, but only if the Vice-President for Interinstitutional Relations and Foresight personally submits it to the College of Commissioners, which then takes the final call on whether to proceed regardless of the Board's assessment. This "override" route has been used only occasionally in practice (examples include the Corporate Sustainability Due Diligence Directive, which proceeded after two negative RSB opinions).

### Evaluations and fitness checks (existing legislation)
1. Opinions are again scored positive, positive with reservations, or negative.
2. A negative opinion does **not** prevent the responsible department from finalising and publishing the evaluation or fitness check report.
3. The department may voluntarily resubmit the revised report for a second Board opinion; a further resubmission after that is not available (unlike impact assessments, which in practice can occasionally see more than two rounds by exception).
4. In all cases, the lead department is expected to take the Board's recommendations into account and to document how it did so.

Board opinions, once the related Commission proposal has been adopted, are published on the Commission's transparency register together with the impact assessment or evaluation itself.

## Composition and independence

Since the January 2023 revision of the Rules of Procedure, the Board has nine members:
- **The Chair**, ranked as Director-General, who manages the entire regulatory scrutiny process
- **One Director**-ranked member
- **Four Commission officials** ("internal members"), drawn from within the Commission for the duration of their term and returning to their Directorate-General of origin afterwards
- **Four externally recruited temporary agents** ("external members"), typically academics, economists or former regulators appointed from outside the Commission

All nine work full-time on Board business with no other policy responsibilities, for a non-renewable three-year term (extendable up to one year in exceptional circumstances). Before January 2023 the Board had seven members (Chair plus three internal and three external members); it was created in 2015 with the same basic internal/external split, replacing the earlier, less formal Impact Assessment Board. Members are required to have expertise in macroeconomics, microeconomics, social policy or environmental policy. The Board reports to the President of the Commission and to the Vice-President for Interinstitutional Relations and Foresight, and by design neither seeks nor takes instructions from any Commission department, Member State, or other EU institution when preparing its opinions.

## Why it matters to a policy professional

- **Timing leverage**: a negative RSB opinion is one of the few internal Commission mechanisms that can materially delay a proposal's expected publication date. Trade associations, NGOs and Member State permanent representations that track a dossier's timeline should watch for RSB resubmission cycles, which routinely add weeks or months to an initiative's internal Commission phase.
- **Substantive leverage**: RSB opinions are public once the related proposal is adopted, and they frequently flag exactly the weaknesses (subsidiarity justification, SME impact, quantification gaps, coherence with other legislation) that stakeholders themselves raise during consultation. Citing the Board's own criticisms strengthens an advocacy position because it comes from inside the Commission's own machinery, not an external lobby.
- **Case studies**: the Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive (CSRD) impact assessment received a positive-with-reservations opinion; the Corporate Sustainability Due Diligence Directive (CSDDD) proceeded despite two negative opinions, using the Vice-President override route; both files were later targeted for scope reduction in the 2025 sustainability Omnibus package, with the Commission explicitly citing the RSB's original proportionality and coherence concerns as part of the justification.
- **Not a veto, but a real gate**: because the Board cannot block an initiative outright (the College always retains the final say via the override route on impact assessments, and evaluations/fitness checks can be published regardless of the opinion), it is best understood as a mandatory quality-and-delay mechanism rather than a stop mechanism. Still, in practice a negative first opinion has historically hit somewhere between roughly 30% and almost half of first submissions in a given year, making it a routine and predictable feature of the Commission's internal legislative timeline.

## RSB vs Fit for Future Platform: do not conflate them

These are two different bodies that both sit inside the Commission's Better Regulation architecture but do fundamentally different jobs:

| | Regulatory Scrutiny Board (RSB) | Fit for Future Platform (F4F) |
|---|---|---|
| Scope | Scrutinises the QUALITY of NEW impact assessments, evaluations and fitness checks before adoption | Advised on SIMPLIFYING and modernising EXISTING EU law |
| Nature | Formal Commission body, embedded in the internal decision-making process, opinions can delay or (via override) still reach the College | External high-level expert group (advisory only), no power to block anything |
| Membership | 9 full-time Commission staff/experts appointed by the Commission | Government Group (Member State representatives, Committee of the Regions, EESC) plus a Stakeholder Group (business, civil society, SME envoys, REGHub regional hubs) |
| Status (16 July 2026) | Active, ongoing, mandatory step in the legislative pipeline | Concluded: held its final plenary meeting 17 October 2024, mandate expired 30 November 2024, after adopting 41 opinions and roughly 260 simplification suggestions over 2021-2024. It succeeded the earlier REFIT Platform (2015-2019). No permanent successor platform has been announced; burden-reduction and simplification work since 2024 has continued instead through the Commission's Omnibus packages (see `eu_omnibus_iv_small_midcap`) and the wider Better Regulation and Enforcement Communication agenda |

In short: the RSB is a gatekeeper for tomorrow's proposals; the Fit for Future Platform (while it existed) was an advisory voice on cleaning up yesterday's rulebook. A user asking "does the RSB simplify existing law?" should be told no, that is not its function, and pointed to the Omnibus simplification files and the Better Regulation and Enforcement Communication instead.

## The "one in, one out" approach

The "one in, one out" approach was introduced by the Commission Communication "Better Regulation: Joining forces to make better laws", COM(2021) 219 final of 29 April 2021: https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:52021DC0219

It requires that any new regulatory burden (in particular compliance costs) introduced by a Commission legislative proposal in a given policy area is offset, as far as possible, by removing or reducing an equivalent existing burden in that same area. It complements, rather than replaces, the long-running REFIT (Regulatory Fitness and Performance) programme. Results are tracked through the Commission's Annual Burden Survey, published each year alongside REFIT progress and (formerly) Fit for Future Platform highlights.

The RSB's relationship to "one in, one out" is indirect but important: the Board scrutinises the quality of the cost and benefit estimates within impact assessments, including the burden calculations that feed the one-in-one-out ledger, but it does not itself administer, validate or report on the one-in-one-out balance sheet. Business stakeholders (for example BusinessEurope) have repeatedly called for the Board to be given an explicit, stronger role in validating one-in-one-out cost estimates, but this has not been formalised in the Board's mandate.

## Where the RSB sits in the Better Regulation cycle

1. A Commission department identifies the need for a new initiative or a review of existing legislation.
2. For significant initiatives, a public consultation is run (see `have_your_say_consultations`) alongside the drafting of an impact assessment following the Better Regulation Guidelines and Toolbox.
3. The completed draft impact assessment, evaluation, or fitness check is submitted to the **Regulatory Scrutiny Board**.
4. The Board issues its opinion (positive, positive with reservations, or negative), which may trigger one or more rounds of revision and resubmission.
5. Once cleared (or overridden via the Vice-President route for a doubly-negative impact assessment), the initiative proceeds to inter-service consultation among Commission departments and then to the College of Commissioners for adoption.
6. After adoption, the impact assessment and the RSB's opinion are published together on the Commission's transparency register.

This positions the RSB as the last internal Commission checkpoint before the political decision-making stage, distinct from earlier stages such as the Call for Evidence and public consultation (external, participatory) and later stages such as trilogue negotiation (inter-institutional, after Parliament and Council are involved).
