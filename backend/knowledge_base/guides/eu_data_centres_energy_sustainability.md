## What the law actually requires

The Energy Efficiency Directive obliged the Commission to create a Union database of data centres and to require operators of facilities at or above 500 kW of installed IT power demand to report on their energy performance. Delegated Regulation (EU) 2024/1364 sets out what must be reported and how.

The duty is annual. It covers, among other things, the data centre type, its year of entry into operation, installed IT power demand, computer room floor area, total energy consumption, energy consumption of IT equipment, energy from backup generators, total and potable water input, waste heat reused, and the share of renewable energy.

Two design choices explain most of what follows. First, the **500 kW floor** removes a long tail of smaller facilities and IT rooms from the picture entirely. Second, the scheme relies on operators reporting themselves, with the enforcement left to Member States. The first cycle shows what that produces.

## What the first reporting cycle found

The Commission's first technical report, prepared by EY, Borderstep and AIT for DG Energy and published in July 2025, is the primary source on this. Its central finding is about coverage, not about efficiency:

**770 facilities reported, roughly 36% of the estimated EU total. Six Member States returned no data at all. In five more, fewer than three facilities reported their indicators.** The report warns that coverage measured by capacity or by energy could be lower still, because data centres vary enormously in size and there is no reason to assume the reporting third is representative.

Reported total consumption came to **14.09 TWh**. Independent work puts EU data centre consumption at **45-65 TWh**, and a bottom-up estimate from server sales suggested about **87 TWh** for 2025. These are not competing measurements of the same thing; the first is what was reported, the others are what exists.

The report's recommendations are administrative rather than political: differentiate requirements between colocation, enterprise and co-hosting facilities because operators of some types cannot access all metrics; harmonise standards across Member States; document clearly how to obtain each indicator; add automated plausibility checks and warnings; improve the platform and allow Excel import and export; and keep communication channels open with operators to encourage participation.

## The four indicators, and why no single one is enough

**PUE, power usage effectiveness**, is total facility energy divided by IT equipment energy. A PUE of 1.5 means half a kilowatt-hour of overhead for every kilowatt-hour of computing. The EU average is **1.36**, ranging from 1.15 to 1.66 across Member States. It measures the building and the cooling, not the whole facility's impact.

**WUE, water usage effectiveness**, is cubic metres of water per megawatt-hour of IT energy. The EU value is **0.58 m³/MWh**, ranging from 0.07 to 1.28. Water consumption depends mostly on cooling technology: evaporative systems use a lot, dry coolers use little or none. The indicator does not record what kind of water is used, or how scarce water is where the facility sits — a serious limitation in a Union that contains both Finland and southern Spain.

**ERF, energy reuse factor**, is the share of consumed energy recovered and reused, typically as waste heat for district heating. Values run 0 to 1. The EU figure is **0.205** among facilities that reuse any heat at all, and **0.018 across all data centres**.

**REF, renewable energy factor**, is the renewable share of energy consumption. The EU value is **0.86**.

The report and the researchers behind it stress that these indicators trade off against one another. Improving PUE by moving to water-based cooling worsens WUE. Efficient cooling can make waste heat harder to recover, lowering ERF. A low PUE says nothing about where the energy came from. Any assessment using one indicator alone will mislead.

On the renewable figure specifically: it includes guarantees of origin, and researchers at the Borderstep Institute argue these do not reliably mean new renewable generation was added. They point to power purchase agreements with new wind or solar plants, hourly matching of consumption to actual green generation, and on-site generation as stronger alternatives.

## Where the data centres are

Nearly 2,800 facilities across the EU. Germany leads with 562, France follows with 418, Italy third with 270 — Germany and France together hold more than a third of everything recorded. By city: Paris 180 (about 4,075 MW), Frankfurt 150 (2,821 MW), Dublin 116, Milan 105 (2,019 MW), then Amsterdam 80, Madrid 75, and Berlin, Helsinki and Stockholm at about 50 each. The top four cities alone hold 551 facilities, close to a fifth of the European total.

Facility counts are a poor proxy for scale. Gdańsk has **four** data centres and **3,201 MW** of installed capacity — more than Frankfurt's 150 facilities.

About 78% of the recorded stock is operational. **425 are planned and 163 under construction**, so roughly 600 facilities in the dataset are not yet running.

Installed IT power across Europe grew from **10,539 MW in 2023 to 14,784 MW in 2025**, ahead of earlier forecasts. The industry association's own framing is that growth is "no longer constrained by capital" but by "power availability, grid readiness and permitting".

## Spain and Catalonia

**Spain** has become one of Europe's fastest-moving markets. Madrid capacity is projected to grow from 116 MW in 2022 to roughly **613 MW by the end of 2026**, which would put it alongside Paris. That growth ran into the grid: Madrid's pipeline exceeds guaranteed grid capacity by more than four times. Spain responded with an instrument worth noticing — grid capacity auctions requiring **performance bonds of €40,000 per MW**, with unused allocations expiring if half the capacity is not used within the first period. It is an attempt to price out speculative grid reservations, a problem every congested European hub has.

The consequence was geographic: **19 large projects migrated to Aragón**, where AWS has operated campuses near Zaragoza since 2022 (about 108 MW installed) and Microsoft is building a very large new campus. Announced Aragonese projects exceed **1,800 MW** over the next decade, channelled through regional projects of general interest, and Aragón is forecast to reach about **1,608 MW by 2030** — behind only London and Frankfurt in Europe. Around 28 major projects are in play, including a 300 MW facility proposed at Villamayor. In July 2026 a regional party proposed a moratorium on new data centres in Aragón pending a plan for sustainable energy and water use.

**Catalonia** made its move on **14 April 2026**, when the Govern identified **26 potential data centre projects** and designated **seven implantation poles**, among them Parc de l'Alba, the Anoia and the Ebre. The combined potential is around **2,000 MW**, against roughly **120 MW** installed across the 17 facilities operating today — close to a twentyfold increase. The Government itself notes about ten projects at a high maturity level that could reach 300 to 500 MW progressively between 2030 and 2035.

The energy arithmetic is what makes this contested. At full operation the projected capacity implies **12 to 17.5 TWh a year**; Catalonia's total annual electricity consumption is around **40 TWh**. The upper estimate comes from Ramon López de Mántaras, emeritus research professor at the CSIC. Campaigners put the figure at roughly double the electricity consumption of the city of Barcelona. Catalonia generated only **19% of its electricity from renewables in 2024**, against a 50% target for 2030, and came through a severe drought between 2021 and 2024. A civil-society campaign, backed by a coalition of organisations, opposes the plan and cites the UN special rapporteur on the human right to water, who has called for a global moratorium on megadata centres.

Barcelona is the second Spanish cluster, with a €230 million project at Sant Adrià de Besòs adding about 21 MW in 2026.

## The politics of siting

The clearest European case is **Kronstorf in Upper Austria**, where Google is building the country's first hyperscale facility. Phase one draws about **150 MW**, an expected **1.31 TWh a year**. A planned expansion would reach **500 MW and 4.4 TWh** — more than the roughly 3.4 TWh used by all households in Vienna, a city of two million, and equal to about **7% of Austria's current power demand**.

Austria runs on close to 90% renewable electricity, which is why the site was chosen; but hydropower output fell by up to a third during the 2026 drought. The facility will keep diesel backup generators, which must be test-run regularly.

The regulatory detail that mobilised local opposition: **the data centre did not require a full environmental impact assessment under Austrian law, while a single wind turbine does.** Parties in the governing coalition and in opposition have since proposed subjecting data centres to such assessments.

Resistance is not confined to Austria. Campaigns are active in France, Italy, Ireland and Catalonia. The counter-argument was put plainly by Austria's energy state secretary: the EU cannot call for greater AI sovereignty while obstructing the infrastructure that sovereignty depends on. An Austrian Energy Agency expert proposed the middle path: require operators to contribute to building new clean generation, so that new demand arrives with new supply.

## The funding architecture

The EU is co-funding **seven AI gigafactories**, three large and four smaller. Governments must commit to buying compute from their national project before development starts, and their commitment must match or exceed EU funding.

**Eighteen of twenty-seven governments pledged; nine did not.** Around **€3 billion** was committed in total. Public funding is capped at **35%** of total investment, so industry must supply the rest. Germany committed **€1 billion**; Portugal, Spain, Germany, Italy and Greece around €200 million each for a large site; France, Denmark, Poland and Czechia €100 million for a smaller one; Sweden €50 million; **Lithuania €1 million**. Croatia, Hungary and Lithuania jointly back Poland's bid with €36 million combined. Ireland pledged €10 million to a French-hosted site. The Netherlands declined, citing budget.

Selection is expected in early 2027, construction over the following eighteen months, and most public payments begin in **2028**, spread over five years. The industry association describes the programme as backed by up to €10 billion in EU and national funding, expected to unlock at least €20 billion privately; that is programme ambition rather than money currently pledged, and the two figures should not be conflated.

Other relevant instruments: a **€180 million sovereign cloud tender** awarded to four European providers in April 2026, and **EURO-3C**, a €75 million federated telco-edge-cloud project announced in March 2026.

## What to watch

The **Electrification Action Plan**, adopted 20 July 2026, came with a proposed amendment to the electricity market regulation intended to future-proof electricity bills — the clearest sign that the Commission has registered the price-transfer question that has become politically explosive in the United States. An **EU data centre rating scheme** is expected. The industry runs a voluntary **Heat Reuse Platform** with Euroheat & Power and others, which matters given the 1.8% figure. And the industry's own technical committee has published work on responding to AI demand without overbuilding for demand that is hard to predict.

The second reporting cycle is the thing to watch most closely. If coverage stays near a third, the Union will have a reporting duty in name and an estimate in practice.
