# Pay Transparency Directive (Directive (EU) 2023/970)

## QUICK FACTS
- Instrument: Directive (EU) 2023/970 of the European Parliament and of the Council of 10 May 2023 to strengthen the application of the principle of equal pay for equal work or work of equal value between men and women through pay transparency and enforcement mechanisms
- CELEX: 32023L0970 -- OJ L 132, 17.5.2023
- Legal basis: Article 157(3) TFEU (equal pay for equal work)
- Entry into force: 6/7 June 2023 (20 days after OJ publication)
- **Transposition deadline: 7 June 2026** -- Member States must have national implementing law in force by this date
- Scope: all employers, public and private, in all Member States (some reporting duties are size-staged, see below)
- Key obligation 1 -- pay transparency before employment: job vacancies/titles must be gender-neutral; candidates must be told the initial pay or pay range (in the vacancy notice or before interview); employers cannot ask candidates about their current or past pay
- Key obligation 2 -- worker's right to information: any worker can request, and must receive within 2 months, their individual pay level and the average pay levels (broken down by sex) for categories of workers doing the same work or work of equal value; employers must inform all workers annually of this right; pay-secrecy clauses banned
- Key obligation 3 -- gender pay gap reporting (staged by employer size): 250+ employees report annually from 7 June 2027; 150-249 employees report every three years, first report by 7 June 2027; 100-149 employees report every three years, first report by 7 June 2031; employers under 100 employees are exempt from mandatory reporting (voluntary reporting encouraged)
- Key obligation 4 -- joint pay assessment: triggered where a pay report shows an unjustified average gender pay gap of 5% or more in any category of workers, and the employer has not remedied it within 6 months
- Key obligation 5 -- gender-neutral job evaluation and classification criteria for setting pay and pay progression (skills, effort, responsibility, working conditions)
- Enforcement: burden of proof shifts to the employer in pay-discrimination claims; compensation includes full recovery of back pay plus related bonuses and payments in kind; Member States must set effective, proportionate and dissuasive penalties, including fines; each Member State designates a monitoring body (typically the national equality body)
- EU average gender pay gap: around 12% (Eurostat, most recent data); the directive is one of the flagship EU gender-equality strategy actions

The Pay Transparency Directive is the EU's binding response to a pay gap that has proven resistant to two decades of soft-law recommendations. It converts the abstract Treaty principle of "equal pay for equal work" (Article 157 TFEU) into concrete, auditable duties on employers, with a phased reporting calendar running from 2027 to 2031.

## Why It Exists

Article 157 TFEU has guaranteed equal pay for equal work since the Treaty of Rome, and a 2006 recast directive (2006/54/EC) restated the principle. Yet the EU gender pay gap has stayed stubbornly around 12-13% for years, and enforcement of individual equal-pay claims was hampered by one structural problem: workers usually cannot prove discrimination because they do not know what colleagues of the other sex earn. Directive (EU) 2023/970 addresses that information asymmetry directly -- transparency is the enforcement mechanism, not a side effect of it.

The Commission's 2020-2025 Gender Equality Strategy identified pay transparency as a priority action; the proposal was tabled in March 2021, agreed by co-legislators in December 2022, formally adopted 10 May 2023, and entered into force in June 2023 with a three-year transposition window.

## What the Directive Requires

### 1. Pay transparency before and during recruitment
- Job vacancy notices, or the information given before a job interview, must state the initial pay level or pay range for the post.
- Job titles and recruitment processes must be gender-neutral.
- Employers are prohibited from asking candidates about their current or previous pay.

### 2. Right to information for workers
- Any worker can request information on their own individual pay level and on average pay levels, broken down by sex, for categories of workers doing the same work or work of equal value.
- Employers must respond within two months.
- Employers must inform all workers, annually, that this right exists.
- Contract clauses that prevent workers disclosing their own pay for equal-pay purposes are void (the "pay secrecy" ban).

### 3. Gender pay gap reporting (staged by employer size)
| Employer size | Frequency | First report due |
|---|---|---|
| 250+ employees | Annual | 7 June 2027 |
| 150-249 employees | Every 3 years | 7 June 2027 |
| 100-149 employees | Every 3 years | 7 June 2031 |
| Under 100 employees | Not mandatory | Voluntary |

Reports must break down the pay gap by category of worker and, where relevant, by variable components of pay (bonuses, overtime, benefits in kind).

### 4. Joint pay assessment
If a pay report shows an average pay gap of 5% or more between women and men in any category of workers, and the employer cannot justify the gap with objective, gender-neutral factors, and has not corrected it within six months, the employer must carry out a joint pay assessment in cooperation with workers' representatives, covering the causes of the gap and a remediation plan.

### 5. Gender-neutral job evaluation
Employers must use job evaluation and classification systems based on gender-neutral criteria such as skills, effort, responsibility and working conditions, to ensure comparability of "work of equal value" across roles typically segregated by sex.

### 6. Enforcement and remedies
- Burden of proof shifts to the employer: once a worker shows facts from which discrimination may be presumed, the employer must prove there was no breach of the equal-pay principle.
- Compensation for victims must include full recovery of back pay and related bonuses or payments in kind, plus compensation for related losses.
- Member States must set effective, proportionate and dissuasive penalties, including fines, for infringements (including of the reporting obligation).
- Each Member State designates a monitoring body (often the existing national equality body) to collect data, publish aggregated pay-gap figures and support enforcement.
- Collective redress: Member States must ensure that equality bodies and workers' representatives/organisations can act on behalf of workers in pay-discrimination proceedings.

## Practical Implications

**For employers (250+ employees):** transposition legislation will land in national law across all 27 Member States by 7 June 2026; internal readiness work -- job architecture/classification review, pay data systems capable of sex-disaggregated reporting, recruitment process changes (pay ranges in postings) -- should be under way well before the first reporting deadline of June 2027.

**For mid-sized employers (100-249 employees):** the staged thresholds mean smaller firms have a longer runway (first report not until 2027 or 2031 depending on size), but the individual worker's right to information and the ban on asking about salary history apply from the transposition deadline regardless of size, since those obligations are not threshold-gated.

**For HR, legal and public affairs teams:** national transposition will vary in strictness (this is a minimum-harmonisation directive; Member States may go further), so multinational employers should track each jurisdiction's implementing law rather than assume a single EU-wide standard. Sector social partners (collective bargaining bodies) are also directly engaged in the job-evaluation and joint-assessment provisions.

**For advocacy and social partners:** the directive gives equality bodies and trade unions standing to support or bring collective pay-discrimination claims, and mandates cooperation with workers' representatives on job evaluation criteria and joint pay assessments -- a significant expansion of collective voice in pay-setting compared with the 2006 recast directive.
