# In Vitro Diagnostic Medical Devices Regulation (IVDR)

## QUICK FACTS
- Full name: Regulation (EU) 2017/746 of the European Parliament and of the Council of 5 April 2017 on in vitro diagnostic medical devices
- CELEX: 32017R0746
- Official Journal text: https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32017R0746
- Repeals: Directive 98/79/EC (the old IVD Directive, "IVDD") and Commission Decision 2010/227/EU
- Legal basis: Article 114 TFEU (internal market) and Article 168(4)(c) TFEU (public health)
- Entered into force: 25 May 2017 (20 days after OJ publication)
- Full application (Date of Application): 26 May 2022
- Scope: devices that examine specimens taken from the human body (blood, urine, tissue, saliva, other samples) to provide diagnostic, prognostic or predictive information, or to monitor treatment
- Classic examples: HIV tests, pregnancy tests, blood glucose self-test strips, COVID-19 antigen tests, genetic tests, companion diagnostics, blood-typing and blood-donor screening tests, laboratory instruments and reagents
- Risk classification: four classes, A (lowest) to D (highest), set by rule-based criteria in Annex VIII, not a fixed product list
- Notified Body involvement: roughly 80% of IVDs now need a Notified Body (up from around 20% self-certified devices under the old IVDD list-based system)
- Responsible DG: DG SANTE (Health and Food Safety)
- Responsible Commissioner: Oliver Varhelyi (Health and Animal Welfare)
- Key coordination body: Medical Device Coordination Group (MDCG)
- Sister regulation: Regulation (EU) 2017/745 on medical devices (MDR), covered in Brubru's `medical_devices_regulation` guide. IVDR and MDR share most structural concepts (EUDAMED, UDI, Notified Bodies, MDCG) but IVDR has its own classification system and its own, later transitional timeline
- EUDAMED: European database on medical devices, shared IT backbone for both MDR and IVDR, phased rollout
- UDI: Unique Device Identification, mandatory traceability code on IVD labelling and in EUDAMED
- Transitional deadlines extended twice: Regulation (EU) 2022/112 (January 2022, first staggered extension) and Regulation (EU) 2024/1860 (CELEX 32024R1860, adopted 13 June 2024, published in the Official Journal 9 July 2024) which set the current legacy-device deadlines: Class D devices to 31 December 2027, Class C devices to 31 December 2028, Class B and sterile Class A devices to 31 December 2029
- Notified Body capacity shortfall is the stated reason for both extensions: at Date of Application in May 2022 only a handful of Notified Bodies had been designated under IVDR, against roughly 80 bodies that had certified IVDs under the old directive's largely self-declaration model
- Recent update: Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) 2026/977 (adopted 4 May 2026) harmonises Notified Body quotation and timeline practices under both MDR and IVDR, applying to agreements signed on or after 25 February 2027

## What it is

The In Vitro Diagnostic Medical Devices Regulation, generally referred to by its acronym IVDR, is the EU's legal framework for diagnostic tests performed on samples taken from the human body rather than on the body itself. "In vitro" means "in glass", i.e. outside a living organism: an IVD examines a specimen such as blood, urine, saliva or tissue in a laboratory, on a bedside device, or in a home test kit, and reports back information about a person's health.

The regulation covers an extremely wide range of products: a home pregnancy test, a finger-prick blood glucose strip used by a diabetic patient, a rapid antigen test for COVID-19, a laboratory analyser that screens donated blood for HIV or hepatitis, a genetic test that looks for a hereditary cancer mutation, and a companion diagnostic that determines whether a cancer patient is eligible for a specific targeted therapy. It also covers the reagents, calibrators, control materials, instruments and software used to run these tests in a laboratory setting.

IVDR is directly applicable in all EU Member States (it is a regulation, not a directive requiring national transposition), which is one reason it replaced the old IVD Directive 98/79/EC: the Commission wanted a single, uniformly applied legal text rather than 27 different national implementations.

## Why the IVDR was needed

The previous regime, Directive 98/79/EC (the "IVDD"), classified the large majority of IVDs by a fixed, closed list in its Annex II. Devices not on that list, which was the vast majority of products on the market, could be self-certified by the manufacturer with no third-party (Notified Body) scrutiny at all. Industry estimates put self-certified IVDs at roughly 80 to 90 percent of the market under the IVDD.

Several safety incidents involving poorly validated tests, alongside the broader post-PIP-breast-implant push to tighten the whole medical device regime, led the Commission to redesign the classification system entirely. IVDR replaced the closed list with a rule-based, risk-driven classification (Annex VIII), which inverted the proportions: an estimated 80 percent of IVDs now require Notified Body conformity assessment, and only the lowest-risk Class A general-purpose laboratory products remain self-declared.

## Risk classification: Class A to Class D

Unlike the old IVDD's product list, IVDR classifies devices using seven implementing rules set out in Annex VIII, based on the intended purpose declared by the manufacturer and the risk the device poses both to the individual patient and to public health.

- **Class A (lowest risk):** general laboratory instruments, specimen receptacles, buffer solutions, and other products with no direct diagnostic result attached to a specific disease. Self-declaration by the manufacturer, except sterile Class A devices, which need Notified Body involvement for the sterility aspect only.
- **Class B (low to moderate risk):** devices intended to detect physiological states not linked to a life-threatening or highly contagious condition, for example pregnancy tests, fertility tests, urine test strips, and most home-use tests without a serious clinical consequence if wrong. Notified Body conformity assessment required.
- **Class C (high individual risk and/or moderate public health risk):** devices whose result has a significant impact on patient management, including blood glucose self-monitoring tests, genetic tests, HLA tissue-typing tests, tests for sexually transmitted infections, cancer companion diagnostics, and prenatal screening tests. Companion diagnostics are classified under Annex VIII Rule 3(h) and typically fall in Class C, with a mandatory consultation procedure involving the European Medicines Agency or a national competent authority where the companion diagnostic supports a medicine subject to central or national authorisation. Full Notified Body scrutiny required.
- **Class D (highest risk):** devices whose failure would have a severe impact both on the individual and on public health, above all tests used to detect transmissible agents in blood, tissue, cell or organ donations, such as HIV, hepatitis B and C, and blood-group determination for transfusion compatibility. Mandatory Notified Body assessment plus involvement of an EU reference laboratory, which independently verifies the manufacturer's performance claims before the device can be certified.

Manufacturers self-classify their device against Annex VIII, but Notified Bodies and national competent authorities can and do challenge classification decisions, particularly on companion diagnostics and Class C/D borderline cases. The Medical Device Coordination Group (MDCG) issues non-binding guidance documents (the MDCG 2020-16 series) to harmonise classification practice across Member States.

## Scope and laboratory-developed tests

IVDR applies to commercial IVDs placed on the EU market by manufacturers established anywhere in the world. It also, for the first time in EU law, brings so-called "in-house" devices or laboratory-developed tests (LDTs) into scope. Article 5(5) allows a health institution (typically a hospital laboratory) to manufacture and use a test only within that same institution without a CE mark, but only if it meets a defined set of conditions: a compliant quality management system, justification that no equivalent CE-marked device is available on the market, public transparency about the device's use, and vigilance and incident-reporting obligations equivalent to those for CE-marked devices. This provision reshapes how European hospital laboratories can continue running custom-built genomic, oncology and rare-disease tests that would otherwise fall outside the commercial device market entirely.

## Notified Bodies and the capacity shortage

Conformity assessment for Class B, C and D devices must be carried out by an independent Notified Body designated by a Member State authority and listed in the NANDO database. Because IVDR moved most of the market from self-certification to third-party assessment, demand for Notified Body capacity rose sharply just as the number of bodies actually designated under the new, stricter IVDR designation criteria (Article 47 and Annex VII) remained very low. At the May 2022 Date of Application only a small number of Notified Bodies (in the low double digits) held IVDR designation, a fraction of the roughly 80 bodies that had certified IVDs under the old directive.

This mismatch between demand and Notified Body capacity, combined with warnings from industry (Team-NB, the European association of Notified Bodies) and from manufacturers about the risk of critical diagnostic shortages, led the Council and Parliament to legislate two rounds of transitional relief: Regulation (EU) 2022/112 in January 2022, followed by the more substantial Regulation (EU) 2024/1860 in 2024 (see Transitional deadlines below).

On 4 May 2026 the Commission adopted Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) 2026/977, which harmonises how Notified Bodies quote for and schedule conformity assessment work under both the MDR and the IVDR: it requires documented minimum-information procedures before a Notified Body issues a quotation, and sets timeline and "clock-stop" rules intended to make certification more predictable. These new procedural rules apply to agreements signed by manufacturers and Notified Bodies from 25 February 2027 onward, and are a direct response to years of complaints about opaque and unpredictable Notified Body queues.

## Transitional deadlines: extended to 2027-2029

Legacy devices, meaning IVDs that were already certified under the old IVD Directive 98/79/EC or that were self-declared under it, can continue to be placed on the market beyond IVDR's original May 2022 or May 2025 cut-offs, but only under conditions set by Regulation (EU) 2024/1860 (CELEX 32024R1860), adopted 13 June 2024 and published in the Official Journal on 9 July 2024, which amended both the MDR and the IVDR.

| Device category | Deadline under Regulation (EU) 2024/1860 |
|---|---|
| Legacy devices already certified under the IVD Directive 98/79/EC, and self-declared Class D devices transitioning to IVDR | 31 December 2027 |
| Self-declared Class C devices transitioning to IVDR | 31 December 2028 |
| Self-declared Class B devices and sterile Class A devices transitioning to IVDR | 31 December 2029 |
| Class A devices not requiring Notified Body involvement, and any new device not previously on the market under the IVDD | No extension; full IVDR compliance required from the original Date of Application (26 May 2022) |

To benefit from the extended transitional periods, a manufacturer must meet conditions set in the amended IVDR, including: the device presents no unacceptable risk to health or safety; a compliant quality management system was in place by 26 May 2025; and, for devices needing Notified Body assessment, a formal application was lodged with a Notified Body by 26 May 2025 (Class D and IVDD-certified legacy devices), 26 May 2026 (Class C) or 26 May 2027 (Class B and sterile Class A), followed by a signed written agreement with that Notified Body a few months later. The Commission has published a Questions and Answers document on the practical application of these transitional conditions.

Regulation (EU) 2024/1860 also introduced a gradual, module-by-module mandatory rollout of EUDAMED (rather than an all-at-once "big bang" go-live) and a new obligation on manufacturers to notify competent authorities in advance of an anticipated interruption or discontinuation of supply of a device, where that interruption is likely to cause serious harm or a risk of serious harm to patients or public health.

## EUDAMED

The European Database on Medical Devices (EUDAMED) is the shared IT infrastructure underpinning both the MDR and the IVDR. It has six interlinked modules: actor registration, UDI/device registration, Notified Bodies and certificates, clinical investigations and performance studies, vigilance and post-market surveillance, and market surveillance. For IVDR specifically, the vigilance and market-surveillance modules are particularly important given the higher public-health stakes of Class C and D diagnostic failures (for example, a failed HIV or hepatitis screening test in a blood bank).

EUDAMED's full mandatory go-live has been repeatedly delayed since the MDR and IVDR were first drafted. Regulation (EU) 2024/1860 formalised a staggered, module-by-module mandatory-use approach instead of requiring all modules simultaneously, with market-surveillance and vigilance modules for medical devices generally expected to become mandatory around 28 May 2026 as later modules complete their independent audit.

## UDI (Unique Device Identification)

Every IVD placed on the EU market must carry a Unique Device Identifier, a machine-readable and human-readable code (UDI-DI for the device model, UDI-PI for the specific production batch or lot) that must appear on labelling and be registered in EUDAMED. UDI is designed to allow rapid traceability in the event of a safety recall, an important feature for high-risk Class D diagnostics used in blood and organ screening, where a defective test could compromise the safety of the entire donor supply chain in a given period.

## Relationship to the MDR

IVDR is the sister regulation to Regulation (EU) 2017/745, the Medical Devices Regulation (MDR), covered in Brubru's `medical_devices_regulation` guide. Both regulations were adopted on the same day (5 April 2017) as part of the same legislative package responding to the PIP breast implant scandal and broader calls to tighten EU medical device oversight. They share the same core architecture: EUDAMED, UDI, the Medical Device Coordination Group (MDCG), Notified Body designation criteria, general safety and performance requirements (GSPR), and post-market surveillance obligations. They differ in scope (devices used on the body versus devices used on specimens from the body), in their risk classification systems (MDR uses Class I/IIa/IIb/III based on Annex VIII of the MDR; IVDR uses Class A/B/C/D based on its own Annex VIII), and in their transitional timelines, which were extended by the same amending Regulation (EU) 2024/1860 but to different dates for each regulation.

A joint Commission simplification proposal, procedure 2025/0404(COD), tabled in late 2025, proposes further amendments to both the MDR and the IVDR (alongside Regulation (EU) 2022/123 on EMA's role in expert panels and Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, the AI Act, on Annex I interplay with medical device software). It was presented to the European Parliament's SANT committee on 14 April 2026, with Oliver Schenk (EPP, Germany) as rapporteur. Track this file via its OEIL procedure page for any further changes to IVDR classification or transitional rules.

## Why it matters to a policy professional

IVDR sits at the intersection of internal-market law, public health, and industrial competitiveness. For health policy professionals it is a live case study in how a well-intentioned safety-driven regulatory redesign can create unintended market disruption: the shift from self-certification to mandatory third-party assessment, combined with insufficient Notified Body capacity, created a genuine risk of diagnostic test shortages in the EU, particularly for niche, low-volume, high-value tests such as rare-disease genetic panels and paediatric diagnostics that smaller manufacturers might otherwise withdraw from the EU market rather than pay for full Class C or D certification. The two rounds of deadline extensions (2022 and 2024) and the 2026 Notified Body procedural harmonisation are direct legislative and administrative responses to that risk, and the pending 2025/0404(COD) simplification file is the next stage of that same policy conversation. Anyone tracking EU health, biotech or diagnostics policy needs to understand both the substantive classification rules and this recurring capacity/timeline story.
