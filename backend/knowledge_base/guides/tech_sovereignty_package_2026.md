## WARNING -- PRE-ADOPTION GUIDE
This guide describes a package that has NOT yet been adopted. As of 26 May 2026 it is expected on 3 June 2026,
having slipped three times. Treat every figure below as a Commission ambition or a pre-announcement reported by
trade press, NOT as enacted law. There are no authentic CELEX numbers, no OJ references and no procedure files
for the individual acts yet. When asked "is it adopted?", the answer is NO -- it is a Commission proposal expected
on 3 June 2026, after which it begins the ordinary legislative procedure (CAIDA and Chips Act 2 are expected to be
COD files; binding adoption of CAIDA is targeted only for Q4 2027). Refresh this guide on adoption day.

## What the Tech Sovereignty Package is
The Tech Sovereignty Package is the Commission's flagship attempt to move European digital autonomy from strategy
documents into binding industrial policy. It bundles together cloud infrastructure, semiconductors, energy-system
digitalisation and (tentatively) open-source software. The unifying objective is to reduce the EU's structural
dependence on non-EU technology suppliers -- in particular the three US hyperscalers that control more than 70% of
the EU cloud market, and the foreign semiconductor supply chains on which European industry relies.

It is the operational follow-through to the AI Continent Action Plan and the Apply AI Strategy: those set the
ambition and the money (InvestAI, GigaFactories), the Tech Sovereignty Package sets the rules.

## Component 1 -- Cloud and AI Development Act (CAIDA)
The centrepiece. Expected to:
- Define what legally counts as a "sovereign" cloud (moving sovereignty from voluntary certification toward binding
  criteria).
- Triple the EU's sovereign cloud computing capacity within seven years.
- Introduce "Buy European cloud" rules for the public sector, focused on sensitive workloads (healthcare, finance,
  judicial systems) that would have to be hosted on European cloud capacity.
- Streamline permits for data-centre construction.
- Channel R&D funding into energy-efficient data processing.
- Crowd-in private finance for data centres, drawing on the future European Competitiveness Fund (~EUR 234 billion)
  in the MFF 2028-2034.
Binding adoption of CAIDA is targeted for Q4 2027. The proposal does not ban US providers outright, but it makes the
distinction between a US-controlled cloud running in Frankfurt and a genuinely sovereign one legally meaningful for
the public sector -- the first time the Commission has shifted the sovereignty debate from theoretical risk to a
procurement restriction. The underlying tension (US CLOUD Act extraterritoriality vs GDPR and Schrems II) is
unchanged by CAIDA; what changes is the procurement posture.

## Component 2 -- Chips Act 2
A follow-up to the European Chips Act (Regulation (EU) 2023/1781). The original act set a 20% global-production
target for 2030 but is widely judged to have under-delivered: on current trajectory the EU is projected to hold only
about 11.7% of the global semiconductor value chain by 2030. Chips Act 2 is expected to:
- Grant the Commission a direct investment authority to scale specific fabs -- a step beyond the original act, which
  mainly coordinated national R&D and pilot production lines.
- Refine industrial-policy tools to reduce reliance on foreign suppliers (onshoring design and manufacturing of both
  AI and mature chips).
Commissioner Virkkunen has signalled the revised act will "double down" on the original ambitions. Analysts (e.g.
Bruegel) caution against pursuing self-sufficiency and argue for "indispensability" -- building on European strengths
rather than replicating entire ecosystems. See eu_chips_act for the original 2023 regulation.

## Component 3 -- Strategic Roadmap for Digitalisation and AI in Energy
Led by EVP Teresa Ribera. A roadmap (soft-law / communication, not a regulation) mapping how digital tools and AI are
deployed across the EU energy system -- grids, demand response, data-centre energy demand, efficiency. It is the
energy-sector counterpart to the AI sustainability framework and connects to the data-centre energy-reporting
obligations already in force (Energy Efficiency Directive Article 12). See eu_energy_policy and
ai_sustainability_eu_framework.

## Component 4 -- Open Source Strategy (STATUS UNCERTAIN)
Originally intended to support European software ecosystems, encourage commercially viable open-source projects and
reduce dependence on large US technology firms, strengthening long-term digital autonomy. As of late May 2026 it has
vanished from the College agenda; sources suggest it may reappear as part of a broader European tech-sovereignty
communication rather than as a standalone strategy. Do not state that the Open Source Strategy was adopted on
3 June -- its fate is unresolved.

## Why it keeps slipping
Three delays in three months. No official reason has been given for each, but the reported drivers are:
- Internal Commission concerns over the lack of data justifying some of the sovereignty requirements.
- The EU-US trade negotiations: the US ambassador to the EU publicly warned that "protectionist" tech rules could
  derail the deal, raising the political cost of a hard-sovereignty posture.
- Unresolved scope questions (notably the open-source strategy).

## What advocacy professionals should watch
- The exact definition of "sovereign cloud" and whether sensitive-sector hosting becomes mandatory or recommended.
- Whether Chips Act 2's direct investment authority survives Council scrutiny (Member States guard state-aid and
  budget prerogatives).
- The interaction with the EU-US trade deal -- "Buy European" provisions are the most exposed.
- Whether the Open Source Strategy returns, and in what legal form.
- The financing link to the European Competitiveness Fund in the MFF 2028-2034 negotiations (see fp10_ecf_competitiveness).
