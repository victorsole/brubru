# EU Common Military List

## QUICK FACTS
- Full name: Common Military List of the European Union
- Latest version: Adopted by Council 23 February 2026 (published OJ 13 March 2026, CELEX 52026XG01640)
- Legal basis: Council Common Position 2008/944/CFSP (common rules governing control of exports of military technology and equipment)
- Type: Council political commitment (not legislation), updated annually
- Purpose: Harmonised list of military items subject to export controls across EU Member States
- Structure: 22 categories (ML1-ML22) covering weapons, ammunition, vehicles, aircraft, vessels, electronics, materials, software, technology
- Relationship to Wassenaar Arrangement: EU list aligned with Wassenaar Munitions List
- Lead Council configuration: Foreign Affairs Council (FAC)
- Lead DG: EEAS (European External Action Service) + FPI (Foreign Policy Instruments)

- First adopted: 2000 (replacing earlier national lists)
- Annual update cycle: Council working group (COARM) reviews and adopts updated list, typically in February

- Related guides: safe_rearm_europe, eu_trade_policy

## Overview

The Common Military List of the European Union is the EU's shared reference catalogue of military equipment and technology subject to export controls. It serves as the baseline for all 27 Member States when assessing licence applications for military exports. Although not a regulation or directive in itself, the list is a politically binding annex to Council Common Position 2008/944/CFSP, which establishes common rules governing the control of exports of military technology and equipment.

Every EU Member State is expected to apply its national export control regime using this list as the minimum standard. Member States retain sovereign authority over individual export licensing decisions, but the list ensures a common definition of what constitutes "military technology and equipment" across the single market.

The 2026 update, adopted on 23 February 2026 and published in the Official Journal on 13 March 2026, reflects the latest amendments agreed within the Wassenaar Arrangement and incorporates technical adjustments to keep pace with evolving defence technologies, including unmanned systems, cyber capabilities, and advanced materials.

## Structure: 22 Categories (ML1-ML22)

The list is organised into 22 categories, each covering a distinct class of military goods. The numbering follows the Wassenaar Munitions List structure for maximum international alignment.

| Category | Description |
|----------|-------------|
| ML1 | Smooth-bore weapons with a calibre of less than 20 mm, other arms and automatic weapons with a calibre of 12.7 mm or less, and accessories |
| ML2 | Smooth-bore weapons with a calibre of 20 mm or more, other weapons or armament with a calibre greater than 12.7 mm, projectors and accessories |
| ML3 | Ammunition and fuze setting devices, and specially designed components |
| ML4 | Bombs, torpedoes, rockets, missiles, other explosive devices and charges, and related equipment and accessories |
| ML5 | Fire control, and related alerting and warning equipment, and related systems, test and alignment equipment |
| ML6 | Ground vehicles and components |
| ML7 | Chemical or biological toxic agents, riot control agents, radioactive materials, and related equipment and components |
| ML8 | Energetic materials, and related substances |
| ML9 | Vessels of war, special naval equipment and accessories, and components |
| ML10 | Aircraft, lighter-than-air vehicles, unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs), aero-engines and aircraft equipment |
| ML11 | Electronic equipment not specified elsewhere on the Common Military List, and specially designed components |
| ML12 | High velocity kinetic energy weapon systems and related equipment |
| ML13 | Armoured or protective equipment, constructions and components |
| ML14 | Specialised equipment for military training or for simulating military scenarios |
| ML15 | Imaging or countermeasure equipment, specially designed for military use |
| ML16 | Semi-finished products specifically designed for items specified in the list |
| ML17 | Miscellaneous equipment, materials and libraries, and specially designed components |

Each category contains detailed technical specifications, parameters, and thresholds that determine whether a specific item falls within scope. The notes and definitions at the beginning of the list provide critical interpretive guidance.

## Annual Update Process

The Common Military List is reviewed and updated annually through the following institutional process:

1. **COARM Working Party**: The Council Working Party on Conventional Arms Exports (COARM) is the principal body responsible for maintaining and updating the list. It meets regularly throughout the year in Brussels under the auspices of the EEAS.

2. **Wassenaar Alignment**: Each year, the Wassenaar Arrangement Plenary (held in December) adopts changes to the Munitions List. COARM reviews these changes and incorporates them into the EU list.

3. **Technical Review**: COARM experts from Member State export control authorities propose technical amendments to reflect new defence technologies, clarify ambiguities, or correct drafting issues.

4. **Council Adoption**: The updated list is adopted by the Foreign Affairs Council (FAC), typically in February of the following year.

5. **Official Journal Publication**: The adopted list is published in the Official Journal C series as an informational notice.

The 2026 cycle followed this standard pattern: the Wassenaar Plenary of December 2025 agreed changes, COARM incorporated them in January-February 2026, the FAC adopted the updated list on 23 February 2026, and publication in the OJ followed on 13 March 2026.

## Export Control Framework

The Common Military List does not operate in isolation. It is part of a broader EU export control architecture:

### Council Common Position 2008/944/CFSP

This is the overarching legal framework for EU military export controls. It establishes eight criteria that Member States must apply when assessing export licence applications:

| Criterion | Subject |
|-----------|---------|
| 1 | Respect for international obligations and commitments (UN sanctions, etc.) |
| 2 | Respect for human rights in the country of final destination |
| 3 | Internal situation in the country of final destination (armed conflict, tensions) |
| 4 | Preservation of regional peace, security and stability |
| 5 | National security of Member States and allies |
| 6 | Behaviour of the buyer country with regard to the international community |
| 7 | Risk of diversion of technology or re-export under undesirable conditions |
| 8 | Compatibility of arms exports with the technical and economic capacity of the recipient country |

### Dual-Use Regulation (EU) 2021/821

While the Common Military List covers purely military items, the Dual-Use Regulation controls items that have both civilian and military applications. The two regimes are complementary but legally distinct. The dual-use regime is an EU regulation (directly applicable), while the military list operates through national transposition of the Common Position.

### National Implementation

Each Member State maintains its own national military list and export licensing authority. The EU Common Military List serves as the minimum standard, but Member States may impose additional controls. For example, several Member States control items below the thresholds specified in the EU list.

## Relationship with the Wassenaar Arrangement

The Wassenaar Arrangement on Export Controls for Conventional Arms and Dual-Use Goods and Technologies, established in 1996, is the principal multilateral regime governing conventional arms export controls. Its 42 participating states (including all EU Member States) maintain two control lists:

- **Munitions List**: Military items (basis for the EU Common Military List)
- **List of Dual-Use Goods and Technologies**: Civil-military items (basis for EU Dual-Use Regulation Annex I)

The EU Common Military List is deliberately aligned with the Wassenaar Munitions List to ensure international consistency. However, the EU list occasionally diverges where EU-specific policy considerations apply, such as the inclusion of riot control agents in ML7 (a sensitive category given EU human rights commitments under Criterion 2).

## 2026 Update: Key Changes

The 2026 edition (CELEX 52026XG01640) incorporates the following notable amendments:

- **Unmanned Systems (ML10, ML4)**: Updated definitions and control parameters for unmanned aerial vehicles, unmanned ground vehicles, and autonomous weapons systems, reflecting rapid technological developments in military robotics and AI-enabled platforms.

- **Cyber and Electronic Warfare (ML11, ML21)**: Refined controls on cyber surveillance tools, electronic warfare equipment, and military-grade software, particularly in the context of AI-assisted targeting and command-and-control systems.

- **Advanced Materials (ML8, ML13)**: Updated specifications for energetic materials and composite armour, including new additive manufacturing (3D printing) considerations for defence applications.

- **Space-Related Items (ML11, ML15)**: Clarified controls on military satellite components and space situational awareness equipment, reflecting the growing militarisation of space.

- **Definitions and Notes**: Technical clarifications to the general notes, particularly regarding "specially designed" and "required" thresholds, to reduce ambiguity in licensing decisions.

## Institutional Landscape

| Body | Role |
|------|------|
| COARM (Council Working Party) | Maintains and updates the list; exchanges information on national export decisions |
| EEAS | Provides secretariat support to COARM; coordinates with third countries |
| FPI (Foreign Policy Instruments) | Manages CFSP implementation instruments |
| FAC (Foreign Affairs Council) | Adopts the annual update |
| European Parliament | Consulted but no co-decision role (CFSP is intergovernmental) |
| National export control authorities | Implement controls using the list as minimum standard |
| Wassenaar Arrangement Secretariat (Vienna) | Coordinates multilateral control list updates |

## Significance for EU Defence Policy

The Common Military List takes on additional importance in the context of the EU's rapidly evolving defence policy landscape. With the SAFE instrument mobilising EUR 150 billion for defence investment, the EDIP programme supporting joint procurement, and the European Defence Industrial Strategy (EDIS) promoting industrial consolidation, the volume of intra-EU and extra-EU defence trade is set to increase significantly.

The list ensures that this increased defence trade remains subject to consistent export controls, preventing a "race to the bottom" among Member States competing for defence exports. It also supports the credibility of the EU's arms control commitments in international fora.
