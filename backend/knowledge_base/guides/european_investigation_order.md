## What the EIO does

The European Investigation Order replaced and consolidated the patchwork of pre-Lisbon mutual-legal-assistance (MLA) instruments in criminal matters within the EU (in particular the 2000 EU MLA Convention, the 2003 Freezing Order Framework Decision, and the 2008 European Evidence Warrant). It establishes a **single judicial instrument** for cross-border evidence gathering based on the **principle of mutual recognition**.

The issuing authority (judge, court, investigating judge, prosecutor, or in some cases police authority validated by a judge) of one Member State can issue an EIO requesting a specific investigative measure (witness hearing, document production, search, surveillance, etc.) to be carried out in another Member State. The executing authority **must** execute the EIO under the same procedural conditions as it would for a similar domestic measure, **unless** a refusal ground in Article 11 applies (such as immunity, lack of dual criminality for certain categories, ne bis in idem, fundamental-rights risk, or territoriality limits).

## Key features of the 2014 Directive

1. **Comprehensive scope**: covers virtually all investigative measures (Article 3); excludes only Joint Investigation Teams (JITs) which remain under the 2002 JIT Framework Decision
2. **Tight deadlines**: 30 days for the executing authority to acknowledge + decide on recognition (Article 12); 90 days for execution (extendable on justification)
3. **Mutual recognition default**: refusal grounds are exhaustively listed (Article 11) and narrowly interpreted
4. **Proportionality requirement**: Article 6 — issuing authority must verify the EIO is necessary + proportionate, and that the measure could have been ordered under the same conditions in a similar domestic case
5. **Right to legal remedies**: Article 14 — Member States must ensure legal remedies equivalent to those available in similar domestic cases against the substantive reasons for the EIO
6. **Provisional measures**: Article 32 allows requesting Member State to ask for measures to prevent destruction, transfer or disposal of evidence pending the EIO decision

## State of play (May 2026)

The EIO is the **backbone of EU cross-border criminal investigations** in practice. Eurojust + EJN coordination figures show consistent year-on-year increases in EIO traffic. Persistent implementation gaps flagged by the 10 May 2026 EPRS briefing:
- **Translation delays** in the issuing/executing authorities
- **Inconsistent application of the proportionality test** by issuing authorities
- **Fundamental-rights review** — CJEU jurisprudence (Aranyosi/Căldăraru standard applied by analogy; specific rulings on detention conditions, prosecutor independence, judicial review of EIOs)
- **Digital evidence** — interaction with the **e-Evidence Regulation** (Regulation (EU) 2023/1543 and Directive (EU) 2023/1544) which provides a parallel direct-to-service-provider track for electronic evidence, narrowing EIO use for digital data
- **DK + IE opt-outs / opt-ins** create gaps with non-participating Member States

## Interaction with adjacent instruments

| Instrument | Reference | Role vs EIO |
|---|---|---|
| e-Evidence Regulation | Reg (EU) 2023/1543 (CELEX 32023R1543) | Direct production / preservation orders to service providers; bypasses EIO for digital data |
| e-Evidence Directive | Dir (EU) 2023/1544 (CELEX 32023L1544) | Legal representatives for service providers |
| European Arrest Warrant | Council Framework Decision 2002/584/JHA | Surrender procedure (persons, not evidence) |
| UN Convention against Cybercrime | adopted 24 December 2024, EU consent procedure 2025/0231(NLE) | Global cybercrime cooperation, complementary in third-country dimension |
| Budapest Convention on Cybercrime | CoE CETS 185 | Existing cybercrime treaty; Second Additional Protocol on enhanced cooperation + e-evidence |
| Mutual Legal Assistance Treaties (third countries) | Bilateral / Council Decisions | Used where EIO does not apply (non-EU executing state) |
