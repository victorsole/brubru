# Facilitation Directive Reform (Anti-Smuggling Package)

## QUICK FACTS
- Current law: Council Directive 2002/90/EC (Facilitation Directive) + Council Framework Decision 2002/946/JHA
- Reform proposal: COM(2023)755, 28 November 2023
- Procedure: 2023/0340(COD)
- Package: part of the EU anti-smuggling legislative package (November 2023)
- Companion proposal: Regulation on enhancing police cooperation (Europol role), COM(2023)754
- Key issue: criminalisation of humanitarian assistance (SAR NGOs, civil society)
- Humanitarian exemption: recital 7 (non-binding), civil society demands it be moved to binding article
- Replaces: both Directive 2002/90/EC and Framework Decision 2002/946/JHA
- Responsible DG: DG HOME (Migration and Home Affairs)
- EP lead committee: LIBE (Civil Liberties, Justice and Home Affairs)
- Global Alliance to Counter Migrant Smuggling: launched alongside the package
- Context: EU Pact on Migration and Asylum (adopted 14 May 2024), EU Action Plan against Migrant Smuggling 2021-2025
- Key stakeholders: Amnesty International, SOLIDAR, ECRE (all critical of weak humanitarian exemption)
- EPRS study: comprehensive impact assessment published March 2025

## Overview

The current EU framework criminalising migrant smuggling dates from 2002 and has been widely criticised as outdated, inconsistently applied across Member States, and inadequate to address the scale and profitability of contemporary smuggling networks. The Commission's November 2023 package was the first comprehensive reform in over two decades.

The proposal aims to: harmonise criminal penalties (minimum maximum of 10 years imprisonment for aggravated offences), clarify definitions of facilitation, strengthen Europol's role in cross-border investigations, and introduce minimum standards for national prosecution strategies. However, the most contested element has been the treatment of humanitarian actors.

## Current Framework vs Proposed Regulation

| Element | Directive 2002/90/EC and FD 2002/946/JHA | COM(2023)755 |
|---|---|---|
| Legal instrument | Directive plus Framework Decision (pre-Lisbon architecture) | Single Regulation (directly applicable) |
| Definition of facilitation | Intentional facilitation of unauthorised entry, transit, or residence for financial gain | Retains financial gain; adds "benefit" (broader); includes facilitation of stay |
| Minimum maximum penalty | 8 years (aggravated) | 10 years (aggravated), 15 years if linked to criminal organisation or causes death |
| Humanitarian exemption | Optional: Member States may choose not to criminalise humanitarian assistance | Non-binding recital (recital 7); no mandatory exemption in operative text |
| Europol role | Limited | Enhanced coordination role, joint investigation teams |
| Jurisdiction | Territoriality principle | Extended to vessels and aircraft under Member State flag |
| NGO liability | Ambiguous, applied variably | Ambiguous: civil society organisations warn of continued criminalisation risk |

## The Humanitarian Exemption Controversy

The most debated aspect of the proposal is whether humanitarian assistance should be explicitly and bindingly exempted from criminal liability. The current Directive 2002/90/EC includes an optional exemption clause (Article 1(2)) that Member States may transpose; many have not done so effectively, leading to prosecutions of SAR (search and rescue) NGO crew members and volunteers in Greece, Italy, and France.

COM(2023)755 addresses this only through recital 7, a non-binding preambular statement. Civil society organisations, the UNHCR, and the Council of Europe have all called for a binding Article-level exemption covering:

- Humanitarian organisations providing food, medical care, and shelter
- SAR operations at sea
- Individuals providing informal solidarity assistance

The EP LIBE committee introduced amendments to create a binding exemption in its first-reading position. The Council's general approach has been more restrictive, reflecting pressure from several Member States to avoid any text that could be perceived as facilitating secondary movements.

The debate is closely linked to the broader criminalisation of solidarity discourse and several high-profile prosecutions including the "Lesvos 35" and prosecutions of Alarm Phone volunteers.
