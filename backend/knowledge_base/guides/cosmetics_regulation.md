# Cosmetic Products Regulation (EC) No 1223/2009

## QUICK FACTS
- Full name: Regulation (EC) No 1223/2009 of the European Parliament and of the Council of 30 November 2009 on cosmetic products (recast)
- CELEX: 32009R1223. EUR-Lex: https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32009R1223
- Type: Regulation (directly applicable in all Member States, no national transposition needed)
- Adopted: 30 November 2009. Entered into force: 11 January 2010. Full application (all obligations live): **11 July 2013**
- Repealed and replaced Council Directive 76/768/EEC (the old Cosmetics Directive) as of the full-application date
- No pre-market authorisation regime: products are placed on the market on the responsible person's own liability after **notification** (not approval) via the Cosmetic Products Notification Portal (CPNP), https://webgate.ec.europa.eu/cpnp/
- Core safety actors: the **responsible person** (Art. 4, EU-established, legally accountable), the **Cosmetic Product Safety Report** (CPSR, Art. 10 and Annex I), the **Product Information File** (PIF, Art. 11, kept 10 years), and the **Scientific Committee on Consumer Safety (SCCS)**, which gives the scientific opinions behind ingredient bans and restrictions
- Ingredient control by annex: Annex II = prohibited substances, Annex III = restricted substances, Annex IV = allowed colourants, Annex V = allowed preservatives, Annex VI = allowed UV filters
- Automatic CMR ban mechanism (Art. 15): substances classified carcinogenic, mutagenic or toxic for reproduction (CMR) categories 1A, 1B or 2 under the CLP Regulation are automatically banned in cosmetics unless SCCS grants a narrow derogation
- Animal testing: testing ban on finished cosmetic products since 11 September 2004; testing ban on ingredients and marketing ban for most human-health endpoints since 11 March 2009; full marketing ban (covering repeated-dose toxicity, reproductive toxicity and toxicokinetics data) since **11 March 2013**
- Microplastics: covered not by the Cosmetics Regulation itself but by REACH, via Commission Regulation (EU) 2023/2055 (CELEX 32023R2055), in force 17 October 2023, phasing out intentionally added microplastics in rinse-off and leave-on cosmetics by 2027, lip/nail products by 2029, and colour cosmetics (make-up) by 2035
- Lead DG: DG SANTE (Health and Food Safety). Lead EP committee: ENVI
- **Live file (as of 16 July 2026):** a targeted revision of the Cosmetics Regulation is moving through the "Omnibus VI" chemicals simplification package alongside CLP and the Fertilising Products Regulation. Commission proposal COM(2025) 531 final (8 July 2025); Council general position 5 November 2025; European Parliament joint-committee mandate adopted 15 April 2026 (114-6-7); Council and Parliament reached a **provisional trilogue agreement on 17 June 2026**. Not yet formally adopted or in force as of this guide's date, still requires plenary and Council sign-off plus OJ publication.

Guide for professionals working on cosmetics compliance, chemicals policy, and product safety law in the EU. Covers the safety architecture of Regulation (EC) 1223/2009, the animal-testing framework and its friction with REACH, the SCCS's role, nanomaterials, the CMR automatic-restriction mechanism, microplastics, and the ongoing Omnibus VI revision.

## What the Regulation covers (Article 2)

A "cosmetic product" is any substance or mixture intended for external contact with the skin, hair, nails, lips, external genital organs, teeth or oral mucous membranes, applied wholly or mainly to clean, perfume, change the appearance of, protect, keep in good condition, or correct the odour of the body. The definition is deliberately broad and functional (based on intended use and site of application), not a fixed product list.

The hard edge is the borderline with three neighbouring regimes:
- **Medicinal products** (Directive 2001/83/EC and successors): a product with a genuine therapeutic or pharmacological claim or effect is regulated exclusively as a medicine, never as a cosmetic, even if it looks and is sold like one.
- **Biocidal products** (Regulation (EU) 528/2012): products whose primary function is to destroy or control a harmful organism (e.g. some hand sanitisers, insect-repellent-labelled products) fall under the Biocidal Products Regulation instead.
- **Medical devices** (Regulation (EU) 2017/745): products with a medical intended purpose (e.g. certain dermal fillers) fall under the Medical Devices Regulation.

Classification is case-by-case; the Commission maintains a manual and specific guidance (e.g. on hand sanitisers during COVID-19, on dermal fillers) rather than a bright-line rule. Guidance: https://single-market-economy.ec.europa.eu/sectors/cosmetics/cosmetic-products-specific-topics/borderline-products_en

## The safety framework: no pre-market authorisation, but strict due diligence

Regulation 1223/2009 does not require Commission or Member State approval before a cosmetic product is sold. Instead, it puts the burden entirely on industry through four linked obligations.

**1. Responsible person (Article 4).** Every cosmetic product placed on the EU market must have a designated responsible person established in the EU: the EU manufacturer, the importer (if the manufacturer is outside the EU), or a distributor who becomes responsible person by placing a product under their own name, or a person formally mandated in writing by the manufacturer. The responsible person is legally accountable for the product's compliance, including the CPSR, PIF, notification, and post-market vigilance (Article 23 serious-undesirable-effect reporting).

**2. Cosmetic Product Safety Report (Article 10, Annex I).** Before a product can be placed on the market, a qualified safety assessor must produce a CPSR with two parts:
- **Part A: cosmetic product safety information.** Quantitative and qualitative composition, physical/chemical characteristics and stability, microbiological quality, impurities and traces, packaging material interaction, normal and reasonably foreseeable use, exposure data, toxicological profile of each ingredient (including CMR status), undesirable effects, and other relevant information.
- **Part B: cosmetic product safety assessment.** The assessor's reasoned conclusion on safety, warnings and instructions for use, and the assessor's argumentation and qualifications.

The safety assessor must hold a recognised qualification (a degree in pharmacy, toxicology, medicine or a related discipline) under Article 10(2).

**3. Product Information File (Article 11).** The responsible person must keep a PIF containing the product description, the CPSR, a description of the manufacturing method demonstrating compliance with good manufacturing practice (GMP, referencing the ISO 22716 standard), proof of the effect claimed for the product where justified, and data on any animal testing performed by or on behalf of the manufacturer. The PIF must be kept for **10 years** from the date the last batch of the product was placed on the market, and made readily accessible in electronic or other format to the market surveillance authority of the Member State where it is kept.

**4. CPNP notification (Article 13).** Before placing a product on the market, the responsible person must notify it electronically to the Commission via the Cosmetic Products Notification Portal (https://webgate.ec.europa.eu/cpnp/), submitting the product category, name, responsible-person contact details, country of manufacture/import, presence of nanomaterials, and (where relevant) CMR classification of ingredients. This is a one-off, EU-wide notification, not a Member-State-by-Member-State filing, and critically **it is not a marketing authorisation**: the Commission does not review or approve the file before market entry, it simply becomes visible to Member State authorities and poison centres for market-surveillance and emergency-response purposes.

## Ingredient control: Annexes II-VI

Instead of pre-approving finished products, the Regulation controls the substances that can go into them, through five annexes updated by Commission implementing/delegated regulations on SCCS advice:

| Annex | Content |
|-------|---------|
| **Annex II** | Prohibited substances: banned outright in cosmetic products |
| **Annex III** | Restricted substances: allowed only within specified concentration limits, product types, or with mandatory warnings |
| **Annex IV** | Positive list of allowed colourants |
| **Annex V** | Positive list of allowed preservatives |
| **Annex VI** | Positive list of allowed UV filters |

Colourants, preservatives and UV filters are the three ingredient classes subject to closed "positive lists" (only what is listed may be used, at the concentrations listed); everything else is regulated negatively, through Annexes II and III plus the general safety obligation.

The annexes are amended frequently. Recent examples: Commission Regulation (EU) 2026/78 (published 13 January 2026, applicable from 1 May 2026) added newly CLP-classified CMR substances to Annexes II-V, including perboric acid and its salts (CMR 1B) and silver in massive, powder and nano forms (CMR 2), and restricted hexyl salicylate; earlier 2024-2025 amendments (Commission Regulation (EU) 2024/996, Commission Regulation (EU) 2025/877, Commission Regulation (EU) 2024/858) addressed vitamin A, alpha-arbutin, endocrine disruptors and nanomaterial colloids (gold, platinum, silver, copper). None of these figures should be treated as exhaustive: always check the Commission's own legislation tracker before advising a client on current annex status: https://single-market-economy.ec.europa.eu/sectors/cosmetics/legislation_en

## The CMR automatic-restriction mechanism (Article 15)

Article 15 creates an automatic link between the EU's chemical hazard-classification system and cosmetics law: once a substance is classified as carcinogenic, mutagenic or toxic for reproduction (CMR) category 1A, 1B or 2 under the **CLP Regulation** (Regulation (EC) 1272/2008, see `eu_clp_regulation_classification_labelling`), it is automatically prohibited in cosmetic products, without a separate cosmetics-specific rulemaking step. Two narrow derogation routes exist, both requiring a positive SCCS opinion: Article 15(1) for CMR 1A/1B substances meeting strict food-safety-equivalent conditions, and Article 15(2) for exceptional circumstances where no safe alternative exists and use is justified by an overriding societal need. This is why a CLP reclassification decision (see `eu_clp_regulation_classification_labelling` and `reach_chemicals_regulation`) routinely triggers a follow-on Annex II/III cosmetics amendment within months.

The Omnibus VI proposal (below) would change the derogation-request deadline and clarify that CMR classifications based solely on the oral or inhalation route (irrelevant to dermal cosmetic use) do not automatically trigger a ban; MEPs pushed back on parts of this streamlining in their April 2026 negotiating mandate, favouring a longer (six-month, not three-month) derogation-request window with extended transitional periods rather than a broader exclusion of CMR substances from the automatic ban.

## The animal-testing ban and the REACH tension

Article 18 of the Regulation imposes both a **testing ban** (no cosmetic ingredient or finished product may be tested on animals in the EU for cosmetics purposes) and a **marketing ban** (no product or ingredient tested on animals, anywhere in the world, for cosmetics purposes may be sold in the EU). The two bans phased in separately by endpoint:
- Testing ban on **finished cosmetic products**: in force since 11 September 2004.
- Testing ban on **ingredients**, and marketing ban for **most human-health endpoints**: in force since 11 March 2009.
- Full marketing ban, extended to cover the hardest-to-replace endpoints (**repeated-dose toxicity, reproductive toxicity, toxicokinetics**): in force since **11 March 2013**, regardless of whether validated non-animal alternative methods existed for those endpoints at the time.

The unresolved friction is with **REACH** (Regulation (EC) 1907/2006, see `reach_chemicals_regulation`), which can still require vertebrate-animal testing data from ECHA for the same substance if it is also registered as an industrial chemical, even where its only actual use is as a cosmetic ingredient. This came to a head in the **Symrise case**: ECHA required Symrise to submit animal-test data on two UV filters (homosalate and ethylhexyl salicylate) used exclusively in cosmetics, under REACH registration duties. The **Court of Justice (Grand Chamber, Case C-592/18, 2020)** and a follow-on **General Court ruling (November 2023)** both held that neither Regulation takes automatic legal primacy over the other, meaning REACH testing duties can still require animal data even for cosmetics-only ingredients. Animal-welfare groups criticised the outcome as leaving the cosmetics testing ban only partially effective in practice.

Public pressure culminated in the **European Citizens' Initiative "Save Cruelty-Free Cosmetics: Commit to a Europe Without Animal Testing"**, submitted 25 January 2023 with 1,217,916 validated signatures across a record 21 Member States. The Commission's formal reply (25 July 2023) committed to continued enforcement of the existing ban, consideration of legislative clarification of the REACH/cosmetics interface pending the Symrise litigation outcome, and a roadmap to phase out animal testing more broadly in chemical safety assessment. That roadmap materialised on **1 June 2026** as the Commission's "Roadmap towards phasing out animal testing for chemical safety assessments": 22 actions across three pillars covering 15 regulatory domains (industrial and consumer chemicals, pesticides, biocides, pharmaceuticals, food and feed additives), with a stocktaking conference planned for 2029. Press release: https://ec.europa.eu/commission/presscorner/detail/en/ip_26_1214. Commission roadmap page: https://single-market-economy.ec.europa.eu/sectors/chemicals/reach/roadmap-towards-phasing-out-animal-testing_en

## The SCCS: the scientific engine behind ingredient bans

The **Scientific Committee on Consumer Safety (SCCS)** is the independent expert body that assesses the safety of cosmetic ingredients (and other non-food consumer products such as toys, textiles, and tattoo inks) and produces the Opinions and Notes of Guidance that underpin every Annex II-VI amendment. It reports to **DG SANTE**. As of mid-2026 the SCCS remains a distinct committee from **SCHEER** (the Scientific Committee on Health, Environmental and Emerging Risks, see `scheer_scientific_committees`), coordinated with it and with SCHEER's counterpart committees through an Inter-Committee Coordination Group; there is no evidence of a merger or rename. Official page: https://health.ec.europa.eu/scientific-committees/scientific-committee-consumer-safety-sccs_en

Under the Omnibus VI proposal, a new fast-track ingredient approval procedure (proposed Article 14a) would give the SCCS a binding 12-month deadline to deliver its opinion on new colourant, preservative and UV-filter applications, aiming to speed up the current multi-year approval backlog for new UV filters in particular, an issue that has drawn sustained industry criticism relative to jurisdictions like the US, Japan and South Korea.

## Nanomaterials (Article 16)

Article 16 requires manufacturers to notify the Commission electronically **six months before** placing a product containing a nanomaterial on the market (a distinct, additional notification module within the CPNP, on top of the standard Article 13 notification), giving the Commission and SCCS time to request a safety opinion if concerns arise. The Commission also maintains and periodically updates a public Catalogue of Nanomaterials Used in Cosmetic Products. A wave of Annex II additions effective from 1 February 2025 (with a compliance deadline of 1 November 2025 for existing stock) prohibited several nano ingredients, including certain nano-gold, nano-platinum and styrene/acrylates-copolymer nanoforms, following adverse SCCS evaluations.

The Omnibus VI proposal would delete the standalone six-month pre-notification requirement and instead fold nanomaterial safety data directly into the CPSR. As of 16 July 2026 this change is only provisionally agreed at trilogue level and not yet in force: the six-month pre-notification obligation remains current law.

## Microplastics: regulated through REACH, not the Cosmetics Regulation itself

Rinse-off exfoliants and glitter-containing cosmetics were a headline target of the EU's intentionally-added-microplastics restriction, but the legal instrument sits in REACH, not in Regulation 1223/2009: **Commission Regulation (EU) 2023/2055** (CELEX 32023R2055) added entry 78 to REACH Annex XVII, entering into force 17 October 2023. It bans loose plastic glitter and rinse-off exfoliating microbeads immediately, and phases out other uses on a staggered timetable: **rinse-off cosmetics** (shampoo, body wash, face scrub, toothpaste) and **leave-on cosmetics** (moisturiser, serum, sunscreen, foundation) must comply by **17 October 2027**; **lip and nail products** by **17 October 2029**; and **colour cosmetics (make-up)** by **2035**. Products sold during the transition periods must carry a "contains microplastics" label. See `reach_chemicals_regulation` for the full REACH restriction context.

This sits within the Commission's broader shift under the **Chemicals Strategy for Sustainability** toward a **generic risk-management approach**: banning entire hazard-based groups of substances (CMRs, endocrine disruptors, persistent/bioaccumulative/toxic substances) by classification rather than assessing each one individually on a case-by-case exposure basis. Critics and industry commentators note this hazard-based "grouping" logic sits in some tension with the Cosmetics Regulation's traditional exposure-based risk-assessment model (the CPSR framework described above), and it is visible in practice in the recent Article 15 CMR annex amendments, which increasingly ban whole substance families (e.g. entire classes of silver salts or perboric-acid compounds) in a single implementing act rather than one named substance at a time.

## The Omnibus VI targeted revision

The Commission opened a full evaluation of the Cosmetics Regulation running mid-2025 to mid-2026, feeding into the **Sixth Simplification Omnibus (Omnibus VI)**, a joint package amending CLP, the Cosmetics Regulation, and the Fertilising Products Regulation together as part of the wider July 2025 Chemicals Action Plan. The Commission's proposal, COM(2025) 531 final of 8 July 2025 (https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:52025PC0531), covers the fast-track ingredient approval procedure, the streamlined CMR derogation process under Article 15, folding nanomaterial data into the CPSR instead of separate pre-notification, and removing some duplicative Member State reporting obligations.

Legislative progress: the Council agreed its general position on 5 November 2025; the European Parliament's joint responsible committee adopted its negotiating mandate on 15 April 2026 by 114 votes to 6 with 7 abstentions, notably rejecting parts of the Commission's proposed CMR-timeline extensions in favour of longer but narrower transitional periods; Council and Parliament reached a **provisional political agreement at trilogue level on 17 June 2026**. As of 16 July 2026 this deal is not yet formally adopted: it still requires European Parliament plenary and Council formal adoption, followed by Official Journal publication, before it becomes law. Track ongoing status via the European Parliament Legislative Train: https://www.europarl.europa.eu/legislative-train/theme-a-new-plan-for-europe-s-sustainable-prosperity-and-competitiveness/file-omnibus-vi-chemicals
