# European Peace Facility: the EU's off-budget military and defence financing tool

## QUICK FACTS
- **Legal instrument:** Council Decision (CFSP) 2021/509 of 22 March 2021 establishing a European Peace Facility (CELEX 32021D0509), adopted by the Council under Articles 28(1), 30(1), 41(2) and 42(4) TEU.
- **Off-budget, not on-budget:** the EPF sits entirely OUTSIDE the EU's Multiannual Financial Framework (MFF). It is financed by direct annual Member State contributions, not by the EU general budget. This is a direct consequence of Article 41(2) TEU, which expressly bars charging "expenditure arising from operations having military or defence implications" to the Union budget.
- **Financial ceiling:** the original 2021-2027 ceiling was EUR 5.692 billion (current prices). It has been topped up repeatedly: March 2023 (to close to EUR 7.98 billion), June 2023 (a further EUR 4.06 billion), and March 2024 (a further EUR 5 billion, ringfenced as the dedicated Ukraine Assistance Fund). Cumulatively this brought the ceiling to over EUR 17 billion for 2021-2027, the figure still cited as current in April 2025 sources. HEDGE: Brubru has not been able to confirm any top-up beyond that April 2025 point; verify against the Council's EPF timeline page or a fresh press search before quoting a higher figure or asserting no further top-up has occurred.
- **Two pillars:** (1) Operations, covering common costs of CSDP military missions and operations; (2) Assistance measures, covering support (training, equipment, including lethal equipment) to third states, regional organisations and international organisations, plus support to partner-led peace-support operations (African Union, UN).
- **Contribution key:** Member States contribute according to the Gross National Income (GNI) scale, under Article 41(2) TEU, the same distribution logic used for other CFSP costs charged to Member States rather than the Union budget.
- **Largest single use:** Ukraine Assistance Measures, funding both lethal and non-lethal military equipment, training and reimbursement of Member State in-kind contributions to Ukraine's armed forces since February 2022. As of March 2024, roughly EUR 11.1 billion of cumulative EPF support had been committed to Ukraine; the dedicated Ukraine Assistance Fund (established March 2024, EUR 5 billion) sits inside the EPF specifically for this purpose.
- **Governance:** the Council decides by unanimity in the Foreign Affairs Council (FAC) on the political framework and individual assistance measures; day-to-day financial administration runs through the Facility Committee (one representative per Member State, chaired by the rotating Council Presidency, unanimity on substantive financial decisions, simple majority on procedural matters). The High Representative/Vice-President (HR/VP, currently Kaja Kallas) proposes assistance measures; the EEAS (via its Crisis Management and Planning Directorate and Military Staff) provides operational planning support; the Commission's Service for Foreign Policy Instruments (FPI) administers disbursement of assistance measures.
- **Contrast with on-budget defence spending:** the European Defence Fund (EDF), the European Defence Industry Programme (EDIP) and SAFE (Security Action for Europe) are ON-BUDGET or EU-borrowing-backed instruments funding defence-industrial capacity, R&D and joint procurement. They are legally distinct from the EPF, which funds operational/CFSP expenditure (missions, assistance measures), not industrial capacity-building. Do not conflate the two spending tracks when answering questions about "EU defence money."

## What the EPF is, and why it sits off-budget

The European Peace Facility was established by Council Decision (CFSP) 2021/509 of 22 March 2021. It is the EU's dedicated financing tool for Common Foreign and Security Policy (CFSP) actions that have military or defence implications, precisely the category of spending that Article 41(2) TEU says CANNOT be charged to the general EU budget.

Before the EPF, the EU used a patchwork of narrower mechanisms, notably the Athena mechanism (which funded only the common costs of CSDP military operations) and the African Peace Facility (funding African Union-led peace-support operations out of the European Development Fund). The EPF consolidated and substantially expanded these precedents into a single, standing, off-budget facility with a multiannual financial ceiling, run by unanimous Council decision-making rather than the ordinary EU budgetary procedure.

Because it is off-budget, the EPF has no European Parliament budgetary co-decision role (Parliament is briefed and can raise political scrutiny questions but does not vote on the EPF budget the way it does on the MFF), and disbursement decisions are Council instruments (CFSP Decisions), not EU regulations subject to the ordinary legislative procedure.

## The two pillars

**Pillar 1: Operations.** The EPF finances the common costs of CSDP military missions and operations, the costs that would otherwise fall to individual troop-contributing Member States (headquarters running costs, infrastructure, medical services, certain transport and lodging costs). This is the direct successor function of the pre-EPF Athena mechanism.

**Pillar 2: Assistance measures.** The EPF funds support to the armed forces of partner (third) states, to regional organisations, and to international organisations, covering training, equipment (including, since the EPF's creation, lethal equipment, a genuine departure from the EU's pre-2021 practice of funding only non-lethal support), infrastructure and medical support. This pillar also funds EU support to peace-support operations led by partners, principally the African Union.

Each assistance measure is adopted by a separate Council Decision (CFSP), proposed by the HR/VP (or a Member State), requiring unanimity in the Council. Individual measures name the beneficiary, the ceiling amount, the type of support authorised, and the implementing arrangements (bilateral in-kind delivery by Member States with EPF reimbursement, or centralised procurement).

## Ukraine Assistance Measures and the Ukraine Assistance Fund

Ukraine is by a wide margin the largest beneficiary of EPF assistance measures. Since Russia's full-scale invasion in February 2022, successive Council Decisions have authorised EPF-funded support for the Armed Forces of Ukraine, covering lethal equipment (ammunition, air defence systems), non-lethal equipment, training (including via the EU Military Assistance Mission, EUMAM Ukraine, established in 2022), and reimbursement of Member States' in-kind military deliveries.

In March 2024, recognising that Ukraine's needs were outstripping the EPF's general assistance-measure envelope, the Council created a dedicated Ukraine Assistance Fund inside the EPF, ring-fencing an additional EUR 5 billion specifically for Ukraine and enabling new financing mechanisms, including support for initiatives such as the Czech-led ammunition procurement initiative sourcing shells from outside the EU. As of March 2024, cumulative EPF commitments to Ukraine stood at roughly EUR 11.1 billion; this figure has continued to rise with subsequent tranches and should be re-verified against the Council's EPF page or FPI's factsheet before being quoted in any Brubru output.

Separately from the EPF, Ukraine also receives EU support via the Ukraine Facility (an on-budget MFF instrument for macro-financial, budgetary and reconstruction support, not military) and the Ukraine Loan Cooperation Mechanism (financed by windfall profits on immobilised Russian sovereign assets). Brubru must never conflate these three distinct funding tracks: EPF assistance measures (military, off-budget), the Ukraine Facility (economic/reconstruction, on-budget), and the Ukraine Loan Cooperation Mechanism (windfall-profit-backed loan, its own legal basis). See `ukraine_support_loan_recovery_2026` for the loan/windfall-profit track.

## Governance

- **Council of the EU (Foreign Affairs Council):** sets the EPF's strategic priorities and adopts, by unanimity, both the overarching EPF Decision and every individual assistance measure.
- **HR/VP (currently Kaja Kallas):** proposes assistance measures and the annual budget; chairs Political and Security Committee discussions feeding into Council decisions. See `eeas_institution_hrvp_kallas`.
- **Facility Committee:** the EPF's dedicated financial-governance body, one representative per Member State, chaired by the country holding the rotating Council Presidency. It adopts the annual budget, approves accounts, appoints external auditors, and sets implementing rules. Substantive financial decisions require unanimity; procedural decisions can pass by simple majority.
- **EEAS:** provides strategic and military planning support via its Crisis Management and Planning Directorate, Civilian Planning and Conduct Capability, and EU Military Staff; day-to-day operational management of missions that draw on EPF Pillar 1 funding.
- **Commission's Service for Foreign Policy Instruments (FPI):** administers the disbursement of assistance measures on the Council's behalf, acting as the financial implementing arm even though the Commission has no decision-making role over the EPF itself.
- **European Parliament:** briefed regularly (notably via AFET and SEDE committees) and can adopt resolutions and ask parliamentary questions, but has no budgetary co-decision power over the EPF, unlike its role over the MFF-funded European Defence Fund.

## Contribution key

Member States fund the EPF directly, in accordance with the Gross National Income (GNI) scale set out under Article 41(2) TEU, the same logic used for other CFSP operational costs kept outside the general budget. Unlike MFF own-resources contributions (customs duties, VAT-based resource, GNI-based resource, plastics own resource), EPF contributions are a standalone annual assessment tied purely to each Member State's share of total EU GNI, paid directly to the Facility rather than routed through the EU budget.

Denmark has historically had an opt-out from EU defence-related CFSP matters (Article 5 of Protocol No 22 TEU/TFEU) and does not participate in operations with defence implications, though its position on EPF assistance measures specifically should be checked against the latest Danish opt-out review, since Denmark's 2022 referendum abolished its CSDP-operations opt-out but questions can still arise on financing mechanics.

## Contrast with on-budget defence instruments

The EPF must never be confused with the EU's ON-BUDGET, MFF-anchored defence-industrial instruments:

- **European Defence Fund (EDF):** an MFF-funded (Multiannual Financial Framework 2021-2027) instrument financing collaborative defence research and capability development among Member State industries. Ordinary legislative procedure, Parliament co-decides. See `european_defence_fund_edf` (planned guide).
- **European Defence Industry Programme (EDIP):** the Commission's proposal (and, once adopted, regulation) to scale up joint defence procurement and industrial capacity, also on-budget/EU-instrument based, distinct from EPF's operational/military-assistance function.
- **SAFE (Security Action for Europe):** the EU's defence-industrial loan instrument enabling Member States to borrow via the EU for joint defence procurement, again an on-budget/EU-borrowing mechanism, not a CFSP operational-expenditure tool. See `safe_rearm_europe`.

The clean dividing line for Brubru answers: if the question is about funding a mission, training a partner's armed forces, or sending equipment to Ukraine's military, the answer is the EPF (off-budget, CFSP). If the question is about funding joint EU defence industry, research or procurement capacity, the answer is EDF/EDIP/SAFE (on-budget or EU-borrowing, ordinary legislative procedure applies to at least the enabling regulations).
