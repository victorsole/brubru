# Anti-Trafficking Directive Recast (Directive (EU) 2024/1712)

## QUICK FACTS
- Instrument: Directive (EU) 2024/1712 of the European Parliament and of the Council, amending Directive 2011/36/EU on preventing and combating trafficking in human beings and protecting its victims
- Adopted: 13 June 2024. Published in the Official Journal: 24 June 2024 (OJ L, 2024/1712). CELEX: 32024L1712
- Entry into force: 14 July 2024 (twentieth day following publication)
- Transposition deadline: 15 July 2026 (Member States have until this date to bring national law into compliance)
- Legal base: amends, not replaces, the 2011 Anti-Trafficking Directive (Directive 2011/36/EU); the 2011 directive's core structure (definitions, jurisdiction, victim protection baseline) remains, with targeted insertions and revisions
- New exploitation forms added to the definition of trafficking: forced marriage, illegal adoption, and exploitation through surrogacy, alongside the existing list (sexual exploitation, forced labour or services including begging, slavery/servitude, criminal activities, removal of organs)
- New demand-side offence: knowingly using services provided by a person the user knows to be a trafficking victim is now itself a criminal offence, punishable with effective, proportionate and dissuasive penalties
- ICT/online trafficking: use of information and communication technologies to recruit, transport, transfer, harbour or receive victims, or to disseminate material of the exploitation (including online sexual exploitation), is an aggravating circumstance carrying higher penalties
- Corporate liability strengthened: legal entities face enhanced sanctions including exclusion from public tenders, grants, concessions and licences, and withdrawal of authorisations to conduct business
- Victim protection: expanded non-punishment principle so victims are not penalised for administrative offences linked to their exploitation (e.g. related to prostitution, begging, loitering, undeclared work) committed as a direct consequence of being trafficked
- Governance: Member States must designate a national anti-trafficking coordinator (or equivalent mechanism), adopt/update national action plans, and strengthen national referral mechanisms; annual data collection on trafficking is reinforced EU-wide
- Sits alongside the EU Strategy on Combatting Trafficking in Human Beings (2021-2025), EMPACT THB (the EU's operational platform against organised crime, including a trafficking priority), and the work of Europol and Eurojust on cross-border trafficking investigations
- Distinct from but adjacent to the Facilitation Package on migrant smuggling and the Victims' Rights Directive framework, which cover overlapping but legally separate conduct

## Why It Matters

Trafficking in human beings remains one of the most profitable forms of organised crime affecting the EU, spanning sexual exploitation, forced labour, forced criminality and, now explicitly, forced marriage, illegal adoption and surrogacy-based exploitation. Directive (EU) 2024/1712 is the first substantive amendment to the EU's 2011 anti-trafficking framework. It responds to two gaps identified by the Commission and Europol: traffickers increasingly use online platforms and encrypted communications to recruit and exploit victims, and demand-side conduct (knowingly benefiting from a trafficking victim's services) had no dedicated EU-wide criminal offence. For compliance, justice, home affairs and platform-liability stakeholders, this recast changes both the substantive criminal law baseline Member States must transpose and the institutional architecture (coordinators, action plans, data collection) national authorities must stand up by 15 July 2026.

## What Changed From the 2011 Directive

### Expanded definition of exploitation

The original 2011 directive's list of exploitation purposes (sexual exploitation, forced labour or services including begging, slavery or practices similar to slavery, servitude, exploitation of criminal activities, and removal of organs) is extended to explicitly cover:

- **Forced marriage**: a person coerced or deceived into marriage as a trafficking outcome
- **Illegal adoption**: trafficking for the purpose of illegal adoption of children
- **Surrogacy exploitation**: exploitation of a person through a surrogacy arrangement

These additions close a long-standing gap: several Member States' national laws did not clearly capture these conduct types as trafficking, complicating cross-border prosecution and victim identification.

### The demand-side offence

For the first time, the EU framework requires Member States to criminalise the **knowing use of services provided by a trafficking victim**. This targets the demand side of trafficking economies (for example, a business knowingly employing a trafficked worker, or an individual knowingly using services from a victim of sexual exploitation), not only the trafficking networks themselves. Penalties must be effective, proportionate and dissuasive; the directive leaves the precise sanction levels to national transposition.

### Technology-facilitated trafficking

The directive recognises that recruitment, control and exploitation of victims increasingly happen through digital channels: social media grooming, online advertisement of victims, live-streamed exploitation, and encrypted coordination among trafficking networks. Use of information and communication technologies in the commission of the offence is treated as an **aggravating circumstance**, and dissemination of material depicting a victim's exploitation online can trigger separate, heavier liability. This aligns the anti-trafficking framework with the online-harms logic already present in the Child Sexual Abuse Material framework and the Digital Services Act's illegal-content provisions.

### Corporate liability and penalties

Legal entities that benefit from or are complicit in trafficking face a wider range of sanctions: exclusion from public tenders, grants, concessions and licences, and withdrawal of authorisations to conduct business, in addition to fines. This mirrors the enforcement logic seen in EU corporate sustainability and forced-labour instruments, tightening the link between public procurement eligibility and trafficking compliance.

### Victim support and the non-punishment principle

The directive reinforces the principle that victims should not be prosecuted or penalised for administrative or criminal offences they were compelled to commit as a direct consequence of being trafficked (for example, offences connected to prostitution, begging, loitering or undeclared work). This is intended to reduce victims' reluctance to come forward and cooperate with investigations.

### Institutional architecture

Member States must designate a **national anti-trafficking coordinator** or equivalent body responsible for coordinating national action, adopt or update **national action plans**, and strengthen **national referral mechanisms** for identifying and assisting victims. **Annual data collection** on trafficking (numbers of identified victims, prosecutions, convictions, disaggregated by exploitation type and demographic) is reinforced at EU level to feed into Eurostat and Commission reporting.

## How This Fits the Wider EU Anti-Trafficking Architecture

- **EU Strategy on Combatting Trafficking in Human Beings (2021-2025)**: the policy umbrella under which this legislative recast sits, alongside prevention, victim protection and law enforcement cooperation strands
- **EU Anti-Trafficking Coordinator**: the Commission-level coordinator responsible for coherence across EU institutions, agencies, Member States and international partners; national coordinators created by the recast report into this wider EU-level coordination structure
- **EMPACT THB**: the EU's operational platform (European Multidisciplinary Platform Against Criminal Threats) with a dedicated trafficking-in-human-beings priority, coordinating joint operations, information exchange and capacity building
- **Europol and Eurojust**: operational and judicial cooperation bodies supporting cross-border trafficking investigations and prosecutions; the recast's stronger criminalisation gives both agencies a clearer legal basis for cross-border case-building
- **Facilitation Directive / migrant smuggling framework**: a legally distinct but often overlapping area, since smuggling networks and trafficking networks can share routes, intermediaries and victims
- **Victims' Rights Directive**: provides the horizontal victim-support baseline (information, protection, compensation) that trafficking-specific victim provisions build on top of

## Key Terms

- **Trafficking in human beings**: the recruitment, transportation, transfer, harbouring or receipt of persons, by means of threat, force, coercion, deception or abuse of a position of vulnerability, for the purpose of exploitation
- **Demand-side offence**: criminalisation of knowingly using services provided by a trafficking victim, independent of criminalising the trafficker
- **Non-punishment principle**: the rule that victims should not be prosecuted for unlawful acts they were compelled to commit as a direct consequence of being trafficked
- **EMPACT**: European Multidisciplinary Platform Against Criminal Threats, the EU's operational framework for tackling organised and serious international crime, including trafficking in human beings
- **National anti-trafficking coordinator**: the national body or official designated under the recast to coordinate a Member State's anti-trafficking policy and report into the EU-level coordination structure
