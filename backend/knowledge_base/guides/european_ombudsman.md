# European Ombudsman: maladministration, strategic inquiries, complaint procedure

## Overview

The European Ombudsman is an independent institution created by the Maastricht Treaty (1992) and operational since 1995. It investigates complaints of maladministration in the activities of EU institutions, bodies, offices, and agencies, with the sole exclusion of the Court of Justice acting in its judicial capacity.

The Ombudsman is elected by the European Parliament for the duration of each parliamentary term (5 years), renewable once. Teresa Anjinho (Portugal) was elected on 17 December 2024, succeeding Emily O'Reilly who held the role from 2013 to 2024.

The Ombudsman cannot impose binding decisions on institutions, but its recommendations carry significant political weight. A refusal to follow a recommendation can trigger a special report to the EP, which can apply political pressure. In practice, EU institutions comply with the great majority of the Ombudsman's findings.

The institution is entirely free to use and requires no legal representation.

## Legal Basis and Statute

### Article 228 TFEU
Article 228 TFEU establishes the Ombudsman, grants it the right to receive complaints, conduct inquiries, and refer cases to the EP where an institution fails to follow its recommendations.

### Regulation (EU, Euratom) 2021/1163 (the Statute)
Regulation 2021/1163, adopted on 28 June 2021, is the current Statute of the Ombudsman. It replaced Decision 94/262/ECSC, EC, Euratom. Key provisions:

- **Article 1:** the Ombudsman is elected by the EP, is completely independent, does not accept instructions from any government or institution
- **Article 2:** persons entitled to complain; scope of mandate; 3-year time limit from the date of becoming aware of the facts
- **Article 3:** the inquiry process; institution's right of reply; accessibility of conclusions to the complainant
- **Article 4:** duty of institutions to cooperate; access to classified documents
- **Article 7:** relations with EP; obligation to submit an annual report; possibility of a special report when an institution fails to act on a recommendation
- **Article 9:** the Ombudsman may not be dismissed except by the CJEU at the EP's request

The Charter of Fundamental Rights (Article 43) also codifies the right to refer to the Ombudsman.

## Mandate Scope

### What the Ombudsman CAN investigate
- European Commission (DGs, Cabinets, Agencies supervised by Commission)
- European Parliament (administration, EPRS, services -- not political activity of MEPs)
- Council of the EU (Secretariat -- not political decisions of Member States)
- European Central Bank (administrative acts -- not monetary policy decisions)
- European External Action Service (EEAS)
- EU decentralised agencies (EMA, EFSA, ECHA, Frontex, etc.)
- European Investment Bank (administrative acts)
- Other EU bodies and offices

### What the Ombudsman CANNOT investigate
- Courts of the EU (CJEU, General Court) in their judicial role
- National, regional, or local administrations, even when applying EU law -- those fall under national ombudsmen
- The European Parliament acting in its legislative capacity
- The European Court of Auditors in its audit function (though the ECA and the Ombudsman cooperate on access-to-documents issues)

### Definition of maladministration (Ombudsman's operational definition)
The Ombudsman has identified the following as examples of maladministration:
- failure to comply with legally binding rules or principles
- breach of the principles of good administration (impartiality, proportionality, consistency, legitimate expectations, proper reasoning of decisions)
- discrimination, unfairness, or abuse of power
- failure to provide information or respond to requests for information
- unnecessary delays in responding or acting
- breach of fundamental rights under the Charter

## Who Can Complain and How

### Who is eligible
- Any EU citizen (wherever resident)
- Any natural person residing in a Member State
- Any legal person (company, association, NGO, trade body) with a registered office in a Member State

Third-country nationals residing outside the EU cannot use the complaints procedure.

### Time limit
Complaints must be lodged within 3 years of the complainant becoming aware of the facts, and only after the relevant institution has already been contacted (administrative phase exhausted).

### How to submit a complaint
1. Contact the institution first and allow it to respond (the Ombudsman requires evidence this has been done)
2. Submit the complaint via the Ombudsman's online portal: https://www.ombudsman.europa.eu/how-we-work
3. The complaint must identify: the complainant; the institution concerned; the facts and grounds of the complaint; the remedy sought
4. The complaint can be submitted in any of the 24 official EU languages
5. No legal representation is required; the process is free

### What happens next
The Office screens the complaint for admissibility. If admissible, it opens an inquiry and requests the institution's observations. The complainant can respond to those observations. The Ombudsman then closes the case with one of the following outcomes:
- **No maladministration found:** case closed with an explanation
- **Maladministration found, settled by the institution:** case closed with a positive outcome
- **Suggestion for improvement:** a less formal recommendation for future practice
- **Recommendation:** a formal finding with a specific remedy requested; the institution has 3 months to respond
- **Special report to EP:** issued if the institution refuses or fails to act on a recommendation

## Inquiry Types

### Complaint-based inquiries
Opened following a complaint from an eligible person. The most common type. The Ombudsman assesses admissibility (EU institution, within mandate, time limit, administrative phase exhausted) before opening a formal inquiry.

### Own-initiative inquiries (OI)
The Ombudsman opens these without receiving a complaint, typically when a systemic issue is identified through patterns in complaints, media coverage, or civil-society reporting. They carry the reference format OI/[number]/[year].

### Strategic inquiries
A subset of own-initiative inquiries targeting **systemic** or **high-impact** issues affecting many people or revealing broader institutional failures. They tend to run longer and produce more detailed recommendations. The Ombudsman publishes all strategic inquiries at:
https://www.ombudsman.europa.eu/strategic-issues/strategic-inquiries/all-strategic-inquiries

### Strategic initiatives
Shorter, lighter-touch instruments that do not open a formal inquiry. Used to promote best administrative practice, flag an emerging issue, or engage proactively with an institution before problems escalate.

## Outputs and How Recommendations Move

### Hierarchy of outputs (ascending gravity)
1. **Case closed, no maladministration:** the institution acted correctly or has corrected the situation
2. **Suggestion for improvement:** non-binding; addressed to the institution for future practice
3. **Draft recommendation:** the Ombudsman proposes a specific remedy; the institution has 3 months to respond with observations
4. **Recommendation:** a formal finding of maladministration with a specific remedy; the institution must comply or explain non-compliance
5. **Special report to the European Parliament:** issued when an institution refuses a recommendation; the EP can then apply political pressure or take legislative action

### Compliance rate
The Ombudsman publishes annual follow-up data. Historically, institutions comply fully or partially with the large majority of recommendations. Non-compliance is rare but creates reputational pressure.

### Impact on policymaking
Ombudsman inquiries regularly feed into:
- Commission reviews of its transparency register, expert-group rules, and Staff Regulation enforcement
- EP PETI hearings and legislative initiatives (e.g. the moribund revision of Regulation 1049/2001 on public access to documents -- see `public_access_to_documents_1049_2001`)
- CJEU litigation (complainants may use Ombudsman findings as supporting evidence in judicial proceedings, though the Ombudsman's decisions are not binding on courts)

## European Network of Ombudsmen

The European Network of Ombudsmen connects:
- The European Ombudsman
- National ombudsmen of all 27 EU Member States
- Regional ombudsmen (e.g. Catalonia's Síndic de Greuges, the Flemish Ombudsman)
- Ombudsmen from EFTA and EU candidate countries
- The EP Committee on Petitions (PETI) as an associated member

The Network has more than 100 member bodies from 36 countries. It coordinates on cross-border complaints, shares best practice, and produces the joint annual seminar. The Network website: https://www.ombudsman.europa.eu/european-network-of-ombudsmen

For Brubru users: if a complaint concerns national administration applying EU law (e.g. a national agency misapplying a regulation), it must go to the relevant national or regional ombudsman, not to the European Ombudsman. The network website lists the correct body for each country.

## Annual Report Cadence

The Ombudsman submits an annual report to the European Parliament each spring, covering:
- Number of complaints received, admissibility rate, closure rate
- Breakdown by institution and policy area
- Strategic inquiries progress
- Follow-up on recommendations
- Staff and budget figures

The EP presents the report in plenary and the Ombudsman addresses the relevant committee. Annual reports since 1995 are available in the Ombudsman's online archive.

## Award for Good Administration

The Ombudsman organises the Award for Good Administration every 5 years, recognising EU institutions and staff who demonstrate exceptional examples of service, transparency, or innovation. The award draws on the concept of good administration articulated in Article 41 of the Charter of Fundamental Rights.
