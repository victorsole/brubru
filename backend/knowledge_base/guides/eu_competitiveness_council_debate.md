## Overview

The EU competitiveness debate is the defining policy battleground of the 2024-2029 institutional cycle. Triggered by the Draghi Report (September 2024) and the Letta Report (April 2024), it pits member states against each other on fundamental questions: how much state aid flexibility to allow, whether to introduce European preference in public procurement, how far to roll back or delay climate legislation, and whether regulatory simplification should mean deregulation or smarter regulation.

This guide maps the political positioning of member states and coalitions in the Council on the competitiveness agenda, complementing Brubru's legislative guides on the specific proposals (Industrial Accelerator Act, European Climate Law, FP10/ECF, MFF 2028-2034).

## The Competitiveness Compass (COM(2025)30)

Adopted 29 January 2025, the Competitiveness Compass is the Commission's strategic framework structuring the competitiveness agenda around five pillars:

1. **Closing the innovation gap** with the US and China
2. **Decarbonisation as a competitiveness lever** (Clean Industrial Deal)
3. **Reducing dependencies** and increasing economic security
4. **Strengthening the single market** and simplifying regulation
5. **Investing in skills and social resilience**

The Compass was Commission President von der Leyen's direct response to the Draghi Report's diagnosis that Europe faces an "existential challenge" to its prosperity model.

## Member State Coalitions and Positions

### State Aid and Industrial Subsidies

The most divisive issue. The question is whether to relax EU state aid rules to allow member states to subsidise national champions, or whether this would fragment the single market by favouring large economies (France, Germany) over smaller ones.

**Pro-flexibility (more national subsidies):**
- **France**: Strongest advocate for relaxed state aid rules. Argues Europe cannot compete with US IRA and Chinese subsidies without matching them. Pushes for "European preference" in procurement and a sovereign fund. Industrial policy tradition (Colbertism).
- **Germany**: Supports flexibility but more cautious than France. Coalition agreement includes industrial policy ambitions. Concerned about level playing field within EU. Key tension: German industry wants subsidies but German fiscal orthodoxy resists EU-level debt.
- **Italy**: Supports targeted industrial support, especially for automotive transition (Stellantis/Fiat restructuring). Pushes for flexibility on ETS compliance costs for energy-intensive industries.

**Pro-discipline (maintain strict state aid rules):**
- **Netherlands**: Traditional guardian of level playing field. Fears relaxed state aid rules would let France and Germany outspend smaller economies. Advocates for single market deepening instead.
- **Nordic group (Sweden, Denmark, Finland)**: Free-market orientation. Prefer regulatory simplification over subsidies. Denmark and Sweden particularly wary of EU-level industrial policy.
- **Ireland**: Opposes relaxation that could undermine tax competition and single market integrity.
- **Baltic states (Estonia, Latvia, Lithuania)**: Cannot match large member state subsidies. Advocate for EU-level instruments (ECF) rather than national flexibility.
- **Austria**: Fiscal conservative, aligned with Netherlands on discipline.

**Swing states:**
- **Spain**: Open to flexibility but wants cohesion safeguards. Concerned about north-south subsidy gap.
- **Poland**: Interested in industrial support (especially energy transition) but wary of Western European dominance in allocation.
- **Belgium**: Hosts many EU institutions, tends to seek compromise. Presidency in H1 2024 shaped the initial Competitiveness Council conclusions.

### European Preference and "Made in EU"

Whether EU public procurement should favour EU-made products over imports, especially from China.

**Strong supporters:**
- **France**: Flagship demand. Wants mandatory EU-origin requirements in strategic sectors (defence, energy, transport, digital). Aligned with Industrial Accelerator Act Chapter III provisions.
- **Italy**: Supports for strategic sectors, especially automotive and steel.
- **Poland**: Supports for defence procurement (aligned with SAFE/ReArm Europe).

**Opponents or cautious:**
- **Netherlands**: Fears trade retaliation and higher procurement costs. Prefers WTO-compatible approach.
- **Germany**: Split internally. Industry federations (BDI) support European preference for critical sectors; economics ministry warns of protectionism.
- **Nordic states**: Free trade tradition. Oppose anything resembling protectionism.
- **Ireland**: Relies on open trade (US multinationals). Cautious on preference.

### Climate Ambition: 2040 Target and ETS

Whether to maintain the 90% GHG reduction target for 2040 or soften it in the name of competitiveness, and whether to delay or modify the ETS (especially ETS2 for buildings and transport).

**Maintain ambition:**
- **Denmark**: Climate leader. Insists competitiveness and climate are complementary, not contradictory.
- **Sweden**: Green industrial transition as competitive advantage (HYBRIT, H2 Green Steel).
- **Germany**: Green coalition legacy, though CDU/CSU-led government more pragmatic.
- **Netherlands**: Generally pro-climate, but pragmatic on implementation pace.
- **Spain**: Invested in renewable energy transition. Supports 2040 target.
- **Luxembourg, Finland**: Climate-forward.

**Roll back or delay:**
- **Poland**: Largest coal dependency. Consistently pushes for longer transition periods, more flexibility, compensation for energy-intensive sectors. Led opposition to ETS2 timeline.
- **Czech Republic**: Similar to Poland on energy transition pace.
- **Hungary**: Opposes climate costs on industry. Viktor Orban frames climate policy as "Brussels overreach."
- **Italy**: PM Meloni publicly called for "technological neutrality" -- code for keeping gas and nuclear options open.
- **Bulgaria, Romania**: Energy transition costs disproportionately affect lower-income member states.

**Pragmatic middle:**
- **France**: Strongly pro-nuclear (which it frames as clean energy). Supports 2040 target but wants nuclear recognised in taxonomy and energy mix flexibility.
- **Belgium**: Supports ambition with social safeguards.

### Regulatory Simplification

The omnibus simplification package and whether "cutting red tape" means genuine streamlining or rolling back environmental and social standards.

**Aggressive simplification:**
- **France, Italy**: Want faster permitting for industrial projects (addressed by Industrial Accelerator Act).
- **Germany**: BDI "25% less bureaucracy" campaign. Wants reporting burden cuts.
- **Business-oriented smaller states (Netherlands, Denmark, Ireland)**: Support simplification of reporting (CSRD, CSDDD) but not rollback of substance.

**Cautious approach:**
- **Nordic states**: Support simplification but oppose lowering environmental standards.
- **European Parliament**: Cross-party push to simplify but not gut sustainability reporting.

## European Council Summits: Competitiveness Trajectory

### 20-21 March 2025 (First Costa-Presidency EUCO)
- First summit under European Council President Antonio Costa
- Competitiveness declared a "top priority" alongside defence
- Leaders endorsed the Competitiveness Compass as the framework
- No binding conclusions on state aid reform -- kicked to Competitiveness Council
- Defence spending pledge (SAFE programme context)

### June 2025
- Clean Industrial Deal Communication (COM(2025)85) on the table
- Leaders debated industrial subsidies vs single market integrity
- France-Netherlands tension on state aid visible in conclusions language
- Carbon border adjustment (CBAM) implementation timeline confirmed

### October 2025
- Informal EUCO on economic security and dependencies
- Discussion on de-risking from China vs maintaining trade relations
- European preference language softened after Nordic and Dutch pushback

### 19-20 March 2026
- Industrial Accelerator Act (COM(2026)100) freshly adopted by Commission (4 March)
- Member states divided on Chapter III (procurement preferences) and Chapter IV (FDI conditions)
- Climate: ETS2 postponement to 2028 already agreed (European Climate Law amendment)
- Defence spending and competitiveness framed as complementary
- MFF 2028-2034 discussions intensifying -- budget envelope for ECF contested

## Why This Matters for Advocacy

Understanding member state positions is essential for:

1. **Council lobbying**: Knowing which Permanent Representation to approach and which coalition your position aligns with
2. **Coalition building**: Identifying potential allies among member states for your policy position
3. **Anticipating outcomes**: Predicting where Council compromises will land based on coalition dynamics
4. **Timing interventions**: Aligning advocacy with Council working party calendars and Coreper discussions
5. **Framing arguments**: Adapting your messaging to resonate with specific national positions (e.g., framing climate action as competitiveness for Denmark, as energy security for Poland)
