# EP Transparency Rules on Lobbying, MEP Meetings and the Transparency Register

## QUICK FACTS

- **Transparency Register (joint EP + Commission + Council, since 2021):** Established by the Interinstitutional Agreement of 20 May 2021 (IIA) -- OJ L 207, 11.06.2021, p. 1; EUR-Lex CELEX 32021Q0611(01). The register replaced the voluntary joint Parliament-Commission register created in 2011. Council joined for the first time in 2021, but with weaker scope (see "Differences vs Council" section below).
- **EP badge and access: registration is a condition.** Since the 2021 IIA entered into force, interest representatives who seek physical access to European Parliament premises (accredited passes) must be registered in the Transparency Register and comply with its Code of Conduct. Unregistered entities are denied EP access badges.
- **Rule 11(2) meeting publication (MEPs):** Article 11(2) of the EP Rules of Procedure (10th legislature version) requires MEPs to publish, on their personal EP profile page, scheduled meetings with interest representatives who fall within the scope of the Transparency Register. This applies when the MEP is acting as rapporteur, shadow rapporteur, or committee chair on a specific file.
- **Rule 11(3) rapporteur input declarations:** When drafting a report, opinion or amendment, rapporteurs and shadow rapporteurs must attach a "legislative footprint" declaration listing interest representatives who made a substantial contribution to their work. This is the EU analogue of a lobbying disclosure at the drafting stage.
- **Intergroup support disclosures (Annex VI RoP):** MEPs belonging to official intergroups must declare in the Register of Interests any financial support, including secretariat services, staff secondments, or logistical contributions received from outside bodies. Annex VI of the Rules of Procedure governs the registration and operation of intergroups; intergroups that fail to meet disclosure requirements can lose official EP recognition.
- **May 2025 Bureau decision -- management staff meeting publication:** In May 2025 the EP Bureau extended the meeting-publication obligation beyond MEPs to senior management-level EP staff (Directors-General and Directors). This brought the EP's internal transparency architecture closer to the Commission's model, where cabinet members and Directors-General have been subject to publication requirements since 2014.
- **Political driver -- Qatargate (December 2022):** The arrest on 9 December 2022 of then-Vice-President Eva Kaili (and associated MEPs, assistants and lobbyists) on allegations of corruption linked to Qatari and Moroccan influence was the immediate political catalyst for a major tightening of EP transparency rules. The scandal triggered multiple internal reforms: stricter gift rules, suspension of temporary passes for non-registered organisations, accelerated revision of the Code of Conduct, and political pressure for a mandatory lobbying register applying to the Council.
- **Commission regime (for comparison):** Commissioners, members of Commissioners' private offices (cabinets), and Directors-General must publish all meetings with interest representatives on the Commission's "Lobby Register Meetings" website. This obligation dates from the Juncker Commission (2014 guidelines) and was formalised in the 2021 IIA. DG-level meetings are searchable via the lobby-meeting register at ec.europa.eu/transparencyinitiative.
- **Council regime (weaker):** The Council joined the IIA in 2021 but has not matched the Parliament or Commission on meeting-publication requirements. Council working party chairs and COREPER members are not currently required to publish individual meetings with lobbyists. The 2021 IIA obliges the Council only to encourage its Presidency to promote transparency; civil society organisations (notably Transparency International) continue to advocate for mandatory Council meeting publication.
- **Transparency Register URL:** https://transparency-register.europa.eu/index_en
- **EP transparency portal:** https://www.europarl.europa.eu/at-your-service/en/transparency/lobby-groups
- **EP appropriate behaviour rules:** https://www.europarl.europa.eu/at-your-service/en/transparency/appropriate-behaviour

## 1. Transparency Register: Architecture and Legal Basis

The Transparency Register is a shared EU database listing organisations and individuals who carry out activities aimed at influencing the legislative and policy-making process of the EU institutions. Its legal basis is the Interinstitutional Agreement of 20 May 2021 (IIA) between the Parliament, the Council and the Commission -- the first time all three main institutions signed a single register agreement.

Key structural features:

- **Scope:** Includes in-house lobbyists at companies and trade associations; professional lobbying consultancies; NGOs; think tanks; law firms engaged in advocacy; religious organisations; subnational authorities (regions, cities); and academic/research institutions that carry out advocacy activities.
- **Mandatory for EP access:** Registration is a condition for obtaining EP building access (badge). Organisations that are not registered cannot receive badges for their representatives.
- **Code of Conduct:** All registrants must sign the Code of Conduct annexed to the IIA. Breaches can result in suspension or removal from the register and consequent loss of EP access.
- **Annual update obligation:** Registrants must update their financial data (lobbying budget, EU funding received) and policy interests annually. The Transparency Register Secretariat carries out compliance checks.
- **Not yet mandatory for all institutional interactions:** Registration is de facto required for physical EP access and for scheduled meetings with Commissioners, cabinet members and DGs, but it is not yet a general legal obligation imposed on all parties by EU law. The 2021 IIA is an interinstitutional instrument, not a regulation with direct effect on private parties. The Commission proposed a mandatory Transparency Register regulation in 2021 (COM(2021)305) but this remains under discussion.

## 2. Rule 11(2): MEP Meeting Publication

Article 11 of the EP Rules of Procedure operationalises the 2021 IIA at the level of individual MEPs.

Under Rule 11(2):

- MEPs **who hold the role of rapporteur, shadow rapporteur or committee chair** on a specific legislative or own-initiative file must publish, on their EP profile page, **all scheduled meetings they hold with interest representatives** falling within the scope of the Transparency Register.
- The publication must occur **before the meeting takes place** (or as promptly as possible). The published record shows the date, the organisation met, and a brief description of the subject.
- This requirement applies only to MEPs in one of the three named roles -- it does not (yet) apply to all MEPs for all meetings. A rank-and-file MEP attending an industry event, for example, has no publication obligation under Rule 11(2).

Practical implication for lobbyists: if your organisation meets the rapporteur on a file of interest, that meeting will appear on the rapporteur's EP profile and is publicly searchable. Your organisation must be registered in the Transparency Register to make that meeting lawful under the IIA.

---

## 3. Rule 11(3): Rapporteur Input Declarations ("Legislative Footprint")

Rule 11(3) introduces what is commonly called the "legislative footprint" in EU policy circles.

- When rapporteurs and shadow rapporteurs prepare a report, opinion, or amendments, they must **attach a written declaration** listing the interest representatives who contributed substantially to their work.
- The declaration is appended to the final report document published on the EP website (accessible via the OEIL procedure file or via the document reference in the relevant committee's documents register).
- "Substantial contribution" is not tightly defined in the Rules; in practice it covers written submissions on draft text, meetings that fed directly into drafting choices, and commissioned legal or technical analyses.
- The legislative footprint is a transparency instrument, not a restriction on lobbying. Interest representatives can and do provide substantial input -- the requirement is disclosure, not prohibition.

## 4. Intergroup Support Disclosures (Annex VI)

Intergroups are informal groupings of MEPs from different political groups who share a common policy interest (for example, the Intergroup on AI, the Intergroup on LGBTQI+ Rights, or the Intergroup on Climate Change, Biodiversity and Sustainable Development). They are not official EP bodies -- they cannot draft reports or take formal positions -- but they are influential informal forums.

Under Annex VI of the Rules of Procedure:

- Intergroups must register with the EP administration at the start of each legislature.
- MEPs belonging to an intergroup must declare **all financial support, secretariat services, staff secondments or logistical contributions** received from outside bodies.
- Declarations are published in the EP Register of Interests.
- An intergroup that fails to meet these requirements risks losing official EP recognition, which means it can no longer use EP rooms or appear on official EP communications.

This rule directly affects trade associations, NGOs and corporate foundations that fund intergroup secretariats. Providing staff to an intergroup secretariat triggers a disclosure obligation for the MEP chairs.

## 5. May 2025 Bureau Decision: Extension to Management Staff

In May 2025 the EP Bureau (the body comprising the President, Vice-Presidents and Quaestors that governs EP administration) adopted a decision extending meeting-publication obligations to senior EP management staff.

The key elements:

- **Who is covered:** Directors-General and Directors of EP administrative units (Directorate-General for the Presidency, DG IPOL, DG EXPO, DG COMM, DG PERS, DG FINS, and the EP Secretariat-General).
- **What must be published:** Scheduled meetings with registered interest representatives on matters relating to EP policy work (as distinct from administrative/procurement meetings).
- **Publication channel:** EP institutional website, alongside MEP meeting publications.
- **Why it matters:** Senior EP staff, particularly in DG IPOL (the secretariat serving committees) and DG EXPO (external relations), interact regularly with lobbyists on substantive legislative matters. This decision closes a gap that allowed substantial behind-the-scenes engagement to remain invisible.

This aligns the EP more closely with the Commission model, where DG-level meeting publication has applied since 2014.

## 6. Qatargate Context and Post-2022 Reforms

Qatargate (December 2022) was the largest corruption scandal in EP history. Belgian federal police arrested Eva Kaili (then EP Vice-President) along with former MEP Pier Antonio Panzeri, former EP assistant Francesco Giorgi, and trade union official Luca Visentini on allegations of bribery by Qatar and Morocco in exchange for favourable EP positions on human rights situations and labour conditions in those countries. Bags of cash and mobile phones were seized.

Post-Qatargate reforms included:

1. **Immediate suspension of temporary EP passes** for organisations that were not registered in the Transparency Register.
2. **Revision of the EP Code of Conduct for MEPs:** Strengthened gift ban (threshold reduced from EUR 150 to EUR 0 for cash gifts); extended cooling-off period for post-mandate lobbying activity (from 6 months to 24 months for former MEPs in certain roles); tightened obligation to declare all remunerated outside activities.
3. **Accelerated Ethics Body discussions:** The Commission had already proposed an independent EU Ethics Body (COM(2023)130); Qatargate intensified political pressure for its creation. As of mid-2026 the Ethics Body proposal remains under negotiation.
4. **PEGA Committee (2022-2023):** The Parliament's inquiry committee on the Pegasus spyware scandal ran in parallel; while separate from Qatargate, it reinforced the political climate of transparency reform.
5. **Renewed calls for mandatory Council transparency:** Qatargate put pressure on the Council, which had been the weakest link in the 2021 IIA. The Council has so far resisted mandatory meeting-publication requirements.

The "appropriate behaviour" rules for those wishing to interact with the EP are published at https://www.europarl.europa.eu/at-your-service/en/transparency/appropriate-behaviour.

## 7. Differences vs Commission Regime

| Dimension | European Parliament | European Commission |
|---|---|---|
| Legal basis for meetings publication | Rule 11(2) RoP (applies to rapporteurs, shadow rapporteurs, committee chairs) | Juncker Code of Conduct 2014 + 2021 IIA (applies to Commissioners, cabinet members, DGs) |
| Scope of persons covered | MEPs in named roles + (since May 2025) senior management staff | All Commissioners + full cabinets + all Directors-General |
| All MEPs | Not required | n/a |
| Legislative footprint | Rule 11(3): declaration in report documents | Not a formal requirement (individual DG meeting logs serve this function) |
| Register | Shared Transparency Register (IIA 2021) | Shared Transparency Register (IIA 2021) |
| Lobby meetings database | EP profile pages per MEP | ec.europa.eu/transparencyinitiative/meetings (searchable by institution/person) |
| Expert groups | Not applicable | Expert groups register: ec.europa.eu/transparency/expert-groups-register |

For Commission lobby meeting access, see `eu_commission_transparency_and_college_agenda`.

## 8. Differences vs Council Regime

The Council joined the 2021 IIA but the transparency obligations it accepted are substantially weaker:

- **No mandatory meeting publication:** Council Presidency holders, COREPER ambassadors, working party chairs and national experts in Council working parties are not required to publish meetings with lobbyists.
- **IIA obligation:** The Council committed only to "encourage" its Presidency to promote transparency measures and to share information on how it implements the IIA.
- **Access regime:** The Council does not issue building access badges in the same way the EP does; access to Justus Lipsius / Europa building for lobbying purposes is handled differently (mostly via MS Permanent Representations).
- **Transparency International assessment:** In successive annual reports, Transparency International EU has ranked the Council as "opaque" on lobbying transparency, noting that roughly 80% of trilogue negotiations (where final legislative text is agreed) occur without any public meeting publication.

This asymmetry is a significant gap: much of the most influential legislative negotiation in trilogues takes place between EP shadow rapporteurs and Council working party representatives, yet only the EP side has meaningful publication obligations.

## 9. Practical Guidance for Lobbyists Working with the EP

**Before you engage:**

1. **Register in the Transparency Register:** Without registration you cannot obtain an EP badge, cannot have a meeting published by a rapporteur under Rule 11(2), and risk violating the IIA Code of Conduct. Registration is free and takes 1-3 business days. URL: https://transparency-register.europa.eu/index_en
2. **Keep registration current:** Annual updates are mandatory. Financial data (lobbying budget, EU grants received, client fees for consultancies) must reflect the previous year. Outdated registrations are flagged by the Secretariat.
3. **Check the register before claiming to represent an organisation:** In joint meetings or coalition actions, ensure all organisations providing input are registered.

**When meeting a rapporteur or shadow rapporteur:**

4. The meeting will appear on the MEP's profile page (Rule 11(2)). This is not a problem -- it is expected. Plan the meeting accordingly: clarity on what you are asking for is more important than confidentiality.
5. If your submission will be used in the rapporteur's drafting work, it may appear in the legislative footprint declaration (Rule 11(3)). This is a signal that your input was taken seriously, not a risk.

**For intergroup engagement:**

6. Financial contributions to intergroup secretariats must be disclosed under Annex VI. Secretariat services and staff secondments are particularly sensitive because they tend to provide sustained access. Ensure your legal team has reviewed the disclosure requirements before committing support.

**Post-Qatargate environment:**

7. Cash, gifts above EUR 0, and any benefit that could appear improper are now career-ending for MEPs and legally risky for lobbyists. The post-2022 EP is significantly more cautious than the pre-2022 EP. Substance -- evidence, data, legal text -- is the currency of credible advocacy.
8. The cooling-off period means former MEPs working for lobby firms or joining in-house public affairs teams within 24 months of leaving Parliament must declare this activity. When engaging former MEPs as advisers, verify their compliance.
