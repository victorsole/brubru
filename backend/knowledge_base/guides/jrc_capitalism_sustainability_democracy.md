## Overview

This JRC synthesis report brings together reflections from 20+ senior European scholars who contributed to the JRC's FASE working paper series during 2024-2025. Using a Schumpeterian framework inspired by Joseph Schumpeter's 1942 *Capitalism, Socialism and Democracy*, the report examines the triangular relationship between capitalism, sustainability, and democracy as the fundamental dynamic that will decide Europe's future.

The report explicitly positions itself as a complement to the Draghi report, arguing that an exclusive policy focus on competitiveness risks undermining the overall European project. Europe's DNA and future depend on a dynamic combination of all three dimensions, where no one can be isolated from the others.

## The Trilateral Framework

Three geopolitical models are contrasted:
- **United States**: Prioritises capitalism and democracy at the expense of sustainability. Technology-powered mercantilist capitalism with high import tariffs, sacrificing long-term sustainability commitments.
- **China**: Aligns capitalism with sustainability at the expense of democracy. State capitalism with technological directionality embedded, high savings ratio (40% of GDP), AI-driven industrial automation.
- **Europe**: Tries to combine sustainability and democracy at the expense of raw capitalism. Social market economy (*die soziale Marktwirtschaft*) balancing market forces with welfare state, rule of law, and environmental protection.

The central argument: Europe is potentially well suited to combine all three in a synergistic relationship, but only if it reforms its governance, reinvigorates entrepreneurial dynamism, and addresses territorial inequalities.

## Chapter 1: Capitalism

Key themes from the Capitalism chapter:

**Industrial transformation**: Europe needs "integrated orchestration" -- a governance model that strategically aligns regulatory frameworks, funding, institutional platforms, and stakeholder participation without relying on fiscal federalism (Radosevic). The single market is shifting from a rules-based liberalisation mechanism to a hybrid platform shaped by industrial policy objectives.

**Place-based competitiveness**: Rodriguez-Pose and Dijkstra argue Europe's diversity is its strength, not its weakness. Territorial myopia -- fixating on where Europe lags (Silicon Valley, Shenzhen) rather than leveraging existing strengths (pharmaceuticals, chemicals, aeronautics, *Mittelstand*) -- risks deepening the competitiveness gap.

**Creative destruction challenge**: Europe has lost Schumpeterian dynamism. Rules and regulations stifle endogenous creative destruction. Venture capital is scarce, market fragmentation persists, and the EU cannot connect new technology firms with defence/public procurement demand. Proposal: move fundamental research funding to EU level, create a European DARPA-like network (Kattel/Entsminger), reform Horizon Europe/FP10 partnerships.

**Chips and the middle tech trap**: Daniel Gros argues Europe is stuck in a "middle tech trap" -- producing mature-node automotive chips (15-20nm) while advanced logic chips (3-5nm) come from Asia and chip design/software from the US. Only one European company (Infineon) in the global top 10. ASML is the EU's key strategic asset (20% market share in chip-manufacturing equipment, near-monopoly in EUV lithography). Strengthening software/design capacity matters more than subsidising large fabs.

## Chapter 2: Sustainability

The pursuit of sustainability creates tensions with both capitalism and democracy. The European Green Deal was conceived as a new Keynesian growth engine, but its "space-blind" nature and the failure to align supply with demand have created political backlash.

Key arguments:
- **Lead markets for clean tech**: EU is well positioned to become the world's largest market for clean tech innovation, but needs demand-side policies (public procurement, lead markets, CBAM) in addition to supply-side measures (Georghiou).
- **Circularity as strategic autonomy**: Circular economy strategies can reduce dependency on critical raw materials, linking sustainability with security (Tukker).
- **Energy policy 2025-2035**: Internal dimension (energy efficiency, local employment, affordable solutions) and external dimension (competitiveness, export industries, security) must both be addressed (Kivimaa).
- **Fairness and the green transition**: Perceptions of unfairness undermine democratic support for sustainability policies. The "climate coalition" consists mainly of professional/technocratic elite, not the workers and rural communities most affected (Lunn, Charbonnier).
- **Local missions**: Bottom-up community initiatives can link climate mitigation with cohesion, diminishing perceived unfairness (McCann/Janssen/Stierna).

## Chapter 3: Democracy

Democracy in Europe is under pressure from multiple directions: rising Euroscepticism (from 7% combined vote in 2003 to 28.5% in 2023), growing "geography of discontent" in left-behind regions, AI's potential to concentrate power, and social media amplifying disinformation.

Key arguments:
- **Economic polarisation feeds Euroscepticism**: People in "development traps" -- regions with long-term economic decline, lost jobs, dwindling public services -- are increasingly averse to European integration (Rodriguez-Pose/Dijkstra).
- **Welfare state as democratic defence**: Without binding EU-level minimum standards for income support, the current framework is insufficient for equitable protection. Scaling back social policies to finance defence risks "jeopardizing the very security effort it aims at preserving" (Laurent).
- **AI and democracy**: Generative AI offers data-driven solutions that bypass democratic deliberation. When knowledge shifts from humans to machines, it enables concentration of power (Brynjolfsson's "Turing Trap").
- **Governance complexity**: The EU is a "regulatory giant but a political/military/economic dwarf" with barely 1% of GDP as budget. The Lisbon Treaty did not prepare the EU for 21st-century crises. "Muddling through may prevent you from tumbling down, but it is not enough for the EU any more" (Olli Rehn).
- **"Quantum policymaking"**: Andrea Renda proposes reorganising the MFF with conditionalities, enabling mission-oriented policymaking, starting with dual-use innovations.
- **Well-being beyond GDP**: Laurent et al. propose new indicators and a monitoring framework measuring European well-being including health and cooperation, beyond GDP.

## Conclusions: Six Lines of Policy Action

The report proposes six concrete short-term policy actions:

1. **Position Europe in AI** -- EU as honest broker for integrated AI initiatives, *ex post* assessment rather than *ex ante* regulation, AI in public services, proactive labour market policies for AI-displaced workers
2. **Address innovation gap and science fragmentation** -- Pool national funds for breakthrough science, reorganise civilian/defence R&I synergies, strengthen EIC, EU as global science hub
3. **Place-based industrial policy** -- Connect bottom-up initiatives with EU-wide strategy, smart specialisation with security dimension, outcome-oriented territorial development
4. **Competitive sustainability** -- Lead markets for clean tech, CBAM, circular economy for strategic autonomy, decentralised energy grids, fair distribution of transition costs
5. **Unite and partner** -- Enlarge single market (Letta), coalitions of the willing as "speedboats", innovation-friendly regulation partnerships
6. **Governance reform** -- Revisit the Lisbon Treaty, reassess EU multilevel governance, overcome the Holy Roman Empire analogy (de Gruyter), develop a European political identity (Husserl's "European spirit")

## Relevance for Advocacy

This report is essential reading for anyone working on EU competitiveness, industrial policy, or governance reform. It provides the intellectual framework connecting the Draghi report (competitiveness), Letta report (single market), and the ongoing MFF/FP10 negotiations. For advocacy purposes:
- Use the trilateral framework to argue that competitiveness measures must be paired with sustainability and democratic legitimacy
- Cite the EU investment gap (22.5% vs 40%) in position papers on EU budget and MFF
- Reference the "geography of discontent" data when arguing for place-based policies
- Use the Schumpeterian "creative destruction" analysis to support innovation-friendly regulation arguments
- The governance reform proposals (Lisbon Treaty revision, quantum policymaking) are relevant for any organisation engaged in EU institutional reform debates
