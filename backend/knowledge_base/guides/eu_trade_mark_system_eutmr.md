# EU Trade Mark System: Regulation (EU) 2017/1001 and Directive (EU) 2015/2436

## QUICK FACTS

- **Two instruments, two purposes**: **Regulation (EU) 2017/1001** creates a single, unitary **EU trade mark (EUTM)** administered centrally by EUIPO. **Directive (EU) 2015/2436** harmonises the national trade mark laws Member States keep for their own registers. A brand owner can hold a national mark, an EUTM, or both; the EUTM does not replace national marks.
- **Regulation**: Reg (EU) 2017/1001 of 14 June 2017 on the European Union trade mark (codification). **CELEX**: 32017R1001. **OJ**: L 154, 16.6.2017, p. 1. Directly applicable, no transposition. Codifies Reg (EC) 207/2009 (itself recast of Reg (EC) 40/94, the 1994 "Community trade mark").
- **Directive**: Dir (EU) 2015/2436 of 16 December 2015 approximating national trade mark laws (recast). **CELEX**: 32015L2436. **OJ**: L 336, 23.12.2015, p. 1. Transposition deadline 14 January 2019 (Article 45 administrative procedures: 14 January 2023).
- **Legal base**: Article 118 TFEU (Regulation); Article 114 TFEU (Directive).
- **Administering body**: **EUIPO**, Alicante, Spain.
- **Unitary character (Article 1(2) EUTMR)**: one EUTM has equal effect in **all 27 Member States** at once; cannot be registered, transferred, revoked or invalidated for only part of the Union (narrow exceptions apply).
- **What can be a mark (Article 4)**: any sign distinguishing goods/services, represented clearly and precisely on the register. Graphic representability dropped in 2015, opening the register to sound, multimedia and motion marks.
- **Nice Classification**: 45 classes (1-34 goods, 35-45 services). Class headings read literally; list specific goods/services (CJEU *IP TRANSLATOR*, C-307/10).
- **Filing routes**: direct EUIPO e-filing; via a national/Benelux IP office; via the Madrid System (WIPO), designating "European Union" as a territory.
- **Absolute grounds (Article 7)**: non-distinctive, descriptive, generic, functional/technical shapes, contrary to public policy, deceptive, conflicting with a protected geographical indication. Examined ex officio EU-wide; a ground valid in just one official language can block the whole application.
- **Relative grounds / opposition (Article 8)**: likelihood of confusion with an earlier identical/similar mark on identical/similar goods; unfair advantage of or detriment to a reputed mark, even on dissimilar goods. Raised only via opposition, never ex officio.
- **Process**: e-filing, formalities and absolute-grounds examination, publication in the EU Trade Marks Bulletin, three-month opposition window, registration if unopposed.
- **Term and renewal (Articles 52-53)**: ten years from filing, renewable indefinitely for further ten-year periods.
- **Revocation for non-use (Article 58(1)(a))**: vulnerable after five continuous years without genuine EU use, clock starting at registration, not filing.
- **Invalidity (Articles 59-60)**: absolute grounds (should never have registered) or relative grounds (conflict with an earlier right missed earlier).
- **Enforcement**: EUIPO administrative track (opposition, revocation, invalidity) appealable to the EUIPO Boards of Appeal, then the General Court, then the CJEU on points of law; parallel civil track via national EU trade mark courts; customs detention under Reg (EU) 608/2013.
- **Madrid System**: EU is a Madrid Protocol contracting party; an International Registration can designate the EU with EUTM-equivalent effect, and an EUTM can serve as a Madrid basic mark.
- **SME cost relief**: EUIPO SME Fund ("Ideas Powered for Business") Voucher 2 partially reimburses EUTM fees, see `euipo_sme_fund_ideas_powered_for_business.md`.
- **EUR-Lex (Regulation)**: `https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32017R1001`
- **EUR-Lex (Directive)**: `https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32015L2436`
- **EUIPO trade marks portal**: `https://www.euipo.europa.eu/en/trade-marks`
- **EUIPO legal texts**: `https://www.euipo.europa.eu/en/law`

## Why This Matters for Advisors

The EU trade mark is one of the most heavily used commercial legal instruments in the Union: EUIPO receives well over 180,000 EUTM applications a year, and trade mark strategy touches almost every commercial file an EU policy or legal advisor works on: brand protection, e-commerce enforcement, platform takedowns under the Digital Services Act, parallel imports and exhaustion of rights, customs seizures of counterfeits, and licensing and franchising structures. Unlike patents (which remain largely national or run through the separate European Patent Office / Unitary Patent system) or copyright (which has no unitary EU registration at all), trade marks already have a single, centrally administered, EU-wide registered right. Advisors need to know when a client should file nationally, file an EUTM, or use the Madrid System, and how opposition, revocation and invalidity risk shape brand strategy over the life of a mark.

## Structure: Regulation vs Directive

### Regulation (EU) 2017/1001 (EUTMR)

Establishes and governs the **EU trade mark** itself: a single application, a single registration, a single renewal, with unitary effect across all Member States. It is directly applicable; no national implementing legislation is needed or permitted to alter its substantive content. EUIPO is the registering and administering authority.

The current text is a **codification**: it consolidates Regulation (EC) 207/2009 (itself a recast of Regulation (EC) 40/94, the original 1994 Community Trade Mark Regulation) together with the substantive amendments introduced by the 2015 "Trade Mark Package" reform (Regulation (EU) 2015/2424). Codification means no substantive changes were made in 2017; the numbering and structure were simply consolidated into a single clean text.

### Directive (EU) 2015/2436 (TMD, recast)

Does **not** create any EU-wide right. It requires Member States to align the substantive content of their **national** trade mark laws (definitions of what can be a mark, absolute and relative grounds for refusal, revocation and invalidity grounds, rights conferred, exhaustion, licensing) so that national trade mark systems function consistently alongside the EUTM system. Member States retain their own national trade mark offices, their own registration procedures, and their own fee schedules; only the substantive legal content is harmonised.

The 2015 recast (replacing the earlier Directive 2008/95/EC) introduced administrative revocation and invalidity procedures at national office level (Article 45), removed the graphic-representability requirement to match the Regulation, and clarified goods-in-transit and preparatory-acts provisions to strengthen anti-counterfeiting enforcement.

### Why both exist together

The dual-track design (unitary EU right + harmonised national rights) mirrors the EU's broader approach to intellectual property: create a single EU-wide option for those who want it, while keeping cost-effective national protection available and legally coherent for businesses that only need protection in one or a few Member States. A UK-only or a single-Member-State retailer, for example, may prefer a cheaper national filing; a pan-EU brand typically prefers the unitary EUTM.

## The EUTM Application and Examination Process

1. **Filing.** Applicant (or their representative, since professional representation before EUIPO is mandatory for applicants without a domicile, principal place of business, or real and effective industrial/commercial establishment in the EEA) files electronically via EUIPO's e-filing tool, listing the sign, the goods/services by Nice class, and paying the application fee.
2. **Formalities examination.** EUIPO checks the application is complete (correct fee paid, sign properly represented, applicant identified, classification in order).
3. **Absolute grounds examination.** EUIPO examiners assess the sign against Article 7 grounds (descriptiveness, lack of distinctiveness, deceptiveness, public policy, prior protected geographical indications, etc.) across all 24 official EU languages and all Member States, since refusal in respect of even one Member State's language or public can be enough for an EU-wide refusal.
4. **Search report (optional).** On request, EUIPO issues an EU search report listing similar earlier EUTMs and, where the relevant national office participates, similar earlier national marks. This is informational only; EUIPO does not refuse ex officio on relative grounds.
5. **Publication.** The application is published in the EU Trade Marks Bulletin.
6. **Opposition period.** Third parties with earlier rights have **three months** from the day after publication to file a notice of opposition based on Article 8 relative grounds. A "cooling-off period" typically follows to allow settlement negotiations before formal adversarial proceedings begin.
7. **Opposition proceedings (if filed).** EUIPO's Opposition Division assesses likelihood of confusion / reputation-based claims; either party can request "proof of use" of the earlier mark if it has been registered for five years or more.
8. **Registration.** If unopposed, or if opposition is rejected/withdrawn, EUIPO registers the mark and issues a registration certificate. The registration date, not the filing date, is what starts the five-year non-use clock (see below); the filing date remains relevant for priority and for the ten-year renewal term.

## Grounds for Refusal in Detail

### Absolute grounds (Article 7 EUTMR): examined ex officio, apply EU-wide

Article 7(1) lists the grounds; key categories advisors should recognise:
- (b) devoid of any distinctive character
- (c) descriptive of the kind, quality, quantity, intended purpose, value, geographical origin, or other characteristics of the goods/services
- (d) customary in the current language or bona fide trade practice (generic)
- (e) shapes/characteristics resulting from the nature of the goods, necessary to obtain a technical result, or giving substantial value to the goods (shape marks, three-dimensional marks)
- (f) contrary to public policy or accepted principles of morality
- (g) liable to deceive the public (e.g. as to nature, quality, or geographical origin)
- (j)-(l) conflicts with protected geographical indications, traditional wine terms, or traditional specialities guaranteed
- **Article 7(3), acquired distinctiveness ("secondary meaning")**: a sign otherwise refusable under (b), (c) or (d) can still be registered if the applicant proves it has become distinctive through use, in the relevant part of the Union, before the filing date. This is a common route for descriptive-sounding marks that have built strong market recognition.

### Relative grounds (Article 8 EUTMR): raised only by opposition or invalidity action

- **Article 8(1)(a)**: identical earlier mark, identical goods/services (double identity: no likelihood-of-confusion analysis needed, protection is close to absolute)
- **Article 8(1)(b)**: identical or similar earlier mark, identical or similar goods/services, giving rise to a likelihood of confusion (including likelihood of association), the most commonly litigated ground, assessed via the CJEU's global-appreciation test (visual, aural, conceptual similarity of signs; similarity of goods/services; distinctiveness of the earlier mark; relevant public's level of attention)
- **Article 8(4)**: earlier unregistered/non-registered rights of more than local significance recognised under national law (e.g. certain national unregistered marks, trade names) used in the course of trade before the EUTM filing date
- **Article 8(5)**: earlier mark with a reputation in the EU (or relevant Member State), where use of the later mark without due cause would take unfair advantage of, or be detrimental to, the distinctive character or repute of the earlier mark, extending protection to dissimilar goods/services for well-known marks

## Revocation, Invalidity and Non-Use

An EUTM is a **use-it-or-lose-it** right. Once registered for five years, the proprietor must be able to show genuine use in the EU for the goods/services it is registered for, or the registration becomes vulnerable to revocation (in whole or for the unused goods/services) under Article 58(1)(a). "Genuine use" requires actual commercial use to create or preserve market share, not token use to preserve the registration; the CJEU's case law (starting with *Ansul*, Case C-40/01, decided under the earlier Directive but applied consistently under the current instruments) sets the benchmark.

Non-use is frequently raised defensively: a party accused of infringement, or opposed by an earlier EUTM more than five years old, can demand "proof of use", forcing the earlier right holder to produce evidence (invoices, packaging, advertising, market share data) of genuine use during the relevant five-year window, in the relevant Member States, for the relevant goods/services.

**Invalidity** differs from revocation in that it attacks the validity of the mark from the outset (it should never have been registered), whereas revocation accepts the mark was validly registered but that a subsequent event (non-use, becoming generic, becoming deceptive through the proprietor's own use) has removed the basis for it to continue.

## Appeals and Enforcement Architecture

- **First instance**: EUIPO examiners (absolute grounds), Opposition Division (oppositions), Cancellation Division (revocation/invalidity)
- **Second instance**: **EUIPO Boards of Appeal**: internal, quasi-judicial appeal bodies within EUIPO, organised into several Boards by subject matter
- **Third instance**: **General Court** of the CJEU (Luxembourg): judicial review of Board of Appeal decisions, full review of law and (to a more limited extent) fact
- **Fourth instance**: **Court of Justice of the European Union (CJEU)**: appeal on points of law only, from General Court judgments
- **Parallel civil track**: EUTM infringement and validity counterclaims can also be litigated directly before national **EU trade mark courts** designated by each Member State, with the possibility of EU-wide injunctions; a national court hearing an EUTM case can refer questions of EU law to the CJEU under the preliminary reference procedure (Article 267 TFEU)
- **Customs**: separate administrative enforcement track via Regulation (EU) 608/2013, allowing detention of suspected counterfeit goods at the EU's external border without needing a prior court judgment

## Practical Notes for Advisors

- **National vs EUTM vs Madrid** is a cost/coverage trade-off: a client only trading in one or two Member States may be better served by national filings (cheaper, and immune to the "all-or-nothing" unitary-refusal risk where a ground valid in just one Member State can sink an entire EUTM application); a client with pan-EU ambitions typically wants the EUTM; a client with a global brand already holding a national or EUTM "basic mark" can extend protection efficiently via the Madrid System.
- **The all-or-nothing refusal risk** is the single most important structural feature to explain to clients: because the EUTM has unitary character, an absolute ground that applies in only one Member State's language or market (e.g. a word that is descriptive in Polish but invented in every other EU language) can block registration for the whole EU, whereas the same sign could register without difficulty as a national mark in the other 26 Member States.
- **SME cost relief**: EUIPO's SME Fund ("Ideas Powered for Business") Voucher 2 partially reimburses trade mark and design application fees for SMEs (see `euipo_sme_fund_ideas_powered_for_business.md` for current-year rates, which change edition to edition).
- **Craft and industrial GIs are a separate, newer EUIPO-administered register**, not part of the EUTM system: geographical indications for craft and industrial products are protected under Regulation (EU) 2024/1143 (see `gi_craft_industrial_2024_1143.md`), and EUIPO administers that registry too, but it is legally and procedurally distinct from trade marks.
- **Platform and e-commerce enforcement**: EUTM holders increasingly rely on the notice-and-action and trusted-flagger mechanisms under the Digital Services Act (see `digital_services_act.md`) to have counterfeit listings removed from online marketplaces, in parallel with traditional opposition, invalidity and customs enforcement routes.
- **Do not confuse** the EUTM system with EU copyright (no EU-wide registration exists for copyright at all; protection is automatic and territorial in application though harmonised in substance) or with the Unitary Patent / Unified Patent Court system (a separate, more recent instrument covering patents, not trade marks).
