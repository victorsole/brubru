## Context and Background

Ireland's Revenue Commissioners issued two advance tax rulings to Apple companies: one in January 1991 and a revised ruling in May 2007. Both rulings concerned the allocation of profits between the Irish branches of Apple Sales International and Apple Operations Europe and their respective "head offices." The head offices were not based anywhere, had no employees and performed no business functions. The rulings endorsed profit-allocation methods that directed almost all of ASI's and AOE's multi-billion-dollar annual profits to those empty head offices, so that only a small residual was taxed in Ireland.

The Commission opened a formal investigation in June 2014 and adopted the decision on 30 August 2016, finding the rulings constituted State aid incompatible with the internal market.

---

## The Two Companies and the Ruling Structure

**Apple Sales International (ASI)** was incorporated in Ireland but was not tax-resident in Ireland or anywhere (by operation of Section 23A TCA 97, which exempted Irish-incorporated companies from Irish tax residency if centrally managed and controlled outside Ireland). ASI held the beneficial rights to Apple's intellectual property licences for all markets outside the Americas under a Cost Sharing Agreement (CSA) with Apple Inc. Its Irish branch procured finished goods, distributed products across EMEIA and APAC, operated AppleCare after-sales support, managed warranty liabilities and ran localised marketing. ASI's annual sales grew from around USD 3.7 billion in 2003 to over USD 67.5 billion by 2014.

**The 1991 ruling** allocated to ASI's Irish branch 12.5% of branch operating costs (excluding materials for resale). **The 2007 ruling** revised this to approximately 10-15% of branch operating costs. Neither ruling was accompanied by a transfer-pricing report or functional analysis.

**Apple Operations Europe (AOE)** manufactured specialised computer products at a Cork facility for the EMEIA region. The 1991 ruling allocated 65% of operating costs up to USD 60-70 million and 20% above that. The 2007 ruling changed this to approximately 10-15% of operating costs plus 1-5% of turnover as an IP return.

## The Commission's Legal Test

### Reference system

The reference system is the ordinary rules for taxing corporate profits in Ireland, under which all companies liable to Irish corporate tax -- resident or non-resident -- are assessed on their profits at the standard rate (12.5% for trading income). Section 25 TCA 97 (branch profits of non-resident companies) is part of that general system, not a separate reference framework.

### Arm's length principle as a State aid benchmark

The Commission applies the arm's length principle as derived from Article 107(1) TFEU and the Court of Justice's judgment in Cases C-182/03 and C-217/03 (Belgian coordination centres): where an integrated company employs intra-group pricing that departs from what independent parties dealing at arm's length would agree, the resulting reduction in taxable income constitutes a selective advantage. The Commission does not rely on the OECD transfer-pricing guidelines as its primary source of law; it uses them as non-binding guidance only.

### Primary finding: IP allocation to empty head offices was unsubstantiated

The fundamental flaw was that both rulings assumed the IP licences held by ASI and AOE should be allocated to the head offices. Irish Revenue accepted this without any functional analysis. The Commission found:

- ASI's and AOE's head offices had no employees, no physical premises and no capacity to manage Apple's IP during the entire ruling period.
- Board minutes from 1980 to 2015 show the boards approved accounts, passed dividends and granted powers of attorney. There is no discussion of strategic IP decisions or CSA management.
- The Irish branches employed hundreds of staff performing procurement, distribution, after-sales support and manufacturing: the economically significant functions that actually exploited Apple's IP.
- Under the Authorised OECD Approach, the economic ownership of intangibles follows the entity performing the significant people functions. Since all those functions were in Ireland, the IP profits should have been allocated to the Irish branches.

### Subsidiary finding: methodological defects

Even if the IP allocation to head offices were accepted, the methods were flawed: (a) the Irish branches were incorrectly treated as the less complex tested party; (b) operating costs were used as the profit level indicator for ASI, a distributor with rapidly growing sales, when turnover or total costs would have been appropriate; (c) operating costs were used for AOE, a manufacturer bearing inventory risk, when total costs would have been appropriate; and (d) the resulting returns were not supported by contemporaneous benchmarking.

---

## The Recovery Order

Ireland must calculate the tax that ASI and AOE should have paid on the assumption that all their trading profits, including the profits from Apple's IP licences, were allocated to their Irish branches. Permitted deductions include interest on passively managed liquidity held by Braeburn Capital, limited capital allowances agreed in the 1991 ruling, and Singapore branch profits taxed in Singapore. Recovery runs from 12 June 2003 to 27 September 2014 with compound interest.

---

## The Litigation

**General Court, 15 July 2020 (T-778/16, T-892/16):** The General Court annulled the Commission's decision. It held the Commission had not proved to the required legal standard that the profit allocation methods departed from an arm's length outcome, and accepted that the Commission had not adequately demonstrated that the head-office functions were truly empty.

**Court of Justice, 10 September 2024 (C-465/20 P):** The CJEU set aside the General Court's judgment in its entirety and gave final judgment upholding the Commission's decision. The Court held that the General Court had applied an incorrect standard of review and had misread the Commission's primary and subsidiary lines of reasoning. The Commission's finding of a selective advantage was confirmed. The escrowed funds (approximately EUR 14.3 billion) are to be released to Ireland and applied as the decision requires.

## Key Compliance Principles

1. An entity assigned residual group profits must have genuine employees performing active management functions for the assets concerned.
2. Advance tax ruling applications must be supported by a contemporaneous functional analysis and transfer-pricing study.
3. The tested party in a one-sided profit-allocation method must be the genuinely less complex entity. Branches performing complex, risk-bearing functions cannot be the tested party.
4. For a distributor bearing inventory, warranty and credit risk with growing sales, operating costs are an inappropriate profit level indicator; turnover or total costs should be considered.
5. For a manufacturer bearing inventory and production risk, total costs (not operating costs alone) are the appropriate profit level indicator.
6. The arm's length principle is mandatory under EU State aid law regardless of whether the Member State has codified transfer-pricing rules.
7. Tax rulings must be time-limited and contain material-change triggers.
8. Operating aid cannot be rendered compatible with the internal market by reference to employment or investment objectives.
9. Non-notification of a ruling does not create legitimate expectations against a State aid recovery order.
