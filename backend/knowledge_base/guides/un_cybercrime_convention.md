## Overview

The UN Convention against Cybercrime is the first global treaty on cybercrime, negotiated under UN auspices from 2022 to 2024. It was adopted by the UN General Assembly in December 2024 after contentious negotiations. The convention aims to establish a common legal framework for criminalising cyberoffences, facilitating international cooperation, and mutual legal assistance in cybercrime investigations.

The EU participated in negotiations as a bloc. The Council decision to conclude the convention on behalf of the EU requires European Parliament consent under procedure 2025/0231(NLE).

## Key Provisions

### Criminalisation (Chapter II)
- Illegal access to computer systems
- Illegal interception of data
- Data and system interference
- Computer-related fraud and forgery
- Child sexual abuse material (online)
- Identity theft using ICT
- Money laundering of cybercrime proceeds

### International Cooperation (Chapter IV)
- Mutual legal assistance framework
- Expedited preservation of stored computer data
- Cross-border access to stored data
- 24/7 network for emergency assistance
- Joint investigations

### Procedural Powers (Chapter III)
- Expedited data preservation orders
- Production orders for subscriber information
- Search and seizure of stored data
- Real-time collection of traffic data
- Interception of content data (most controversial)

## Controversy and Human Rights Concerns

The convention has been criticised by:
- **Civil society** (EFF, Access Now, Human Rights Watch): insufficient human rights safeguards, risk of authoritarian abuse for surveillance, overly broad scope
- **Tech companies** (Microsoft, Google, Meta): concerns about government access to data, conflict with GDPR and EU data protection standards
- **EU institutions**: EP resolution called for strong human rights safeguards; Council legal service assessed compatibility with EU law

Key concerns:
- Real-time surveillance provisions could enable mass surveillance
- Broad scope beyond "cyber" crimes (any crime involving ICT)
- No independent oversight mechanism
- Risk of use against journalists, activists, political opposition
- Potential conflict with GDPR extraterritorial provisions

## Relationship with Budapest Convention

The Council of Europe's Budapest Convention (2001) was the first international treaty on cybercrime, with 68+ parties. Key differences:

| Aspect | Budapest Convention | UN Convention |
|--------|-------------------|---------------|
| Scope | Narrower (cybercrime-specific) | Broader (any crime involving ICT) |
| Parties | 68+ (mainly Western democracies) | Potentially universal |
| Human rights | Strong safeguards, CoE standards | Weaker safeguards, contested |
| Oversight | Cybercrime Convention Committee | Conference of States Parties |
| Data access | Mutual legal assistance + Second Protocol | Similar but broader scope |

## EP Position
The EP's LIBE committee is expected to recommend consent, with concerns noted about implementation safeguards. The consent vote means the EP can only approve or reject, not amend the convention text.
