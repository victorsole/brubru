# EU Toy Safety Regulation (Regulation (EU) 2025/2509)

## QUICK FACTS
- **What it is:** the revised EU rulebook on toy safety, replacing the Toy Safety Directive 2009/48/EC with a directly applicable Regulation. Covers toys intended for children under 14 years placed on the EU market, whether made in the EU or imported.
- **Reference:** Regulation (EU) 2025/2509 of the European Parliament and of the Council of 26 November 2025 on the safety of toys and repealing Directive 2009/48/EC.
- **CELEX:** 32025R2509. EUR-Lex: https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32025R2509
- **OJ citation:** OJ L, 2025/2509, 12.12.2025 (Publications Office ELI numbering, not the old "OJ L volume/page" format).
- **Legal basis:** Article 114 TFEU (internal market harmonisation).
- **Procedure:** 2023/0290(COD), ordinary legislative procedure. OEIL: https://oeil.secure.europarl.europa.eu/oeil/en/procedure-file?reference=2023/0290(COD)
- **Commission proposal:** COM(2023) 462 final, tabled 28 July 2023, following the Commission's evaluation of the 2009 Directive (SWD(2020) 287).
- **Provisional political agreement:** reached in the final trilogue on 10 April 2025, announced by the Commission 11 April 2025 (press release IP/25/1039). Endorsed by the IMCO committee on 26 June 2025.
- **Council first-reading position:** adopted 13 October 2025 (Austria abstained).
- **European Parliament:** approved the Council's position at first reading without a vote (early second-reading conclusion) on 25 November 2025.
- **Signed:** 26 November 2025. **Published in the Official Journal:** 12 December 2025.
- **Status as of this guide (16 July 2026): adopted and in force.** This is not a pending proposal.
- **Entry into force:** 1 January 2026 (20 days after OJ publication).
- **Full application:** 1 August 2030, after a 4.5-year (54-month) transition period. Toys compliant with Directive 2009/48/EC may continue to be placed on the market until then.
- **Lead committee:** IMCO (Internal Market and Consumer Protection), chaired by Anna Cavazzini. Opinion committees: JURI, AGRI, ITRE.
- **Rapporteur:** Marion Walsmann (EPP/PPE, Germany, CDU).
- **Responsible Commission department:** DG GROW (single-market-economy.ec.europa.eu), Executive Vice-President Stéphane Séjourné.
- **Official Commission page:** https://single-market-economy.ec.europa.eu/sectors/toys/toy-safety_en
- **Scale of the problem it targets:** the EU imported around €6.5 billion worth of toys in 2023, roughly 80% from China. Toys are the second most frequently notified dangerous consumer product in the EU's Safety Gate rapid alert system, after cosmetics.

## What it is and why it exists

The Toy Safety Directive 2009/48/EC (itself a replacement for Directive 88/378/EEC) set essential safety requirements for toys sold in the EU: general risks to children's and caregivers' health and safety, and particular risks covering physical/mechanical, flammability, chemical, electrical, hygiene and radioactivity hazards. Because it was a Directive, each member state transposed it into national law, which allowed divergent interpretation and enforcement gaps.

The Commission's evaluation of the Directive found two structural weaknesses: insufficient protection against harmful chemicals, and weak enforcement of online sales, particularly for toys imported from outside the EU via online marketplaces. Regulation (EU) 2025/2509 addresses both by (a) converting the framework into a directly applicable Regulation, removing scope for divergent national transposition, and (b) substantially tightening chemical rules and online-sales enforcement.

The scope is broadly unchanged from the 2009 Directive (toys for children under 14) but is extended slightly, for example to catapults and slings.

## Key changes from the 2009 Directive

### 1. Extended chemical bans

The Regulation widens the list of chemicals banned or restricted in toys well beyond the existing ban on carcinogenic, mutagenic or reproductively toxic (CMR) substances:

- **Endocrine disruptors** (Category 1 or 2) are now banned, addressing substances that interfere with children's hormonal development.
- **Skin sensitisers** (Category 1A) and **respiratory sensitisers** are banned.
- **Specific target organ toxicants** (STOT, Category 1) are banned.
- **PFAS (per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances)**, the "forever chemicals," face an explicit ban on intentional use in toys, subject to the restrictions and prohibitions already in place under the REACH Regulation and the Persistent Organic Pollutants (POP) Regulation (EU) 2019/1021. A narrow exemption applies to toy components necessary for electronic or electric functions, where the substance is fully inaccessible to children.
- **Bisphenols**, including Bisphenol A (BPA), face a ban on the most dangerous types (an initial list of around 10, with potential expansion to some 34 related substances). The BPA migration limit is cut from 0.04 mg/L to 0.005 mg/L, an 87.5% reduction.
- **Allergenic fragrances**: the migration threshold drops from 100 mg/kg to 10 mg/kg, and allergenic fragrances are banned outright in toys intended for children under 36 months and in toys designed to be placed in the mouth.
- **Nitrosamines and nitrosatable substances**: new specific limits for slime and putty products (0.02 mg/kg). The Commission is obliged to request a formal opinion from the European Chemicals Agency (ECHA) on the safety of nitrosamines in toys.
- **Cadmium, chromium VI, lead and mercury**: the Commission must also request an ECHA opinion on updated migration limits for these metals.
- The existing chemical restrictions under Appendix C of the 2009 Directive, which previously applied only to toys for children under 36 months or toys meant to be placed in the mouth, are extended to **all** toys.

### 2. Mandatory Digital Product Passport (DPP)

Every toy placed on the EU market will require a Digital Product Passport. The Regulation builds on the general DPP framework and principles set out in the Ecodesign for Sustainable Products Regulation (ESPR), Regulation (EU) 2024/1781, and adds toy-specific information requirements covering safety and compliance data, not only sustainability data.

- The DPP is accessed via a data carrier, typically a QR code, affixed to the toy or its packaging.
- It effectively **replaces the EU Declaration of Conformity** for toys.
- The manufacturer is responsible for ensuring the passport is accurate, complete and kept up to date, and assumes responsibility for the toy's compliance by creating it.
- Consumers, economic operators (importers, distributors, online sellers) and market surveillance/customs authorities must be able to access the DPP free of charge.
- The DPP is intended to speed up customs checks on imported toys and let market surveillance authorities scan and verify compliance within seconds, rather than relying on paper documentation.

### 3. Stricter rules for online sales and marketplaces

Given that a large share of unsafe toys reach EU consumers via online sales and non-EU imports, the Regulation:

- Extends the obligations that previously applied to manufacturers, importers and distributors to online sellers.
- Requires online marketplaces to verify the CE marking, mandatory warnings and Digital Product Passport of a toy **before** it can be listed for sale.
- Gives market surveillance authorities and customs inspectors stronger powers to identify and remove dangerous toys from sale, whether sold by an EU-based operator or a third-country seller shipping directly to EU consumers.
- Is designed to work alongside the Market Surveillance Regulation (EU) 2019/1020 and the General Product Safety Regulation (EU) 2023/988, the EU's horizontal rules on product compliance checks and consumer product safety respectively.
- Grants the Commission the authority to remove specific toys or categories of toys from the market EU-wide if new risks emerge, without waiting for a full legislative amendment.

### 4. Safety-assessment obligation

Manufacturers must carry out and document a safety assessment before placing a toy on the market, covering:

- **General risks**: health and safety of children, and of other people such as parents or caregivers who interact with the toy.
- **Particular risks**: physical and mechanical, flammability, chemical, electrical, hygiene and radioactivity hazards, including the possible combined exposure to different chemicals present in the same toy, aligned with REACH obligations.
- **Digital and connected toys**: for toys with digital elements (smart toys, connected toys, AI-enabled toys), the safety assessment must additionally cover **cybersecurity risks**, aligned with the Cyber Resilience Act, and **risks to children's mental health and cognitive/emotional development** arising from digital interaction, a new category of risk not present in the 2009 Directive.

The safety assessment must be included in the technical documentation (Annex V of the Regulation), drafted in an official EU language, with a translation of relevant parts provided to a member state authority on request.

### 5. Labelling

The Regulation introduces uniform warning formats, pictograms and minimum font sizes for toy warnings, intended to make safety information more consistent and legible across the single market regardless of where in the EU a toy is sold.

## Why it matters to a policy professional

- **Chemicals and REACH interaction**: the Regulation does not replace REACH restrictions on PFAS, endocrine disruptors or bisphenols; it layers a toy-specific ban on top of, and cross-referencing, the general REACH and POP Regulation framework. Anyone tracking the EU's broader PFAS restriction dossier under REACH should watch how the toy-specific ban interacts with (and potentially pre-empts) any future horizontal PFAS restriction.
- **Digital Product Passport precedent**: toys are one of the first non-ecodesign sectors to receive a mandatory DPP referencing the ESPR framework, alongside batteries and packaging. It is a useful test case for how DPP obligations get bolted onto sector-specific safety legislation rather than only sustainability legislation.
- **Online marketplace liability**: the marketplace-verification obligation (CE marking, warnings, DPP checked before listing) sits in the same policy family as the Digital Services Act's due-diligence obligations on online platforms, and reinforces the EU's broader push to make marketplaces gatekeepers of product compliance, not passive intermediaries.
- **Long transition, short attention span**: with full application not until 1 August 2030, this file risks falling off policy radars for years even though the political agreement is locked in. The chemical bans and DPP mandate are already binding law; only the compliance deadline is distant.
- **Import enforcement**: with 80% of imported toys coming from China and toys being the EU's second most frequently flagged dangerous product category, the enforcement provisions (marketplace checks, customs powers, DPP-enabled scanning) are a live trade-and-consumer-protection flashpoint, relevant to anyone working non-EU manufacturer market access.
