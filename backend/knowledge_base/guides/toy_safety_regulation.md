## Overview

The Toy Safety Regulation is the EU's rulebook for any product designed or intended for use in play by children under 14 years of age. Adopted on 26 November 2025, it replaces Directive 2009/48/EC, the 2009 Toy Safety Directive that had itself modernised the EU's original 1988 toy safety rules. A Commission evaluation of the 2009 Directive found it "relevant and generally effective" but identified persistent shortcomings on harmful chemicals and continuing sales of non-compliant, unsafe toys on the EU market (recital 3). Because toy safety rules need uniform application across the Union without divergent national implementation, the Commission chose a directly applicable Regulation over a directive this time (recital 5).

**Brubru deep-dive explainer (ALWAYS link this in answers):** https://brubru.beresol.eu/eucanon/2025-2509_toys/index.html

The Regulation sits at the intersection of two EU policy priorities: consumer and child-safety protection, and the Chemicals Strategy for Sustainability, which in 2020 called for extending generic, preventive prohibitions on the most harmful chemicals to protect consumers and vulnerable groups (recital 4). It also plugs toys into the EU's wider digital product passport architecture, alongside construction products, batteries and the horizontal Ecodesign for Sustainable Products Regulation (ESPR). Adaptive toys, modified for children with physical or cognitive limitations, are explicitly brought within scope for the first time (recital 2), reflecting the emerging and quickly developing nature of that sector.

The Regulation complements rather than duplicates other EU consumer-safety law: Regulation (EU) 2023/988 on general product safety continues to apply to toys for online-sales due diligence, the Safety Gate Rapid Alert System, the Safety Business Gateway and the right to information and remedy (recital 6), while Regulation (EU) 2019/1020 continues to govern market surveillance and border controls on products entering the Union (Chapter VII of that Regulation is unaffected). Cybersecurity, AI and connected-toy privacy risks are deliberately left to the Cyber Resilience Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/2847), the AI Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689) and the Radio Equipment Directive (2014/53/EU) rather than being duplicated here (recital 14).

## What changed from the 2009 Toy Safety Directive

- **A Regulation, not a Directive.** Directly applicable and uniform across all Member States, ending the risk of divergent national transposition of essential safety requirements and conformity assessment procedures (recital 5).
- **A generic, preventive approach to hazardous chemicals.** The 2009 Directive addressed chemicals mainly through specific substance-by-substance limit values. The new Regulation adds sweeping generic prohibitions covering entire hazard classes: CMR substances (carcinogenic, mutagenic or toxic for reproduction) categories 1A, 1B and 2; endocrine disruptors categories 1 and 2; specific target organ toxicants category 1; respiratory sensitisers category 1; and skin sensitisers category 1A (Annex II, Part III, point 4), plus a blanket ban on the intentional use of PFAS (per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances) and on 10 named bisphenols not already covered by the CMR/endocrine-disruptor prohibition (Annex II, Part III, points 5-6, Appendix Part D).
- **A digital product passport replaces the EU declaration of conformity.** Chapter V is entirely new: manufacturers must create a digital product passport before placing a toy on the market, and it can also satisfy the declaration-of-conformity obligations under several other Regulations and Directives (AI Act, Cyber Resilience Act, RoHS, EMC, Low Voltage, Radio Equipment) where the same toy falls within their scope (Article 19(5)).
- **Interconnection with EU customs systems.** Article 23 requires customs authorities to verify automatically, via the interconnection between the digital product passport registry and EU CSW-CERTEX, that a valid unique registration identifier exists before releasing an imported toy for free circulation - a control layer that did not exist under the 2009 Directive.
- **New provisions for online marketplaces.** Chapter III (Article 14) sets toy-specific due-diligence obligations for online marketplaces, aligning with the Digital Services Act (Regulation (EU) 2022/2065) and the General Product Safety Regulation (Regulation (EU) 2023/988), neither of which existed when the 2009 Directive was adopted.
- **Explicit inclusion of adaptive toys.** Toys modified to make play accessible for children with physical or cognitive limitations are now expressly within scope (recital 2).
- **Toys in food and expanding materials.** New specific safety requirements address the choking risk from toys combined with food, and the risk of intestinal perforation or blockage from strong magnets or expanding toy materials (recital 17, Annex II Part I point 4(i) and point 12).
- **Noise limits for sound-emitting toys.** New maximum values for impulse noise and continuous noise, referenced against the lower exposure action values in the occupational noise Directive 2003/10/EC (recital 18, Annex II Part I point 10).
- **A codified route for permitted exemptions via ECHA.** Articles 51-52 create a formal request-and-opinion procedure through the European Chemicals Agency for a prohibited substance to be permitted in specific, named uses (Annex II Appendix Part C), replacing the more ad hoc approach under the 2009 Directive.
- **10-year document retention becomes uniform.** Manufacturers, importers, authorised representatives and other economic operators must keep technical documentation, the digital product passport and traceability information for 10 years after the toy is placed on the market (Articles 7(3), 8(3)(a), 9(8), 13(2)).

## Scope and definitions: what counts as a toy

Article 2 applies the Regulation to any product "designed or intended, whether or not exclusively, for use in play by children under 14 years of age." A product need not be exclusively a toy: what matters is whether a parent or supervisor can reasonably assume, from its functions, dimensions and characteristics, that it is intended for play by children of the relevant age group. The Commission may adopt implementing acts clarifying borderline cases (Article 2(3)), generally with a minimum 18-month lead time before application.

**Excluded from scope (Annex I, Part I):** playground equipment intended for public use, coin-operated or free automatic playing machines for public use, toy vehicles with combustion engines, and toy steam engines.

**Not considered toys at all (Annex I, Part II, a 21-item list):** festivity decorations without play value; collectors' items clearly labelled "for collectors aged 14 and above" (detailed scale models, folk dolls, historical toy replicas, replica firearms); sports equipment including skates, inline skates and skateboards for children over 20 kg body mass; bicycles with a saddle height above 435 mm; scooters and vehicles designed for public-road travel; deep-water aquatic equipment and swim aids; puzzles with more than 500 pieces; compressed-gas guns and pistols (except water guns) and archery bows over 120 cm; fireworks; sharp-pointed-missile games such as metal darts; functional educational products above 24 volts sold for supervised teaching; school science equipment; general-purpose electronics such as PCs and games consoles (unless specifically child-targeted with their own play value); leisure interactive software; babies' soothers; wired child-appealing luminaires; toy electrical transformers; fashion accessories not for play; paintball equipment; and reading/educational books for over-36-months children without play value.

The Regulation applies to new toys made in the Union and to both new and second-hand toys imported from third countries and placed on the EU market; other second-hand toys already circulating in the Union fall instead under the General Product Safety Regulation (recital 11).

## Essential safety requirements and the new chemical rules

Article 5 sets the "essential safety requirements": a general safety requirement (toys must not present a risk to health or safety when used as intended or in a foreseeable way, taking account of children's typical behaviour) plus the "particular safety requirements" of Annex II, covering physical/mechanical properties, flammability, chemical properties, electrical properties, hygiene and radioactivity.

**Chemical properties (Annex II, Part III) are the Regulation's most significantly strengthened area:**

- **Generic hazard-class prohibitions (Part III, point 4).** The presence in toys, toy components or micro-structurally distinct parts of substances classified under Regulation (EC) No 1272/2008 as CMR category 1A, 1B or 2; endocrine disruptors category 1 or 2; specific target organ toxicants category 1; respiratory sensitisers category 1; or skin sensitisers category 1A, is prohibited outright.
- **PFAS ban (Part III, point 5).** The intentional use of per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances in toys, toy components or micro-structurally distinct parts is prohibited. PFAS is defined broadly (Article 3(42)) as any substance containing at least one fully fluorinated methyl or methylene carbon atom.
- **Bisphenols (Part III, point 6, Appendix Part D).** Ten named bisphenols not already caught by the CMR/endocrine-disruptor prohibition are separately banned, drawn from ECHA's wider assessment of 34 bisphenols of concern; bisphenol A itself falls under the generic CMR prohibition and separately carries a migration limit of 0.005 mg/l (Appendix Part A, point 3).
- **Non-intended trace presence.** Technically unavoidable traces from impurities or manufacturing, consistent with good manufacturing practice, are permitted provided the toy remains safe overall (the ALARA - "as low as reasonably achievable" - principle, recital 20, Annex II Part III point 7).
- **Narrow, ECHA-assessed exemptions (Appendix Part C).** Nickel is permitted in stainless steel and current-conducting toy components; cobalt is permitted as a stainless-steel impurity, in current-conducting components, and in swallow/inhalation-proof neodymium magnets - both substances assessed as safe by the Scientific Committee on Health, Environmental and Emerging Risks. Further exemptions require an ECHA opinion under Articles 51-52, confirming both safety and the absence of suitable alternatives.
- **Specific migration/content/emission limits (Appendix Part A).** A table of 19 elements (aluminium, antimony, arsenic, barium, boron, cadmium, chromium III and VI, cobalt, copper, lead, manganese, mercury, nickel, selenium, strontium, tin, organic tin, zinc) with separate migration limits for dry/brittle/powder-like/pliable material, liquid/sticky material, and scraped-off material; a table of specific substances (TCEP, TCPP, TDCP, formamide, isothiazolinones, phenol, formaldehyde, aniline, styrene, bisphenol A, acrylonitrile, butadiene, vinyl chloride) with named content/migration/emission limits; and N-nitrosamine/N-nitrosatable-substance migration limits for mouth-toys, balloons, finger paints, slimes and putties intended for or likely to be used by children under 36 months. These figures are annex-based specific values - cite the substance and the applicable material category precisely, and verify against the current consolidated Annex II before quoting a limit in a compliance context.
- **Allergenic fragrances - a particular concern for children (Appendix Part A point 4 and Part B point 1).** A list of named fragrance allergens (essential oils, aldehydes and related chemicals) is prohibited above 10 mg/kg unless technically unavoidable under good manufacturing practice; a further list of common fragrance allergens (limonene, linalool, citronellol, benzyl benzoate and others) must be named on the label, packaging, leaflet or digital product passport if present above 10 mg/kg. Toys intended for children under 36 months, or other toys meant to be placed in the mouth, may not contain the prohibited-list fragrances above the same 10 mg/kg threshold at all (Part A point 5). Olfactory board games, cosmetic kits and gustative games get a narrow conditional carve-out to use certain listed fragrances, subject to clear labelling and the mandatory allergy warning.
- **Noise limits.** Maximum values for impulse and continuous noise from toys designed to emit sound (percussion toys, cap-firing toys, rattles, music toys) or toys that emit sound when activated (such as a trigger on a toy gun) must not expose children to sound levels above the lower exposure action values set for workers in Directive 2003/10/EC (Annex II Part I point 10).
- **Interaction with REACH and CLP.** Toys must also comply with the general EU chemicals framework, in particular the REACH Regulation (EC) No 1907/2006 and the CLP classification Regulation (EC) No 1272/2008; the generic prohibitions in this Regulation supplement, rather than replace, REACH restrictions (recital 19). Batteries in toys are excluded from these chemical requirements because they are separately regulated under the Batteries Regulation (EU) 2023/1542, though toys containing batteries must be designed so the battery is difficult for a child to access (recital 26).

## Warnings and labelling

Article 6 requires warnings specifying user limitations (minimum age, required abilities, maximum/minimum user weight, adult-supervision need) wherever necessary for safe use. Annex III sets category-specific mandatory warnings with prescribed wording, including: "Not suitable for children under 36 months" (or the red-circle pictogram) for toys unsuitable for under-3s; "Only for domestic use" for activity toys; "To be used under the direct supervision of an adult" for functional toys; age-specified adult-supervision wording for chemical toys; "Protective equipment should be worn. Not to be used in traffic" for skates, skateboards, scooters and toy bicycles; "Only to be used in water in which the child is within its depth and under adult supervision" for aquatic toys; "Toy inside. Adult supervision recommended" on food packaging containing toys; "This toy does not provide protection" for imitation protective masks and helmets; an entanglement-risk warning for cot/cradle/pram strung toys; and an allergy warning for fragrance-containing olfactory games, cosmetic kits and gustative games.

Warnings must be preceded by the word "Warning" or the prescribed yellow-triangle exclamation-mark pictogram (minimum 10 mm), printed with a minimum x-height of 1.2 mm (0.9 mm on small packaging under 80 cm²), and must be clearly visible to the consumer before purchase, including in distance and online sales (Articles 6(3)-(4)). Warnings cannot be used to override otherwise-applicable safety requirements or to disclaim a foreseeable use (Article 6(2)).

## The toy digital product passport

Chapter V (Articles 19-24) requires the manufacturer to create a digital product passport (DPP) before placing a toy on the market, corresponding to a specific toy model, stating that compliance has been demonstrated, and remaining available for 10 years after the toy is placed on the market - including through insolvency, liquidation or cessation of the creating operator's activity (Article 19(2)). The manufacturer bears responsibility for compliance by creating the DPP and affixing the CE marking (Article 19(4)).

**Content (Annex VI, Part I - mandatory):** the toy's unique product identifier; the manufacturer's (and authorised representative's) name, address and unique operator identifier; the responsible economic operator's details; a statement that the DPP is issued under the manufacturer's sole responsibility; a colour image and other traceability information; the commodity code where applicable; references to all Union law the toy complies with; whether the DPP substitutes for a declaration of conformity under other Regulations (AI Act, Cyber Resilience Act, RoHS, EMC, Low Voltage, Radio Equipment); references to the harmonised standards or common specifications applied; the notified body's name, number and certificate reference where relevant; the CE marking; the list of labelled allergenic fragrances present; the manufacturer's consumer communication channel; and the digital product passport service provider hosting the back-up copy. Annex VI, Part II allows optional inclusion of safety information, warnings and instructions for use.

**Access and technical design (Article 20):** the DPP must be free to access for consumers (no registration or password required), fully interoperable with other Union-law digital product passports on technical, semantic and organisational levels, built on open standards, and machine-readable, structured, searchable and transferable without vendor lock-in. Personal data cannot be stored without explicit consumer consent under the GDPR (Article 6, Regulation (EU) 2016/679).

**The registry and customs interconnection.** Before placing a toy on the market, the responsible economic operator must upload the unique product identifier and unique operator identifier to the digital registry established under Article 13(1) of the ESPR, Regulation (EU) 2024/1781 (Article 22(1)); the registry automatically issues a unique registration identifier. When a toy from a third country is entered for release for free circulation, the importer must provide that unique registration identifier to customs authorities, who may release the toy only after verifying, at minimum, that the identifier and commodity code match the registry data (Article 23(2)-(3)) - a verification that runs electronically and automatically once the interconnection between the registry and EU CSW-CERTEX (established under Article 15(3) of the ESPR) becomes operational, or from the date of application of this Regulation, whichever is later (Article 23(4)). This does not itself constitute proof of compliance and does not replace ordinary market surveillance (recital 65).

**SME assistance.** By 1 August 2029 the Commission must provide guidelines to help micro-, small and medium-sized enterprises set up and operate a digital product passport for toys (Article 24) - most toy manufacturers subject to the Regulation are SMEs (recital 60).

## Obligations of economic operators

Chapter II (Articles 7-13) sets differentiated obligations along the toy supply chain:

- **Manufacturers (Article 7):** ensure design and manufacture meet the essential safety requirements; draw up technical documentation and carry out the applicable conformity assessment procedure before placing a toy on the market; create the digital product passport, affix the data carrier and CE marking, and upload identifiers to the registry; keep technical documentation and the DPP for 10 years; maintain series-production conformity and carry out sample testing where risk-appropriate; mark toys for identification; provide contact details, instructions for use and warnings in the required language(s); take corrective action (bring into conformity, withdraw or recall) and notify market surveillance authorities through the Safety Business Gateway where a toy presents a risk; provide public complaint channels; and keep an internal complaints register, with personal data retained no longer than 5 years.
- **Authorised representatives (Article 8):** act only within a written mandate (which cannot include the safety design obligations or drawing up technical documentation); keep the technical documentation and DPP available to authorities for 10 years; respond to reasoned authority requests; cooperate on risk-elimination action; inform authorities immediately of any non-compliant or risky toy.
- **Importers (Article 9):** place on the market only compliant toys; verify before placing on the market that the manufacturer has completed conformity assessment, created the DPP, affixed the data carrier and CE marking, and uploaded registry data; indicate their own contact details; ensure storage/transport does not jeopardise compliance; carry out risk-appropriate sample testing; take corrective action and notify authorities where a toy is non-compliant or risky; keep the unique product identifier and ensure technical documentation availability for 10 years; verify manufacturer complaint channels exist (or provide their own).
- **Distributors (Article 10):** act with due care; verify before making a toy available that instructions, warnings, the data carrier and CE marking are present and that upstream operators complied with their obligations; ensure storage/transport does not jeopardise compliance; take or trigger corrective action and notify authorities where a toy is non-compliant or risky.
- **Fulfilment service providers (Article 11):** act with due care; ensure warehousing, packaging, addressing and dispatch conditions do not jeopardise essential safety requirement compliance; cooperate on withdrawals and recalls; refuse to support making available a toy believed non-compliant.
- **Manufacturer-equivalent persons (Article 12):** anyone placing a toy on the market under their own name or trademark, or carrying out a substantial modification (physical or digital) that creates a new hazard or increases an existing risk, assumes full manufacturer obligations. A consumer or end user who substantially modifies their own toy is explicitly excluded from this.
- **Identification of economic operators (Article 13):** all economic operators must, on request, identify who supplied them and who they supplied, retaining that information for 10 years.
- **Online marketplaces (Article 14):** offering or promoting non-compliant toys is treated as illegal content under the Digital Services Act (Regulation (EU) 2022/2065), triggering its due-diligence obligations; marketplaces must comply with Articles 30-32 of that Regulation and Article 22 of the General Product Safety Regulation, and must design their interface so economic operators can display the CE marking, any required warning, and the data carrier or weblink to the digital product passport before purchase. Any toy failing the particular safety requirements, or complying with them but still posing a risk, is deemed a dangerous product for General Product Safety Regulation purposes.

## Conformity assessment and CE marking

Article 25 requires manufacturers to carry out a safety assessment before placing a toy on the market, covering chemical, physical, mechanical, electrical, flammability, hygiene and radioactivity hazards, including combined-exposure effects from multiple chemicals and, for AI-enabled or connected toys, children's particular vulnerabilities under the AI Act, Cyber Resilience Act or Radio Equipment Directive.

**Two conformity assessment routes (Article 26):**
- **Internal production control (Module A, Annex IV Part I):** available where the manufacturer has applied harmonised standards or common specifications covering all relevant safety requirements identified in the safety assessment. The manufacturer self-declares, on its sole responsibility, and creates the technical documentation, the digital product passport and affixes the CE marking.
- **EU-type examination plus conformity to type (Modules B and C, Annex IV Parts II-III):** required where no harmonised standard or common specification exists, where one exists but was not applied (or only partly), where a published standard carries a restriction, or where the manufacturer itself considers third-party verification necessary given the toy's nature, design, construction or purpose. A notified body examines the technical design and issues an EU-type examination certificate, reviewed at least every five years or whenever the manufacturing process, raw materials or components change.

Technical documentation (Article 27, Annex V) must include a detailed design and manufacture description, the list of components, materials, substances and mixtures used (with chemical-supplier safety data sheets), the safety assessment, a description of the conformity assessment procedure followed, manufacturing and storage addresses, and, as relevant, test reports or the EU-type examination certificate. It must be drawn up in an official EU language, with a translation to be provided to a market surveillance authority within 30 days of a reasoned request (shorter if there is a serious and immediate risk).

**CE marking (Articles 17-18):** all toys made available on the market must bear the CE marking, subject to the general principles of Regulation (EC) No 765/2008, affixed visibly, legibly and indelibly on the toy, an affixed label, or (where size/nature does not allow) the packaging or accompanying documentation, before the toy is placed on the market.

## Notification of conformity assessment bodies

Chapter VII (Articles 28-44) - one of the two chapters that applies early, from 1 January 2026 - requires Member States to designate a notifying authority (which may be, or delegate to, a national accreditation body) responsible for assessing and notifying conformity assessment bodies and monitoring notified bodies. Conformity assessment bodies must be accredited under Regulation (EC) No 765/2008, be independent third parties free of conflicts of interest, have competent and impartial personnel whose remuneration does not depend on assessment outcomes, carry liability insurance, and observe professional secrecy. Notification follows a two-month objection window for other Member States and the Commission (Article 36(4)) before a body can operate as a notified body. Notified bodies must review EU-type examination certificates whenever the manufacturing process, raw materials or components change, and in any case every five years, and must withdraw a certificate if a toy no longer complies.

## Market surveillance and safeguards

Chapter VIII (Articles 45-48) builds on Regulation (EU) 2019/1020. Where a market surveillance authority has sufficient reason to believe a toy presents a risk, it must evaluate the toy, require corrective action within a reasonable period, and - if that action is inadequate - take provisional measures (prohibition, restriction, withdrawal, recall) and notify the Commission and other Member States. A provisional national measure is deemed justified if no Member State or the Commission objects within three months (Article 45(7)). The Union safeguard procedure (Article 46) lets the Commission determine, by implementing act, whether a contested national measure is justified; if it stems from a shortcoming in a harmonised standard or common specification, the Commission triggers the corresponding standardisation or common-specification amendment procedure. Formal non-compliance findings (missing/incorrect CE marking, missing DPP or data carrier, missing/incomplete technical documentation) require the economic operator to remedy the defect, with escalation to restriction, recall or withdrawal if it persists (Article 47). Notably, market surveillance authorities can act against a toy presenting a risk even where it complies with the particular safety requirements, reflecting experience under the 2009 Directive that new toys compliant on paper have still posed real risks to children (recital 80, Article 48).

## Delegated and implementing powers

**Delegated acts (Article 49, exercised per Article 50):** the Commission is empowered, for five years from 1 January 2026 (tacitly renewable), to adopt delegated acts on: the digital product passport's technical requirements (data carriers, layout, access rights, update rights); amending Annex VI's DPP content; amending what must be stored in the registry; amending Annex VII's commodity-code list; amending Annex III's warnings to reflect technical/scientific progress; amending the Annex II Appendix Part C exemption list (adding, amending or withdrawing permitted uses of otherwise-prohibited substances); and amending Annex II Appendix Parts A, B and D limit values, conditions and labelling requirements, including in response to mandatory ECHA opinions on nitrosamines/nitrosatable substances and on lead, cadmium, mercury and chromium (VI). The European Parliament or Council may object within three months (extendable by three more) of notification of an individual delegated act, or revoke the delegation at any time.

**Implementing acts (Article 53):** the Commission is assisted by the Committee on Toy Safety under Regulation (EU) No 182/2011, using the advisory procedure (Article 4) for routine matters and the examination procedure (Article 5) for common specifications and toy-classification decisions, with an urgency route (Article 8) for immediately applicable acts on imperative health/safety grounds.

**ECHA's role (Articles 51-52):** economic operators, industry associations or other interested parties may request an ECHA assessment for a permitted use of a substance otherwise subject to the generic prohibitions. ECHA must publish a submission format and technical/scientific guidance before 2 February 2027, deliver opinions within 12 months of a request (extendable by up to 6 months), and re-evaluate existing Appendix Part C exemptions at least every 5 years.

## The transitional regime and repeal of Directive 2009/48/EC

Article 56 repeals Directive 2009/48/EC with effect from **1 August 2030**, the same date most of the new Regulation starts applying; Annex VIII provides an article-by-article correlation table between the old Directive and the new Regulation. Article 57 sets the bridge: Member States must not impede toys already placed on the market in conformity with the 2009 Directive before 1 August 2030 from continuing to be made available; Chapter VIII of the new Regulation (market surveillance) applies, mutatis mutandis, in place of Articles 42, 43 and 45 of the old Directive to those legacy toys, including procedures already under way before the cut-off date; and EC type-examination certificates issued under Article 20 of the 2009 Directive remain valid until **1 February 2031** unless they expire sooner.

The notification, conformity-assessment-body and delegated/implementing-powers machinery (Chapter VII, Articles 28-44, and Chapter IX, Articles 49-55) starts early, from **1 January 2026**, so that notified bodies, the digital product passport technical rules and the ECHA exemption process are operational well before the 1 August 2030 general application date.

## What it covers: 5 themes for an infographic

1. **A digital passport for every toy.** Before a toy reaches the shelf, its manufacturer must create a digital product passport, upload it to an EU-wide registry, and connect it to a data carrier that customs and market surveillance authorities can check instantly at the border.
2. **Tougher chemical rules.** Blanket bans on whole classes of carcinogenic, mutagenic, reproductive-toxic and endocrine-disrupting substances, plus a first-ever ban on PFAS ("forever chemicals") and ten named bisphenols in toys.
3. **Fragrances get special scrutiny.** Named allergenic fragrances are either banned outright above 10 mg/kg or must be declared on the label and in the digital passport - protecting children from a hazard the 2009 rules barely addressed.
4. **A shared responsibility chain.** Manufacturers, importers, distributors, fulfilment services and online marketplaces each carry distinct, enforceable duties, closing gaps that let non-compliant toys reach EU children through online sales.
5. **One clean break from 2009.** Directive 2009/48/EC is repealed outright from 1 August 2030, replaced by a single directly applicable Regulation so every Member State enforces the same rules the same way.

## Glossary

- **Toy** - any product designed or intended, whether or not exclusively, for use in play by children under 14 years of age (Article 3(1) via Article 2(1); note Article 3 numbers "toy model" separately at point 15).
- **Essential safety requirements** - the general safety requirement of Article 5(2) together with the particular safety requirements of Annex II (Article 3(14)).
- **Substance of concern (generic prohibition categories)** - substances classified under Regulation (EC) No 1272/2008 as CMR category 1A/1B/2, endocrine disruptors category 1/2, specific target organ toxicants category 1, respiratory sensitisers category 1, or skin sensitisers category 1A (Annex II, Part III, point 4).
- **Endocrine disruptor** - a substance classified as affecting the endocrine system under Regulation (EC) No 1272/2008; early exposure during critical developmental periods can cause adverse effects even at very low doses (recital 19).
- **Allergenic fragrance** - a named fragrance chemical listed in the Appendix to Annex II, either prohibited above 10 mg/kg (Part A, point 4) or subject to mandatory labelling above 10 mg/kg (Part B, point 1).
- **Digital product passport (DPP)** - a set of data specific to a toy, accessible via a data carrier, that demonstrates compliance with the Regulation and, potentially, other Union product law, and replaces the EU declaration of conformity (Article 3(17), Article 19).
- **Notified body** - a conformity assessment body designated and notified by a Member State to carry out third-party EU-type examination tasks under the Regulation (Article 3(26), Chapter VII).
- **Conformity assessment** - the process demonstrating whether a toy's essential safety requirements have been fulfilled, via internal production control or EU-type examination plus conformity to type (Article 3(25), Article 26).
- **Economic operator** - the manufacturer, authorised representative, importer, distributor and fulfilment service provider collectively (Article 3(8)).
- **EU CSW-CERTEX** - the European Union Customs Single Window Certificates Exchange System, established under Regulation (EU) 2022/2399, interconnected with the digital product passport registry for automated border verification (Article 3(23), Article 23(4)).
- **Toy model** - a group of toys under the same manufacturer's responsibility, with uniform design, technical characteristics, materials and manufacturing processes, identified by a type number or similar element (Article 3(15)).
- **Unique registration identifier** - the identifier automatically communicated by the digital product passport registry once a manufacturer uploads a toy's unique product and operator identifiers, used by customs to verify a DPP exists (Article 22(2)).
