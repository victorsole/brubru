# State liability for a court's breach of EU law — C-163/24 and C-293/24

## QUICK FACTS
- **Two judgments of the Court of Justice, both 8 September 2026**: **C-163/24** *Statul Român –
  Ministerul Finanţelor Publice and Curtea de Apel Bucureşti* (Romania) and **C-293/24**
  *Ferreira da Silva e Brito and Others* (Portugal). CURIA press release **No 120/26**
- These develop the **Köbler** line: a Member State can be liable in damages for a breach of EU law
  committed by a **court of last instance**
- **Three cumulative conditions**, restated: (1) the rule breached **confers rights on individuals**,
  (2) the breach is **sufficiently serious**, (3) a **direct causal link** to the loss
- **THE HEADLINE HOLDING (C-163/24): failing to make a preliminary reference is NOT, on its own,
  enough to trigger liability.** It must be accompanied by breach of **another** rule of EU law that
  confers rights on individuals
- **A rule that protects only the Union's financial interests confers no rights on individuals** —
  so the Romanian farmer's claim failed at condition (1)
- **C-293/24: the Transfer of Undertakings Directive DOES confer rights**, so condition (1) was met
  and the case turned on "sufficiently serious"
- **Three factors the Court called decisive** for "sufficiently serious": the answer was already clear
  from prior case-law; the court departed from it **without referring** despite the obligation; and it
  could not have been unaware that the very question was **already pending** before the Court
- Whether the breach is sufficiently serious, and the causal link, remain **for the referring court to
  verify** — the Court of Justice strongly signalled the condition is met but did not decide it
- Do not state that either case awarded damages. Neither did

## Why these matter

State liability for judicial breach has been formally available since **Köbler (C-224/01, 2003)** and
almost never succeeds. These two judgments are the Court doing two things at once: **closing one route**
(a bare failure to refer) and **opening a realistic one** (a failure to refer *plus* a rights-conferring
rule, evidenced by the three decisive factors). Read together they are the clearest map the Court has
given of when an individual harmed by a final judgment can actually recover.

## C-163/24 — Romania, agricultural aid

A farmer applied in 2007 for aid on 264.71 ha; the authority found only 180.62 ha eligible and refused.
His action and appeal were dismissed, and in **2012 the Court of Appeal, Bucharest refused to refer** a
question on the integrated administration and control system regulation. He then sued the Romanian State.

**Held:** liability **cannot** be triggered by the failure to refer alone. It must be accompanied by
breach of another rule intended to confer rights on individuals. Here the provision at issue had the
**sole objective of protecting the Union's financial interests**, so it conferred no rights on an aid
applicant, and the claim failed at the first condition.

## C-293/24 — Portugal, Air Atlantis

Employees dismissed on the 1993 dissolution of **Air Atlantis** argued that Transportes Aéreos
Portugueses had taken over part of its activities, a **transfer of a business** under the Transfer of
Undertakings Directive. In **2009 the Portuguese Supreme Court refused to refer** and confirmed the
dismissal of their action. On a later reference the Court of Justice ruled in **2015** that there had
been a transfer **and** that the Supreme Court should have referred. The national courts nonetheless
dismissed the workers' claim, reasoning that the 2015 ruling relied on case-law postdating 2009.

**Held:** the Directive's provisions **do** confer rights on individuals, so condition (1) is satisfied.
On "sufficiently serious", the Court identified **three decisive factors**:

1. The 2015 solution **was already apparent from case-law predating** the 2009 decision, which combined
   several errors of interpretation.
2. The Supreme Court **departed from that prior case-law without referring**, despite its obligation.
3. It **could not have been unaware** that the conformity of its own methodological approach was at that
   moment the subject of a **pending reference** before the Court — which makes the breach **inexcusable**.

The 2009 decision therefore "appears to be vitiated by a sufficiently serious breach", **but the
referring court must verify** that and the causal link.

## How to answer questions on this

- Never say "failing to refer makes the State liable". After C-163/24 that is wrong: the failure must
  be **accompanied by** breach of a rights-conferring rule.
- The **nature of the rule decides the case more often than the seriousness test.** A rule protecting
  the Union budget confers no individual rights; a worker-protection directive does.
- Distinguish the **obligation to refer** (Article 267(3) TFEU, CILFIT) from **liability** for breaching
  it. They are related and not the same, and these judgments are precisely about the gap between them.
- Neither judgment awarded compensation. Both leave the final assessment to the national court.
