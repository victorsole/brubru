# EDPB and the GDPR Consistency Mechanism (One-Stop-Shop, Articles 60-66)

## QUICK FACTS
- Body: European Data Protection Board (EDPB), Brussels-based, independent EU body with legal personality
- Legal basis: GDPR (Regulation (EU) 2016/679, CELEX 32016R0679) Articles 68-76 (Board) and Articles 60-66 (cooperation and consistency mechanism)
- Established: 25 May 2018, replacing the Article 29 Working Party
- Composition: heads of the national supervisory authorities (Data Protection Authorities, DPAs) of all EEA countries, plus the European Data Protection Supervisor (EDPS); the European Commission participates without a voting right
- Chair: Anu Talus (since 2022); Deputy Chairs: Jelena Virant Burnik and Zdravko Vukić; officers serve five-year terms
- Secretariat: provided by the EDPS, handles day-to-day operations
- Core mechanisms it runs: cross-border cooperation (Article 60), mutual assistance (Article 61), joint operations (Article 62), the consistency mechanism (Article 63), Board opinions (Article 64), binding dispute-resolution decisions (Article 65), and the urgency procedure (Article 66)
- One-stop-shop (OSS): for organisations with establishments in multiple Member States, a single lead supervisory authority (determined by the location of the controller's or processor's main establishment) coordinates cross-border cases; concerned supervisory authorities (CSAs) participate throughout
- Ireland's Data Protection Commission (DPC) is lead authority for most large US tech platforms with EU headquarters in Dublin (Meta, TikTok, X/Twitter, and, since 15 February 2024, OpenAI Ireland Limited); this concentration is the main criticism of the OSS system, driving complaints from NOYB (Max Schrems' European Center for Digital Rights) about enforcement delay
- Notable Article 65 binding decisions: Binding Decision 1/2021 (WhatsApp Ireland, transparency, led to a EUR 225 million fine); Binding Decision 3/2022 and follow-ups on Meta/Facebook/Instagram legal basis for behavioural advertising (Meta fined EUR 390 million in January 2023 and separately EUR 1.2 billion in May 2023 over EU-US transfers); Binding Decision 2/2023 (TikTok Technology Limited, children's data and dark patterns, DPC fine EUR 345 million, September 2023)
- Companies with no EU main establishment (e.g. Clearview AI) fall outside the OSS mechanism entirely; each national DPA enforces independently, which is why French, Italian, Greek and Dutch authorities each fined Clearview AI separately (2022-2024) rather than through a single lead-authority decision
- Major reform: the GDPR Procedural Regulation, **Regulation (EU) 2025/2518**, laying down additional procedural rules for cross-border GDPR enforcement, was formally adopted after Council and Parliament reached agreement in 2025 and published in the Official Journal on 12 December 2025. It enters into force 1 January 2026 and applies from **2 April 2027**. It introduces harmonised complaint-admissibility criteria, fixed deadlines for lead-authority draft decisions (in principle 15 months from confirmation of competence, extendable once), harmonised due-process rights for parties under investigation, and an early-resolution mechanism for rectified, non-contested complaints.

## Overview

The consistency mechanism is the enforcement plumbing behind the GDPR's promise of a single EU market for data protection. Without it, a company operating across 27 Member States could face 27 different investigations, 27 different findings and 27 different fines for the same conduct. GDPR Chapter VII (Articles 60-76) instead channels cross-border cases through one lead supervisory authority, with the European Data Protection Board acting as referee when national authorities disagree, and as the EU-level standard-setter through guidelines, opinions and binding decisions.

Understanding this mechanism matters for any organisation facing, or advising on, a cross-border GDPR investigation, and for anyone assessing why enforcement outcomes (fine size, timing, scope of findings) can differ so much from the substantive rules the GDPR sets out.

## The One-Stop-Shop: How a Lead Authority Is Determined

Article 56 GDPR designates the supervisory authority of the controller's or processor's "main establishment" (or "single establishment") as the lead supervisory authority (LSA) for cross-border processing. Main establishment is the place of central administration in the EU, or, if decisions on the purposes and means of processing are taken elsewhere, the establishment that takes those decisions. A company with no EU establishment at all has no LSA and falls outside the OSS mechanism, meaning every national DPA where it processes data can investigate and fine independently (the Clearview AI pattern, below).

Because so many large US technology companies base their EU headquarters in Ireland, the Irish DPC has become LSA for the overwhelming majority of "big tech" GDPR cases: Meta (Facebook, Instagram, WhatsApp), TikTok, X (formerly Twitter), and, since OpenAI Ireland Limited was recognised as OpenAI's single EEA establishment on 15 February 2024, OpenAI. Other DPAs that had opened parallel investigations into OpenAI transferred their files to Dublin once that establishment was confirmed. This concentration is the central structural criticism of the OSS system: NOYB and other civil-society complainants argue that a single, resource-constrained authority cannot process the volume of complaints against the world's largest platforms quickly enough, producing a de facto enforcement bottleneck.

## Article 60: Cooperation Between the Lead and Concerned Authorities

The LSA cooperates with every "concerned supervisory authority" (CSA), meaning any DPA in a Member State where data subjects are substantially affected, where a complaint was lodged, or which has been notified by the LSA. The LSA must submit a draft decision to the CSAs and take "due account" of their views; if a CSA raises a "relevant and reasoned objection," the LSA either follows it or refers the matter to the EDPB under Article 65.

## Article 61: Mutual Assistance

Supervisory authorities must provide each other with relevant information and carry out investigative measures (inspections, information requests) on request, subject to defined response deadlines. A DPA that fails to provide assistance within one month can be challenged before the EDPB.

## Article 62: Joint Operations

Supervisory authorities may conduct joint investigations and joint enforcement measures, including cross-border task forces, with members of other DPAs given investigative powers under the host DPA's law.

## Article 63: The Consistency Mechanism

Article 63 is the umbrella provision: to contribute to consistent GDPR application across the Union, supervisory authorities cooperate through the mechanisms in Articles 64 (opinions) and 65 (dispute resolution), coordinated by the EDPB.

## Article 64: Opinion of the Board

The EDPB must issue an opinion before a supervisory authority adopts certain measures with EU-wide effect, including: approval of criteria for accreditation of certification bodies or codes of conduct; determination of standard contractual clauses; approval of binding corporate rules (BCRs); and determination of whether a draft list of processing operations subject to a Data Protection Impact Assessment is consistent across the Union. Opinions are adopted by simple majority; a DPA that intends to depart from an opinion must justify it and may trigger further Board scrutiny.

## Article 65: Dispute Resolution: Binding Decisions

Where a relevant and reasoned objection from a CSA is not resolved through Article 60 cooperation, or where a DPA disputes competence, or in specified conflicting-opinion scenarios, the matter is referred to the EDPB, which adopts a **binding decision** by two-thirds majority within one month (extendable by one further month for complex cases). The LSA's final decision must be based on the EDPB's binding decision and issued within one month of it. Article 65 decisions are the EDPB's sharpest tool and the ones that produce headline fines: the LSA drafts a decision, one or more CSAs formally object, the EDPB is asked to arbitrate, and the LSA then adopts a final decision reflecting the Board's ruling. Recent examples: Binding Decision 1/2021 on WhatsApp Ireland's transparency obligations; the 2022 Meta/Facebook/Instagram binding decisions on legal basis for behavioural advertising; and Binding Decision 2/2023 on TikTok's processing of children's data and "dark pattern" design, where the EDPB required the Irish DPC to add a fairness-principle (Article 5(1)(a)) infringement finding requested by the Berlin data protection authority, ahead of the DPC's final EUR 345 million fine.

## Article 66: Urgency Procedure

In exceptional circumstances where a supervisory authority considers there is an urgent need to protect data subjects' rights, it may adopt provisional measures in its own territory valid for a maximum of three months, and may request an urgent opinion or urgent binding decision from the EDPB, which must respond within two weeks.

## Governance of the Board (Articles 68-76)

Article 68 establishes the EDPB as a body with legal personality, chaired by an elected Chair supported by two Deputy Chairs (five-year renewable terms). Article 69 guarantees independence from instruction by any government or body. Article 70 lists the Board's tasks: monitoring and ensuring correct GDPR application, advising the Commission, issuing guidelines/recommendations/best practices (on its own initiative or on request), issuing opinions under Article 64, adopting binding decisions under Article 65, promoting cooperation and training, and maintaining a public register of national and Union decisions on GDPR issues. Article 71 requires an annual report on the Board's activities, published and forwarded to the European Parliament, Council and Commission. Article 75 confirms the EDPS provides the Secretariat.

## Why the Mechanism Is Contested

The core tension is speed versus quality control. The OSS was designed to give businesses regulatory certainty (deal with one lead authority, get one EU-wide answer) but concentrates enforcement capacity in a handful of authorities, chiefly Ireland and Luxembourg, whose DPAs must handle a disproportionate share of the EU's largest and most complex cross-border cases. NOYB has repeatedly criticised the Irish DPC for slow-walking major complaints (some Meta/WhatsApp complaints took years to reach a draft decision) and has used Article 60 objections and Article 65 referrals strategically to force stronger findings, as in the TikTok children's-data case. Companies, conversely, complain about legal uncertainty while a case moves through LSA drafting, CSA objection and EDPB arbitration, sometimes years after the original complaint.

The GDPR Procedural Regulation, Regulation (EU) 2025/2518, is the legislative response to this friction. It does not touch GDPR's substantive rules; it hard-wires deadlines and procedural rights into the cooperation and consistency mechanism itself; fixed timelines for lead-authority draft decisions, harmonised complaint-admissibility criteria so individuals across the EU face the same bar to lodge a valid complaint, defined rights to be heard and to access the file for parties under investigation, and an early-resolution route for complaints that become moot once the underlying infringement is fixed. It entered into force on 1 January 2026 and applies in full from 2 April 2027, meaning cross-border complaints lodged from that date follow the new procedural rules.
