The European Central Bank treats climate change and nature loss as material to both its price-stability mandate and its banking-supervision role. This guide covers what the ECB has actually committed to doing, distinct from `ecb_monetary_policy` which covers interest rates and standard instruments.

## Legal and Mandate Basis

The ECB's primary objective under Article 127(1) TFEU is price stability. Without prejudice to that objective, the ECB is required to support the general economic policies of the Union with a view to contributing to the objectives set out in Article 3 of the Treaty on European Union, which includes a high level of protection and improvement of the quality of the environment. Article 11 TFEU requires environmental protection requirements to be integrated into the definition and implementation of all Union policies, which the ECB cites as part of its legal basis for climate work within its secondary mandate. The ECB is explicit that climate action is pursued strictly within its mandate, not as an independent objective that could override price stability.

## The 2024-2025 Climate and Nature Plan

Published 30 January 2024, the plan set out three focus areas for the following two years, building on the ECB's first climate agenda from 2021 and its 2022 climate roadmap.

**1. Transition to a green economy.** The ECB deepened its analysis of transition costs, green investment needs, corporate transition plans, and how the transition affects labour markets, productivity and growth. Findings feed into the ECB's macroeconomic modelling framework. The ECB also examined, within its mandate, whether further changes to monetary policy instruments and portfolios were warranted in view of the transition.

**2. Physical impact of climate change.** Work focused on the effect of extreme weather events on inflation and financial stability, integrating physical risk into climate scenarios and macroeconomic projections, and assessing the economic impact of adaptation (or the lack of it), including the insurance protection gap.

**3. Nature loss and degradation.** A newer strand versus the 2021 agenda: the ECB began assessing how biodiversity loss and ecosystem degradation interact with climate risk and could affect price stability and financial stability through economic and financial-system channels.

On 16 January 2026 the ECB confirmed it had delivered on the 2024-2025 plan and set out continued priorities: assessing banks' prudential transition plans and the energy/fiscal costs of transition; strengthening macroeconomic analysis of physical risks; evaluating banks' capability to manage physical risk; and starting work on water-related ecosystem impacts. ## Monetary Policy Implications

**Tilting of corporate bond holdings.** During the reinvestment phase of the Asset Purchase Programme (APP), the Eurosystem tilted its corporate bond purchases and reinvestments towards issuers with better climate performance (lower carbon emissions, more ambitious decarbonisation targets, better climate-related disclosure). APP reinvestments ended in July 2023, so tilting now applies only to the legacy portfolio run-off pattern rather than active net purchases; the ECB nonetheless reports a roughly 39% reduction in the carbon footprint of its corporate bond holdings and own operations since 2019.

**Collateral framework.** The ECB has moved to limit the share of assets issued by entities with a high carbon footprint that counterparties can use as collateral, and requires increasingly climate-related disclosure from issuers of assets used as Eurosystem collateral, aligning the collateral framework with the EU Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive (CSRD) and EU Taxonomy timelines.

**Own disclosures.** The Eurosystem publishes an annual climate-related financial disclosure report covering its non-monetary policy portfolios and (separately) its monetary policy portfolios, following recognised international disclosure standards.

**Stress testing and scenario analysis.** The ECB has run system-wide climate stress tests (2021, 2022 economy-wide) and contributed climate scenarios to the "Fit for 55" macro-financial exercise, feeding results into both monetary policy risk assessment and macroprudential work with the ESRB.

## Banking Supervision (SSM): Climate and Environmental Risk

Climate and environmental (C&E) risk supervision runs through the ECB's Single Supervisory Mechanism (SSM), separate from monetary policy but coordinated through the ECB's climate change centre.

**The 2020 Guide.** In November 2020 the ECB published its final Guide on climate-related and environmental risks, explaining how it expects significant banks under direct SSM supervision to consider C&E risks in their business strategy, governance and risk management, and to enhance their disclosures in line with existing prudential rules. It is expectation-setting guidance, not binding law, but non-compliance triggers supervisory dialogue and can escalate to formal measures.

**2022 self-assessments and staggered deadlines.** In November 2022 the ECB set institution-specific deadlines for full alignment with the 2020 Guide, structured in stages:
- Q1 2023: banks expected to adequately categorise C&E risk and conduct a full assessment of its impact on their activities
- End of 2023: C&E risk fully integrated into governance, strategy and risk management frameworks
- End of 2024: full alignment with all remaining supervisory expectations, including integration into the Internal Capital Adequacy Assessment Process (ICAAP) and stress testing

**Enforcement.** In 2023 the ECB warned that some banks had not met interim deadlines and set individual improvement targets for roughly 20 institutions that missed the Q1 2023 milestone. The ECB has periodic-penalty-payment powers and can escalate through binding supervisory decisions for banks that remain non-compliant after the end-2024 deadline.

**Ongoing thematic work.** The SSM continues to publish compendiums of good practice on climate and nature-related risk management and stress testing, most recently addressing physical risk, prudential transition planning, scenario analysis and nature-related risk, closing gaps identified in earlier thematic reviews (2022 thematic review covered 112 significant institutions).

## Cooperation with the ESRB and EU Sustainable Finance Framework

The ECB and the European Systemic Risk Board (ESRB) jointly assess systemic climate-related financial risk across the euro area banking and insurance sectors, publishing joint reports on climate scenario analysis and system-wide stress testing. This work feeds into the EU's broader sustainable finance architecture: the EU Taxonomy Regulation (classification of environmentally sustainable activities), the Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive (CSRD, disclosure obligations that also shape what data banks can collect from corporate borrowers), and the EU Green Bond Standard. Supervisory expectations under the ECB's 2020 Guide are also aligned with the European Banking Authority's Guidelines on ESG risk management and disclosure, avoiding duplicate or conflicting requirements for cross-border banking groups.
