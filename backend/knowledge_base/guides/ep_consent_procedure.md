# The Consent Procedure in the European Parliament

## QUICK FACTS
- **What it is**: a special legislative or non-legislative procedure under which the European Parliament must approve ("give its consent to") or reject a text **as a whole, in a single vote, with no amendments possible**. If Parliament withholds consent, the act cannot be adopted, full stop.
- **Former name**: the "assent procedure" before the Lisbon Treaty (2009) renamed it "consent". Older documents and academic literature still use "assent".
- **Legal basis**: Article 289(2) TFEU designates it as one of the two special legislative procedures (alongside consultation). The specific consent requirement is set out separately in each Treaty article that uses it (see below), not in a single unified article.
- **OEIL procedure code**: **`(APP)`** for legislative-consent files (e.g. `2021/0106(APP)`); international-agreement consents are usually tagged **`(NLE)`** (non-legislative enactment) or `(INI)`/`(CNS)` variants depending on the exact Treaty base, since the underlying agreement itself is negotiated and concluded by the Council, not "legislated".
- **Where Parliament's Rules of Procedure govern it**: consent votes are handled under the EP's Rules of Procedure chapter on international agreements and special legislative procedures (the responsible committee, currently AFET, INTA, LIBE, BUDG or others depending on subject matter, tables a recommendation to give or withhold consent; Parliament then votes it up or down).
- **Main uses**: international agreements under Article 218(6)(a) TFEU (trade agreements, association agreements, accession-of-the-EU-to-international-conventions); accession of new EU Member States (Article 49 TEU); withdrawal agreements (Article 50 TEU); the Multiannual Financial Framework regulation (Article 312 TFEU); the Article 7 TEU procedure for a "clear risk" or "serious and persistent breach" of EU values by a Member State; certain flexibility and own-resources measures (Article 352 TFEU, Article 311 TFEU); the uniform electoral procedure for European Parliament elections (Article 223(1) TFEU); and a handful of enhanced-cooperation and passerelle-clause decisions.
- **Voting threshold**: normally a **simple majority of votes cast**. Article 7 TEU consent requires the **stricter two-thirds majority of votes cast, representing a majority of Parliament's component Members**. Own-resources decisions (Article 311 TFEU) also require the Council to act by unanimity and, since the Lisbon Treaty, need EP consent by a majority of component Members in some readings.
- **Real leverage demonstrated**: Parliament has used its consent power to kill agreements the Council and Commission had already negotiated and signed. It rejected the EU-US interim SWIFT banking-data agreement on **11 February 2010** (378 votes to 196, 31 abstentions) and rejected the Anti-Counterfeiting Trade Agreement (ACTA) on **4 July 2012** (478 votes to 39, 165 abstentions): the first ever EP rejection of an international trade agreement under its post-Lisbon consent power.
- **Official reference**: EUR-Lex glossary entry, `https://eur-lex.europa.eu/EN/legal-content/glossary/consent-procedure.html`; European Parliament, "The European Parliament's role in concluding international agreements", EPRS briefing, `https://www.europarl.europa.eu/RegData/etudes/BRIE/2024/762341/EPRS_BRI(2024)762341_EN.pdf`.

## How it works, step by step
1. **Negotiation happens elsewhere.** For international agreements, the Council authorises the Commission (or the High Representative for CFSP matters) to negotiate under Article 218 TFEU. For accession or withdrawal, the European Council and the candidate/departing state negotiate the terms. Parliament is **not at the negotiating table**, though it is kept informed and, for trade deals in particular, has built up strong informal monitoring rights (regular briefings from the Commissioner for Trade, access to negotiating mandates).
2. **The text arrives finished.** By the time the file reaches Parliament for consent, the substantive negotiation is over. The Council has typically already signed the agreement (or, for the MFF, reached political agreement) before asking for EP consent to conclude it.
3. **Committee stage.** The lead committee (varies by subject: AFET for association agreements, INTA for trade agreements, LIBE for agreements touching justice and home affairs, BUDG for the MFF) examines the text and drafts a **recommendation** to the plenary to give or withhold consent. Unlike the ordinary legislative procedure, the committee **cannot table amendments to the substance** of the agreement itself, only a recommendation and, often, an accompanying non-binding resolution setting out political conditions or concerns.
4. **Single plenary vote.** Parliament votes once, on the recommendation as a whole: yes (give consent) or no (withhold consent). There is no second reading, no conciliation, no possibility of sending the text back for renegotiation through the procedure itself.
5. **Legal effect.** If Parliament gives consent, the Council (or, for accession, all Member States plus the applicant state) proceeds to adopt/ratify. If Parliament withholds consent, the act is dead in its current form; the only way forward is for the Council and the third country (or Member States) to renegotiate and restart the process.

## Where it applies
- **International agreements (Article 218(6)(a) TFEU)**: this is the broadest and most frequently used category. Consent is required for association agreements, agreements on EU accession to the European Convention on Human Rights, agreements establishing a specific institutional framework by organising cooperation procedures, agreements with important budgetary implications, and agreements covering fields to which the ordinary legislative procedure applies internally (which today covers most trade and investment agreements, since trade policy is now largely EU exclusive competence run through the ordinary legislative procedure at internal level). See `eu_trade_policy` for how this plays out on trade deals specifically.
- **Accession of new Member States (Article 49 TEU)**: before the Council decides unanimously to admit a new Member State, Parliament must consent by a majority of its component Members (not just votes cast). The most recent enlargement round under negotiation involves Ukraine, Moldova and the Western Balkans candidates.
- **Withdrawal agreements (Article 50 TEU)**: the withdrawal agreement negotiated between the EU and a departing Member State (as with the UK's withdrawal agreement, concluded end-2019/early-2020) requires EP consent before the Council can conclude it by qualified majority.
- **The Multiannual Financial Framework regulation (Article 312 TFEU)**: the Council adopts the MFF regulation **unanimously**, but only **after obtaining the consent of the European Parliament**, given by a majority of its component Members. See `mff_2028_2034` for the current 2028-2034 MFF negotiation, where this consent step is the final gate before the seven-year budget ceiling regulation can enter into force.
- **Article 7 TEU sanctions procedure**: Parliament's consent (by the stricter two-thirds/majority-of-Members threshold) is required both for the Council to determine, by four-fifths majority, that there is a "clear risk of a serious breach" of EU values (Article 7(1)) and for the European Council to determine, by unanimity, the existence of a "serious and persistent breach" (Article 7(2)), which can lead to suspension of a Member State's voting rights.
- **Flexibility and own-resources provisions**: Article 352 TFEU (the "flexibility clause", allowing the EU to act where the Treaties have not provided the necessary powers) requires unanimous Council action after obtaining EP consent. Article 311 TFEU (the Own Resources Decision funding the EU budget) similarly needs EP consent before the unanimous Council decision, though ratification by all Member States according to their constitutional requirements is also needed for own resources to take legal effect.
- **The uniform electoral procedure (Article 223(1) TFEU)**: Parliament proposes the provisions needed to elect its own members by direct universal suffrage; the Council, acting unanimously after obtaining Parliament's consent, lays them down, and Member States must then approve them in accordance with their own constitutional requirements. The most recent use was the 2018 amendment of the 1976 Electoral Act (Council Decision (EU, Euratom) 2018/994).
- **Enhanced cooperation**: Article 329(1) TFEU requires EP consent for the Council to authorise enhanced cooperation between a subset of Member States in an area covered by the ordinary legislative procedure.

## How it differs from the ordinary legislative procedure
| Feature | Ordinary legislative procedure (Art 294 TFEU) | Consent procedure |
|---|---|---|
| Who legislates | Parliament and Council as **co-legislators**, equal footing | Council (or the Council plus Member States/third country) decides; Parliament can only approve or block |
| Amendments | Parliament can table and negotiate amendments across up to three readings | **No amendments possible**, ever, at any stage |
| Rounds | One, two, or three readings, plus conciliation if needed | **Single vote**, no readings, no conciliation |
| Parliament's tool | Legislative co-authorship: shaping the text | **Veto power only**: take-it-or-leave-it |
| Typical OEIL code | `(COD)` | `(APP)` for legislative files; `(NLE)` for most international agreements |
| Outcome if EP disagrees | Text goes back for renegotiation between EP and Council (conciliation) | Text is simply rejected; renegotiation happens **outside** the procedure, from scratch |

This makes consent Parliament's bluntest instrument. It cannot fine-tune a trade agreement's tariff schedule or an accession treaty's transition period the way it can rewrite a directive under codecision. Its only lever is an up-or-down vote on the whole package. For the full comparison across all EU legislative and non-legislative routes, see `eu_legislative_procedures_explained`.

## Parliament's real leverage: it has actually said no
The consent procedure looks weak on paper (no amendment power) but has proven to be a genuine veto with political teeth, because the Council and the negotiating partner know rejection is possible and costly to reverse:

- **SWIFT / Terrorist Finance Tracking Programme, 11 February 2010.** Parliament refused consent to the EU-US interim agreement on transferring European banking-transaction data (via the SWIFT network) to US authorities for counter-terrorism purposes, by 378 votes to 196 with 31 abstentions. MEPs objected primarily on data-protection grounds: insufficient limits on the volume of data transferred and weak safeguards against abuse. This was the first major test of Parliament's post-Lisbon Treaty consent powers over justice-and-home-affairs international agreements, and it forced the Commission and Council back to the table; a revised agreement with stronger data-protection guarantees was approved by Parliament a few months later, on 7 July 2010.
- **Anti-Counterfeiting Trade Agreement (ACTA), 4 July 2012.** Parliament rejected ACTA, a plurilateral trade agreement on intellectual-property enforcement that the EU and most Member States had signed in January 2012, by 478 votes to 39 with 165 abstentions. This was the first time Parliament exercised its Lisbon Treaty power to reject an international trade agreement, and it followed unprecedented public mobilisation (street protests across Europe, mass emails to MEPs) over concerns that ACTA's enforcement measures threatened internet freedom and privacy. The rejection killed ACTA definitively for the EU: without EP consent, the Council could not conclude it, and it never entered into force in the Union.

Both episodes are cited in EU institutional literature as proof that consent, despite being an all-or-nothing tool, gives Parliament genuine agenda-shaping power: negotiators now routinely consult Parliament informally throughout international-agreement negotiations precisely to avoid a consent-stage rejection late in the process, since a "no" cannot be patched with a quick amendment the way a codecision disagreement can.

## Why it matters to a policy professional
- **Timing risk**: because there is no amendment stage, any lobbying or advocacy on a consent file must happen **before** the Council and Commission finalise the text, typically during the negotiating mandate phase or committee scrutiny, not once the plenary vote is scheduled. By the time a recommendation reaches plenary, the only live question is yes/no.
- **Committee recommendations and accompanying resolutions matter**: even though Parliament cannot amend the underlying agreement, the lead committee's recommendation report and any accompanying own-initiative resolution can carry political conditions, monitoring demands, or human-rights benchmarks that shape how the Commission implements the agreement afterwards.
- **Predictive signal**: a committee recommendation to withhold consent, or a narrow expected majority, is a strong early warning that a deal is at risk, useful for Brubru's Predictions feature when tracking `(APP)`/`(NLE)` files.
- **MFF and enlargement cycles**: because both the EU budget's legal ceiling (Article 312) and new-country accession (Article 49) run through consent, these are recurring high-stakes moments where Parliament's single vote is the last formal EU-level check before national ratification processes take over.
