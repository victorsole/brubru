# Asset Recovery and Confiscation Directive (Directive (EU) 2024/1260)

## QUICK FACTS
- Topic: EU-wide minimum rules on tracing, freezing, confiscating and managing criminal assets
- Legal act: Directive (EU) 2024/1260 of the European Parliament and of the Council of 24 April 2024 on asset recovery and confiscation
- CELEX: 32024L1260
- Adopted: 24 April 2024. Published in the Official Journal: 2 May 2024 (OJ L, 2024/1260). Entered into force: 22 May 2024
- Transposition deadline for Member States: 23 November 2026 (verify against national implementing measures nearer the date)
- National asset recovery strategy deadline: 24 May 2027, then updated at intervals no longer than five years
- Legal basis / procedure: ordinary legislative procedure (COD), Commission proposal COM(2022) 245, tabled 25 May 2022, procedure 2022/0167(COD)
- Repeals and replaces: Directive 2014/42/EU on freezing and confiscation of instrumentalities and proceeds of crime, and Council Decision 2007/845/JHA on Asset Recovery Offices (AROs); largely supersedes Council Framework Decision 2005/212/JHA on confiscation of crime-related proceeds, instrumentalities and property (some provisions of the 2005 Framework Decision remain applicable where not covered by the new directive: verify the precise saving clauses in Article 32 of the directive against the consolidated text)
- Scope: the full asset recovery chain: tracing and identification, freezing, confiscation, management and disposal (including social reuse) of criminal assets
- New/strengthened confiscation types: conviction-based confiscation, extended confiscation, third-party confiscation, value-based confiscation, non-conviction-based confiscation (where criminal proceedings were initiated but cannot be pursued due to illness, absconding or death of the suspect/accused, or because the limitation period: under 15 years in national law: expired after proceedings began), and confiscation of unexplained wealth linked to criminal-organisation activity generating significant economic gain
- Institutional strengthening: reinforces Asset Recovery Offices (AROs) with urgent freezing powers and wider database access; requires every Member State to set up or designate Asset Management Offices (AMOs) to manage frozen and confiscated property and prevent value loss before final disposal
- Cooperation mandate: explicit cooperation duties with the European Public Prosecutor's Office (EPPO), Europol, Eurojust and third countries/partners; connects to the CARIN network (Camden Asset Recovery Inter-Agency Network) informal cooperation practice
- Victims: claims by victims of the underlying offence must be taken into account in tracing, freezing and confiscation proceedings, with restitution where entitled
- Statistics: Member States must collect and report data on asset recovery outcomes to measure progress against organised and financial crime
- Companion instrument: Directive (EU) 2024/1226 on the definition of criminal offences and penalties for the violation of Union restrictive measures (sanctions-circumvention criminalisation), which entered into force 19 May 2024: the two files were negotiated in parallel as part of the same anti-organised-crime and anti-sanctions-evasion push
- Policy framing: confiscation is flagged a strategic priority in the Commission's ProtectEU internal security strategy; the persistently low EU asset-recovery rate (Europol estimates around 2% of criminal proceeds frozen, roughly 1% confiscated) is the stated rationale
- Lead DG: DG HOME (Migration and Home Affairs), working with DG JUST

Directive (EU) 2024/1260 is the EU's overhaul of the rules for going after criminal money. It replaces a patchwork of 2005, 2007 and 2014 instruments with a single directive covering the entire asset recovery lifecycle, and it is the legal basis for two of the more consequential recent additions to EU criminal-asset practice: routine non-conviction-based confiscation in defined circumstances, and a mandatory Asset Management Office in every Member State.

## Why It Matters

Confiscation has historically recovered only a small fraction of criminal proceeds in the EU: Europol and Commission impact-assessment figures repeatedly cite roughly 2% of criminal proceeds frozen and about 1% actually confiscated. The Commission's own evaluation of the 2014 Directive and the 2007 ARO Council Decision found fragmented national practice, weak information exchange between Asset Recovery Offices, and no EU-wide obligation to manage seized property professionally (so cars, businesses and real estate lost value while court proceedings dragged on). Directive (EU) 2024/1260 responds to all three gaps in one instrument, and does so against the backdrop of the EU's post-2022 push to freeze Russian-linked assets and tackle sanctions evasion (its companion, Directive (EU) 2024/1226, criminalises sanctions-violation across the Union).

For compliance and legal teams, in-house counsel, and organisations exposed to criminal-asset freezing or third-party confiscation risk (including bona fide third parties who acquired property later found to be criminal proceeds), the directive matters because it widens the circumstances in which assets can be frozen and confiscated without a final criminal conviction, and it puts a statutory floor under how national Asset Management Offices must handle seized property pending a court decision.

## What the Directive Covers

### 1. Tracing and identification
Asset Recovery Offices (AROs) gain reinforced powers and access to a wider set of national registers (property, population/citizenship, vehicle, aircraft and vessel registers, and others specified in the directive) so that tracing and identifying criminal assets becomes a routine, near-automatic step in organised-crime investigations, not a discretionary add-on. AROs must also be able to trace and identify assets on request from another Member State's freezing or confiscation order, and from the EPPO.

### 2. Freezing
The directive introduces urgent freezing powers for AROs so that assets cannot be moved or dissipated in the window before a formal freezing order is issued by the competent judicial authority.

### 3. Confiscation
The directive sets minimum rules across the full spectrum:
- **Conviction-based confiscation** of instrumentalities and proceeds following a final criminal conviction.
- **Extended confiscation** of property belonging to a person convicted of an offence capable of generating economic benefit, where a court is satisfied the property derives from criminal conduct.
- **Third-party confiscation**, where proceeds or instrumentalities have been transferred to a third party who knew or ought to have known of their criminal origin.
- **Value-based confiscation**, where the original asset cannot be confiscated (e.g. it has been consumed, concealed, or its value has diminished).
- **Non-conviction-based confiscation**, newly generalised across the Union for cases where criminal proceedings have been initiated but cannot be pursued because of the suspect's or accused person's illness, absconding, or death, or because the applicable limitation period (below 15 years under national law) has expired after proceedings started.
- **Confiscation of unexplained wealth** linked to activity carried out as part of a criminal organisation and generating significant economic gain, even absent a conviction for that specific activity.

### 4. Management and disposal
Every Member State must set up or designate an Asset Management Office (AMO) responsible for managing frozen and confiscated property, either directly or by supporting other competent bodies. AMOs must be able to sell certain frozen assets before a final confiscation decision (subject to safeguards) to prevent value loss, and Member States retain discretion over further use of confiscated assets, including social reuse (e.g. reallocation to community or victim-support purposes) and, where applicable, restitution to victims.

### 5. Governance
Each Member State must adopt a national strategy on asset recovery by 24 May 2027 at the latest, covering resources, training, and the roles and responsibilities of competent authorities, and update it at intervals of no more than five years. Member States must also collect and report statistics on freezing, management and confiscation outcomes.

## Institutional and Cooperation Architecture

The directive formalises cooperation duties between national AROs, national AMOs, and the EU-level actors most active in cross-border financial crime: the European Public Prosecutor's Office (EPPO) for participating Member States, Europol (including its financial intelligence and asset-tracing support), and Eurojust (judicial cooperation and coordination). It also references cooperation with third countries and partners, connecting to the informal Camden Asset Recovery Inter-Agency Network (CARIN) practice that predates the directive. This sits alongside the EU's broader anti-money-laundering architecture (the new AMLA authority and the AML package) and the sanctions-enforcement track opened by Directive (EU) 2024/1226 on criminalising restrictive-measures violations: both of which generate leads that AROs and AMOs may act on.

## What It Does Not Do

The directive does not create a single EU-level confiscation court or a centralised EU asset-management body; management and disposal remain national competences carried out to a common minimum standard. It does not itself criminalise sanctions violations (that is Directive (EU) 2024/1226, adopted as a companion instrument). It is a minimum-harmonisation directive: Member States may go further (for example, broader unexplained-wealth confiscation or more assertive victim restitution rules) but the directive sets the floor, not the ceiling.

## Practical Implications

- Businesses, individuals and financial institutions with EU-linked assets and any exposure to organised-crime, corruption or sanctions-evasion investigations should note the widened non-conviction-based confiscation route, since it removes the previous default expectation of a final conviction before assets can be permanently confiscated.
- Bona fide third-party holders of property later linked to criminal proceeds should understand the third-party confiscation standard (knew or ought to have known) and the safeguards the directive requires national law to preserve.
- Legal and compliance teams tracking transposition should watch national implementing legislation land through 23 November 2026, since minimum-harmonisation directives typically produce material variation in how far each Member State goes beyond the floor.
- Anyone monitoring the broader anti-financial-crime and anti-sanctions-evasion agenda should read this directive alongside Directive (EU) 2024/1226 (restrictive-measures violations) and the EU's AML package, as the three instruments were developed and are being implemented as a connected policy track.

## Key Terms

- **ARO (Asset Recovery Office):** national body tasked with tracing and identifying criminal assets and facilitating cross-border cooperation, reinforced under this directive with urgent freezing powers and wider register access.
- **AMO (Asset Management Office):** national body every Member State must set up or designate to manage frozen and confiscated property, including value-preserving pre-confiscation sale where appropriate.
- **Non-conviction-based confiscation:** confiscation without a final criminal conviction, permitted under this directive only in the defined circumstances of illness, absconding, death, or expiry of a sub-15-year limitation period after proceedings began.
- **Unexplained wealth confiscation:** confiscation of assets linked to criminal-organisation activity generating significant economic gain, without requiring a conviction tied to that specific gain.
- **CARIN:** the informal Camden Asset Recovery Inter-Agency Network connecting national asset-recovery practitioners, which the directive's cooperation provisions build on.
