## EU Bioeconomy Strategy

### Updated Strategy (COM(2018) 673, updated November 2025)

The Bioeconomy Strategy covers all economic sectors and systems that rely on biological resources: agriculture, forestry, fisheries, food, bio-based industries, and associated services.

### Five Objectives

| Objective | Description |
|-----------|-------------|
| **1. Ensure food and nutrition security** | Sustainable intensification, reducing food waste, diversifying protein sources |
| **2. Manage natural resources sustainably** | Circular bioeconomy, soil health, biodiversity preservation |
| **3. Reduce dependence on non-renewable resources** | Bio-based materials, sustainable chemicals, bioenergy |
| **4. Mitigate and adapt to climate change** | Carbon sequestration, climate-resilient agriculture, forest management |
| **5. Strengthen European competitiveness and create jobs** | Bio-based industries, rural development, bioeconomy hubs |

### 14 Action Areas

Key actions relevant to R&I policy:
- Strengthen bioeconomy research and innovation (Horizon Europe Cluster 6)
- Deploy bioeconomy in rural, coastal, and urban areas
- Develop a sustainable circular bioeconomy
- Establish a monitoring system for the bioeconomy
- Enhance policy coherence across sectors (agriculture, industry, energy, trade)
- Mobilise public and private investment (InvestEU, national bioeconomy strategies)
- Promote international cooperation (G7, G20, FAO, AU)

### 2025 Update Key Changes

- Stronger integration with European Green Deal objectives
- New targets for bio-based product market share
- Enhanced biodiversity considerations
- Digital tools for bioeconomy monitoring (satellite data, AI-based crop analysis)
- Strategic autonomy dimension (reducing dependence on imported feedstocks)

## Alternative Proteins

### Landscape

| Technology | TRL | Scale | Key Challenges |
|------------|-----|-------|----------------|
| **Plant-based proteins** | 8-9 | Commercial | Taste, texture, consumer acceptance, crop diversity |
| **Precision fermentation** | 6-8 | Pilot-commercial | Scale-up costs, regulatory approval, consumer labelling |
| **Cellular agriculture** | 3-6 | Lab-pilot | Cost reduction (culture media), regulatory pathway, public acceptance |
| **Insect farming** | 7-8 | Commercial (feed); Pilot (food) | Consumer acceptance (EU), Novel Food Regulation approval, scaling |
| **Algae/microalgae** | 5-7 | Pilot | Harvesting costs, taste, processing, regulatory status |
| **Mycoprotein** | 8-9 | Commercial | Limited product range, allergenicity monitoring |

### EU Regulatory Framework

| Regulation | Relevance |
|-----------|-----------|
| **Novel Food Regulation (EU) 2015/2283** | Authorisation required for new food sources (insects, cultured meat, novel proteins) |
| **EFSA risk assessment** | Scientific opinion on safety before authorisation |
| **Food Information Regulation (EU) 1169/2011** | Labelling requirements (allergens, nutritional information) |
| **Commission Farm to Fork Strategy** | 2030 targets for sustainable food systems |
| **EU Protein Strategy** | Expected Commission initiative on protein supply diversification |

### Consumer Acceptance

Eurobarometer and academic survey data:
- Plant-based alternatives: ~30-40% of EU consumers regularly buy
- Insects as food: ~10-15% willing to try; strong variation by country (higher in NL, DK, BE; lower in IT, ES, EL)
- Cultured meat: ~20-25% interested; significant knowledge gap
- Key acceptance drivers: taste, price, environmental motivation, health perception
- Key barriers: naturalness concerns, distrust of "ultra-processed", unfamiliarity

## Living Labs Methodology for Food Systems

### Definition

Living labs are user-centred, open innovation ecosystems that integrate co-creation, exploration, experimentation, and evaluation in real-life settings.

### ENoLL (European Network of Living Labs)

- 400+ living labs across Europe
- Accreditation process with quality criteria
- Food-specific living labs: food innovation, sustainable agriculture, circular bioeconomy

### Living Lab Phases

| Phase | Activities | Stakeholders |
|-------|-----------|-------------|
| **Co-creation** | Identify challenges, co-design solutions with users | Consumers, farmers, SMEs, researchers |
| **Exploration** | Discover needs, behaviours, trends through ethnographic methods | End-users, behavioural scientists |
| **Experimentation** | Test prototypes and solutions in real-world conditions | Innovators, users, public authorities |
| **Evaluation** | Assess impact, scalability, user satisfaction, economic viability | All stakeholders, evaluators |

## Farmers' Position in the Food Supply Chain (March 2026)

On 5 March 2026, the European Parliament and Council reached a **provisional trilogue agreement** on amendments to the Common Market Organisation (CMO) Regulation to reinforce farmers' position in the food supply chain.

- **Procedure:** 2024/0319(COD) -- Ordinary legislative procedure
- **Rapporteur:** Celine Imart (EPP, France)
- **Committee:** AGRI (Agriculture and Rural Development)

### Key Measures Agreed
- Member States must establish and publish online **price indicators** for use in farm contracts
- **Producer organisations (POs)** strengthened with direct buyer engagement rights
- Rules preventing direct individual producer contact (bypassing POs)
- "**Fair/equitable**" labelling criteria to include rural development and farmer organisation promotion
- "**Meat**" designation reserved for animal products, excluding lab-grown alternatives; terms like "steak" and "liver" reserved for traditional livestock products
- "**Short supply chain**" labelling restricted to EU-produced goods with limited intermediaries
- Mandatory **written dairy contracts** with opt-out provisions and revision clauses

### Next Steps
The provisional agreement requires formal approval by both Parliament and Council before entry into force. Plenary vote expected Q1-Q2 2026.
