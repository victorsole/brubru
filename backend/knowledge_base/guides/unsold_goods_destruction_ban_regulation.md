# Unsold Goods Destruction Ban Derogations - Commission Delegated Regulation (EU) 2026/296

## Overview

From 19 July 2026, a fashion retailer in the EU can no longer simply shred its unsold clothing. Article 25(1) of the ESPR (Regulation (EU) 2024/1781) prohibits economic operators from destroying certain unsold consumer products, and the first products in scope, listed in ESPR Annex VII, are apparel and clothing accessories and footwear. This Delegated Regulation does not create that ban. It draws the line around it: it sets out the exhaustive list of circumstances in which destroying an in-scope unsold product is still permitted, and the documentation an operator must keep to prove that a derogation applies.

Destruction is a measure of last resort. Even where the ban does not apply, operators may still remanufacture, refurbish, donate or prepare products for reuse. Where a derogation does apply and the product is destroyed, the destruction must follow the waste hierarchy in Article 4 of the Waste Framework Directive 2008/98/EC, prioritising recycling over other recovery, including energy recovery, and over disposal.

## The ten derogations (Article 2, points (a) to (j))

An in-scope unsold consumer product may be destroyed only where one of these applies and the Article 3 documentation can be produced:

- **(a) Dangerous product:** the product is a dangerous product within the meaning of the General Product Safety Regulation (EU) 2023/988.
- **(b) Legal non-compliance:** the product is unfit for purpose because it is non-compliant with Union or national law for reasons other than (a), and destruction is required by law or is the appropriate and proportionate corrective action. This covers ethical non-compliance such as forced labour.
- **(c) IP infringement (established):** the product is found to infringe intellectual property rights by a final judicial decision, an alternative dispute resolution (ADR) decision, a notification by a right holder or competent authority, or a substantiated internal investigation.
- **(d) IP licence expiry:** the product is subject to a valid, enforceable licence or contractual IP restriction on sale or distribution after a specified date, that date has passed, and destruction is the appropriate and proportionate corrective action.
- **(e) Cannot de-brand:** the product is unsuitable for reuse or remanufacturing because it is technically unfeasible to remove or permanently render inaccessible labels, logos or recognisable design characteristics that are IP-protected or considered inappropriate.
- **(f) Damaged or contaminated:** the product is unacceptable for consumer use due to damage, deterioration or contamination, including hygiene issues, and repair or refurbishment is not technically feasible or cost-effective.
- **(g) Defective and unrepairable:** the product is unfit for its intended purpose due to design or manufacturing defects for which repair is not technically feasible.
- **(h) Donation refused:** only where none of (a) to (g) apply, the product was offered for donation, either directly to at least three suitable social economy entities in the Union or on an easily accessible website page for at least eight weeks, and was not accepted.
- **(i) Undonatable donation:** the product was received by a social economy entity in the Union as a donation but no recipient could be found.
- **(j) Unsold after reuse prep:** the product was made available on the market after being prepared for reuse by a waste treatment operator but no recipient could be found.

Note the ordering: the donation route (h) is only available once the safety, legal, IP, damage and defect grounds have all been ruled out. Donation is the preferred outcome, destruction the fallback.

## Proof you must keep (Articles 3 and 4)

- **Five-year retention (Article 3):** for five years after an unsold product is destroyed under a derogation, the operator keeps the relevant documentation and, on request, provides it to competent authorities in electronic form within 30 days (unless the authority already has it under another legal act).
- **Per-ground evidence:** each derogation has its own documentary proof, for example a safety assessment or test report for (a), a self-assessment of the non-compliance for (b), the judicial or ADR decision, notification or internal-investigation record for (c), the licence or contract for (d), inspection reports on technical unfeasibility for (e), quality-assessment or inspection records for damage or defects under (f) and (g), evidence of the donation offer for (h), a declaration of the unfound recipient for (i), and documentation from the waste treatment operator for (j). Documentation may be prepared collectively where multiple products share the same circumstances.
- **Statement to waste operators (Article 4):** the operator must give a statement on the applicable derogation to the waste treatment operator it delivers to, to support sorting, reuse and recycling.
- **Definitions (Article 1):** "cost-effective" means the cost of repair or refurbishment does not outweigh the total cost of destroying and replacing the product; "social economy entity" is defined by reference to Article 3(4i) of Directive 2008/98/EC.

## Where it sits

- **The framework and the ban:** the ESPR (Regulation (EU) 2024/1781), Article 25(1), creates the prohibition and ESPR Annex VII sets the product scope.
- **This act:** the exhaustive derogations and the documentation regime.
- **The sibling act:** the Disclosure Regulation (EU) 2026/2, which makes any remaining destruction public through annual reporting.
- **The wider architecture:** the digital product passport regime and the sectoral product laws that share the ESPR foundation.
