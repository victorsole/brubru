# Mediterranean Fisheries Under Pressure: EU Bottom Trawling Regulation Reform

**Author:** Victor Solé Ferioli

**Date:** December 2024

## Executive Summary

The Mediterranean Sea faces a critical juncture in fisheries management as the EU's Western Mediterranean Multi-Annual Plan (MAP) entered its permanent phase on 1 January 2025. Despite achieving a historic 40% reduction in trawling fishing effort during 2020-2024, overfishing rates remain at 52%—more than double sustainable levels. The December 2024 Council agreement mandated fishing effort reductions of 66% in Spanish/French waters and 38% in Italian/French waters, though an expanded compensation mechanism allows fleets implementing conservation measures to maintain near-2024 fishing levels. European hake populations remain critically depleted, with biomass below 10% of target levels in some areas. Commissioner Costas Kadis now leads EU fisheries policy with mandates to complete the Common Fisheries Policy evaluation by end of 2025 and deliver a Vision 2040 for the sector. This report analyses the regulatory framework, scientific evidence, socio-economic impacts, and policy options for Mediterranean fisheries governance.

## 1. Introduction

The Mediterranean Sea supports one of Europe's oldest and most culturally significant fishing traditions, yet its fish stocks face unprecedented pressure from decades of overexploitation. As the world's most overfished sea, the Mediterranean has become a focal point for EU fisheries policy reform, testing the balance between ecological sustainability and socio-economic viability.

The adoption of the Western Mediterranean Multi-Annual Plan (Regulation EU 2019/1022) in June 2019 marked a turning point in Mediterranean fisheries governance. This plan—the first and only EU management plan in the Mediterranean covering shared stocks—introduced binding fishing effort reductions for demersal species across Spanish, French, and Italian waters. The plan concluded its five-year transitional period on 31 December 2024, entering a permanent phase where maximum sustainable yield (MSY) ranges now apply.

This report examines the current state of Mediterranean fisheries, analysing the regulatory framework, scientific evidence on stock recovery, and the socio-economic implications of conservation measures. It provides a pan-Mediterranean perspective, encompassing all EU Member States with Mediterranean coastlines: Spain, France, Italy, Greece, Croatia, Slovenia, Malta, and Cyprus.

### 1.1 Methodology

This analysis draws upon official EU institutional sources (European Commission DG MARE, EUR-Lex, Eurostat), scientific assessments from the Scientific, Technical and Economic Committee for Fisheries (STECF), data from the General Fisheries Commission for the Mediterranean (GFCM) and the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO), and relevant academic literature. All data has been verified against primary sources where available.

## 2. State of Mediterranean fish stocks

### 2.1 Overview of stock status

The Mediterranean Sea has shown measurable improvement in fisheries sustainability, though significant challenges persist. According to the GFCM's State of Mediterranean and Black Sea Fisheries 2025 (SoMFi 2025), overfishing has fallen from 88% of assessed stocks in 2012 to 52% in 2023—a historic low representing a 31% reduction in fishing pressure over the decade.

However, this progress must be contextualised: fishing mortality in Mediterranean and Black Sea waters remains approximately 2.1 times above sustainable levels. The European Environment Agency reports that only 8.6% of Mediterranean stocks meet both Good Environmental Status criteria for fishing mortality and reproductive capacity, compared to 41% in the North-East Atlantic.

### 2.3 European Hake: a critical case study

European hake (*Merluccius merluccius*) represents the most commercially valuable demersal species in the Mediterranean yet faces the most severe depletion. STECF assessments indicate that hake populations in Western Mediterranean GSAs 1-5-6-7 sit below Blim—the limit reference point indicating reduced reproductive capacity—with biomass below 10% of target levels in certain areas.

The fishing mortality to MSY ratio (F/Fmsy) ranges from 1.7 to an alarming 8.5 in some zones, meaning fishing mortality exceeds sustainable levels by nearly eightfold. Scientific projections suggest that hake biomass will remain below precautionary levels in the medium term even with zero fishing, highlighting the severity of historical overexploitation.

### 2.4 Regional stock trends

The latest STECF assessments (STECF-24-10, September 2024) evaluated 20 demersal fish stocks in the Western Mediterranean:

- **4 stocks (20%)** are fished at or close to Fmsy
- **8 stocks (40%)** show rapid recovery trajectories
- **2 stocks** remain severely overfished (hake and Norway lobster in Spanish waters)

Red mullet demonstrates the most positive regional trend, with sustainable exploitation (F/Fmsy <1) now achieved in multiple areas including the Ligurian/Tyrrhenian Seas (GSAs 9-10), Strait of Sicily (GSA 16), Aegean Sea (GSA 22), and North Levant (GSA 24). Red mullet in the Gulf of Lion has fully recovered under MAP management.

## 3. EU Common Fisheries Policy Framework

### 3.1 Western Mediterranean Multi-Annual Plan

The Western Mediterranean MAP (Regulation EU 2019/1022) established the first EU-level fishing effort regime for demersal stocks in the Mediterranean, covering European hake, red mullet, deep-water shrimps, and Norway lobster across Spanish, French, and Italian waters (GSAs 1-11).

**Key provisions include:**

- Progressive reduction of trawling fishing effort by 40% over five years (2020-2024)
- Annual fishing days limits determined by Council based on scientific advice
- Restrictions on trawling in waters shallower than 100 metres within 6 nautical miles from coast (May-July)
- Establishment of closed areas to protect nursery and spawning grounds
- Regional cooperation requirements among France, Italy, and Spain

### 3.2 December 2024 Council Agreement

The December 2024 Agriculture and Fisheries Council negotiations proved particularly contentious. The Commission's original proposal—based on STECF scientific advice—would have reduced fishing days from approximately 130 to just 27 days, representing a 79% reduction that Mediterranean Member States deemed economically devastating.

Spain, France, and Italy signed a joint declaration on 18 November 2024 calling for a moratorium, arguing such measures would make Mediterranean fishing "economically unviable." The final compromise introduced an expanded compensation mechanism with 12 sustainability measures allowing vessels to recover fishing days by implementing conservation practices:

- Removal of twin-trawl gears (+24% days)
- Selective fishing nets with larger mesh sizes
- Seasonal closures in sensitive areas
- Innovative fishing gear adoption
- Participation in scientific data collection programmes

### 3.3 GFCM Framework and EU Coordination

The General Fisheries Commission for the Mediterranean operates as the primary regional fisheries management organisation, coordinating management across 23 contracting parties including all Mediterranean coastal states. The GFCM achieved historic productivity during its 46th Annual Session (Split, November 2023), adopting 34 decisions—the highest ever at a single session.

EU-GFCM coordination operates through Regulation (EU) 2023/2124, which transposes GFCM binding recommendations into EU law. The EU serves as the main financial donor (€8 million annually) supporting GFCM 2030 Strategy implementation.

**Key GFCM management measures include:**

- 10 active multiannual management plans involving nearly 7,000 vessels
- 11 Fisheries Restricted Areas covering over 1.75 million km²
- Complete ban on bottom trawling beyond 1,000 metres depth
- Minimum conservation reference sizes for priority species
- International joint inspection schemes

## 4. Bottom trawling restrictions and scientific evidence

### 4.1 Current regulatory framework

Bottom trawling represents the most contentious issue in Mediterranean fisheries policy, generating intense debate between conservation advocates and fishing industry representatives.

**Existing restrictions:**

- **Depth limits:** GFCM ban beyond 1,000 metres (first such ban globally); EU prohibition beyond 800 metres
- **VME protection:** 87 Vulnerable Marine Ecosystem protection polygons closed to bottom fishing between 400-800 metres (November 2022)
- **Seasonal closures:** Trawling prohibited within 6 nautical miles from coast in waters shallower than 100 metres (May-July)
- **Marine Protected Areas:** Over 1,000 MPAs in the Mediterranean covering approximately 9.12% of regional waters

Despite nominally high MPA coverage, only 76 Mediterranean MPAs are fully protected, covering a mere 0.04% of the sea. The EU Biodiversity Strategy 2030 targets legal protection of 30% of EU seas by 2030, with the Marine Action Plan (February 2023) calling for phasing out bottom trawling in MPAs by 2030.

### 4.3 Scientific evidence on environmental impacts

**Environmental impacts of bottom trawling:**

- Mortality rates of 20-50% for invertebrates in trawl paths
- 92% of all EU fish discards attributed to bottom trawling
- Potential release of up to 370 million metric tonnes of CO₂ annually through sediment disturbance
- Destruction of benthic habitats including cold-water corals and sponge fields

**Counterarguments from fishing industry:**

- 83 bottom-trawl fisheries worldwide hold MSC certification
- Well-managed trawl fisheries retain 90% of pre-trawled benthic ecosystem state
- Bottom trawling provides 25% of total EU landings (over 1 million tonnes annually)
- No economically viable alternative exists for certain demersal species

### 4.4 Greece's leadership on MPA protection

Greece emerged as a surprising conservation leader when Prime Minister Mitsotakis announced at the Our Ocean Conference (April 2024) a historic ban on bottom trawling in all MPAs by 2030—making Greece the first EU country to commit to a complete MPA trawling ban. The ban takes effect in three national marine parks by 2026, with €780 million committed for enforcement.

### 4.5 Compliance challenges

A January 2025 Science Advances study documented implementation challenges:

- **81% reduction** in bottom-contact fishing within the 87 VME closures post-November 2022
- Approximately **3,500 hours** of fishing persisted in closures
- **19,200 hours** of trawling recorded below 800 metres over two years
- Portuguese vessels identified as primary violators of depth restrictions

### 5.2 Economic significance

Mediterranean and Black Sea fisheries produce nearly 2 million tonnes of seafood annually, generating revenues exceeding USD 20 billion and supporting 700,000 jobs along the value chain. The region accounts for approximately 9% of total EU catches.

**Landing values by Mediterranean Member State (2024):**

- Spain: €1.65 billion (30% of EU total)
- Italy: €0.93 billion (17% of EU total)
- France: €0.88 billion (16% of EU total)
- Greece: €0.44 billion (8% of EU total)

### 5.3 Impact of fishing days reductions

The December 2024 Council negotiations highlighted severe socio-economic concerns. Spain's Minister Planas warned that original Commission proposals would affect 17,000 jobs (3,000 direct on-board positions plus 14,000 indirect). Barcelona fishermen staged a two-day strike in December 2024, with the President of Barcelona Fishermen's Guild asking: "How do they expect us to earn a living working less than a month a year?"

### 5.4 EMFAF support measures

The European Maritime, Fisheries and Aquaculture Fund (EMFAF) 2021-2027 provides €6.108 billion total, with key Mediterranean allocations including:

- Greece: €519.6 million (EU contribution €363.7 million)
- Italy: €537.4 million
- Spain: €1.12 billion (including Atlantic fleet)

- Temporary cessation (up to 12 months per vessel)
- Permanent cessation/decommissioning schemes
- Selective gear investment subsidies
- Decarbonisation measures
- Diversification support (tourism, aquaculture)

Maximum co-financing rates reach 70% standard and 100% for outermost regions.

## 6. Policy options and future outlook

### 6.1 Commissioner Kadis and Vision 2040

Costas Kadis, Cypriot biologist and former Minister of Agriculture, Rural Development and Environment (2018-2023), took office as EU Commissioner for Fisheries and Oceans on 1 December 2024. His mission letter from President von der Leyen mandates:

- Completing the **CFP evaluation by end of 2025**
- Preparing a **Vision 2040 for fisheries and aquaculture** (presentation in 2026)
- Presenting the **European Ocean Pact**

The public consultation for CFP evaluation opened 27 January 2025, closing 21 April 2025, evaluating ten years of CFP implementation (2014-2024).

On bottom trawling, Kadis advocates a science-based, case-by-case approach rather than blanket restrictions: "When there is evidence of an impact, bottom trawling should not be allowed. However, when an impact assessment shows that there is no significant harm—for instance, in Natura 2000 areas that permit compatible activities—then bottom trawling should be allowed to continue."

### 6.2 European Ocean Pact

The European Ocean Pact, adopted 5 June 2025 with €1 billion investment, addresses six priority areas:

1. Protecting ocean health
2. Boosting sustainable blue economy
3. Supporting coastal communities
4. Advancing ocean research
5. Enhancing maritime security
6. Strengthening EU ocean diplomacy

**Key deliverables:**

- Vision 2040 for fisheries (2026)
- **Ocean Act** legislative framework (2027)
- Full operation of the European Digital Twin of the Ocean by 2030
- Complementary **Pact for the Mediterranean** (October 2025)

### 6.3 Policy scenarios

**Scenario 1: Status quo with enhanced compensation**

Continue current MAP implementation with expanded compensation mechanisms. Relies on voluntary adoption of selective gear and conservation measures. Risk: Insufficient stock recovery if compensation uptake is universal without genuine conservation benefit.

**Scenario 2: Accelerated conservation**

Implement full STECF-recommended reductions (79%) with enhanced socio-economic support packages. Would likely accelerate stock recovery but faces strong political resistance. Requires significantly expanded EMFAF funding.

**Scenario 3: Regional differentiation**

Develop area-specific management regimes based on stock status, with stricter measures for critically depleted stocks (hake) and relaxed regimes for recovered stocks (red mullet). Aligns with ecosystem-based approach but increases administrative complexity.

**Scenario 4: Transition support programme**

Comprehensive fleet restructuring programme combining decommissioning schemes, retraining programmes, and alternative livelihood support (aquaculture, maritime tourism). Addresses long-term structural overcapacity but requires significant public investment.

## 7. Conclusions

Mediterranean fisheries policy stands at a pivotal juncture. The transition to the MAP permanent phase in 2025 represents the culmination of five years of effort reduction, yet fundamental challenges persist:

1. **Stock recovery remains incomplete:** European hake populations may not recover to precautionary levels even with zero fishing in the medium term, while roughly half of assessed stocks remain overfished.

2. **Compensation mechanism uncertainty:** The December 2024 compromise reflects political reality—scientific advice calling for near-elimination of fishing effort (79% reduction) proved politically untenable given 17,000+ jobs at stake. Whether compensation mechanisms deliver genuine conservation outcomes or provide loopholes undermining the MAP's objectives remains to be seen.

3. **Member State divergence:** The contrast between Greece's conservation leadership (complete MPA trawling ban by 2030) and the Spain-France-Italy coalition's resistance suggests future negotiations will remain contentious.

4. **Structural overcapacity persists:** Fleet reductions have occurred primarily through economic attrition rather than managed transition, leaving underlying overcapacity issues unaddressed.

5. **Scientific-political disconnect:** The gap between STECF recommendations (27 fishing days) and politically acceptable outcomes (130+ days with compensation) illustrates the challenge of evidence-based policy in economically sensitive sectors.

**Recommendations for stakeholders:**

- **EU institutions:** Strengthen monitoring of compensation mechanism implementation; ensure fishing mortality actually declines
- **Member States:** Develop comprehensive transition support programmes beyond temporary cessation measures
- **Fishing industry:** Accelerate adoption of selective gear technologies; engage constructively with scientific advisory processes
- **Research community:** Enhance socio-economic impact assessments alongside biological stock evaluations

The most critical question is whether the 2025 permanent phase succeeds in achieving MSY across Mediterranean stocks while maintaining viable fishing communities. The answer will depend on implementation quality, enforcement effectiveness, and the political will to adjust course if monitoring reveals insufficient progress toward sustainability targets.
