# The European Citizens' Initiative: Direct Democracy in the EU

**A Practical Guide to the EU's Only Instrument of Transnational Participatory Democracy**

**Author:** Victor Sole Ferioli, Founder & Director, Beresol

**Date:** February 2026

**Location:** Brussels, Belgium

---

## Executive Summary

The European Citizens' Initiative (ECI) is the only instrument of transnational participatory democracy in the world, allowing EU citizens to directly request the European Commission to propose legislation. Established by the Treaty of Lisbon (Article 11(4) TEU) and governed by Regulation (EU) 2019/788, the ECI requires at least 1 million signatures from citizens of at least 7 EU Member States to compel the Commission to formally examine and respond to a legislative proposal.

**Key Findings:**

- **Scale:** Since 2012, over 125 initiatives have been registered and approximately 20 million signatures collected across the EU
- **Success rate:** 14 initiatives have successfully reached the 1 million signature threshold and been submitted to the Commission as of January 2026
- **Top performers:** "One of Us" (1,695,328 signatures), "Right2Water" (1,673,181), and "Fur Free Europe" (1,502,319) are the three most-signed ECIs
- **Geographic concentration:** Germany leads with over 4.47 million total signatures, followed by Italy (1.9 million) and France (1.1 million)
- **Latest success:** "Stop Destroying Videogames" became the 14th successful ECI in January 2026 with 1,294,188 validated signatures across 24 Member States
- **Commission responsiveness:** The Commission must respond within 6 months of submission, but is not obliged to propose legislation; responses range from new legislative proposals to non-legislative measures or reasoned refusals

# 1. Legal Framework

## 1.1 Treaty basis

The European Citizens' Initiative finds its legal basis in Article 11(4) of the Treaty on European Union (TEU), introduced by the Treaty of Lisbon in 2009:

> "Not less than one million citizens who are nationals of a significant number of Member States may take the initiative of inviting the European Commission, within the framework of its powers, to submit any appropriate proposal on matters where citizens consider that a legal act of the Union is required for the purpose of implementing the Treaties."

## 1.2 Governing regulation

The current rules are set out in [Regulation (EU) 2019/788](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2019/788/oj), which replaced the original Regulation (EU) No 211/2011 on 1 January 2020. The reform simplified the process significantly:

- **Lowered the minimum age** for signatories to the voting age for European elections (or 16 in some Member States)
- **Centralised online collection** through a Commission-hosted platform (replacing the requirement for organisers to build their own systems)
- **Extended the collection period** and added flexibility for setting the campaign start date
- **Improved the examination stage** with a public hearing at the European Parliament

Implementing rules on online collection are found in [Regulation (EU) 2019/1799](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg_impl/2019/1799/oj).

## 1.3 Scope and limits

An ECI can only request legislation within the Commission's powers under the Treaties. The Commission will refuse registration if:

- The initiative falls manifestly outside the framework of the Commission's powers
- It is manifestly abusive, frivolous, or vexatious
- It is manifestly contrary to the values of the Union (Article 2 TEU)

The Commission has 2 months (extendable to 4) to decide on registration. Partial registration is possible where only part of an initiative falls within Commission competence.

# 2. How the ECI Works: Step by Step

## Step 1: Formation of the Organisers' Group

A group of at least **7 EU citizens residing in at least 7 different Member States** must be formed. The organisers designate a representative and a substitute to liaise with the Commission throughout the lifecycle.

Key consideration: the group must be genuinely transnational, reflecting the ECI's purpose as an instrument of pan-European civic engagement.

## Step 2: Registration

Organisers submit their initiative for registration via the [organiser account portal](https://citizens-initiative.europa.eu/organiser-account_en), providing:

- The title and description in one official EU language
- Details on the group of organisers
- Information on sources of support and funding

The Commission assesses the initiative against the registration criteria and responds within **2 months** (or 4 months in complex cases). If registered, the initiative is published on the official ECI register in all EU languages.

## Step 3: Signature Collection

Once registered, organisers have **6 months** to set a kick-off date (notifying the Commission at least 10 working days in advance), after which they have **12 months** to collect signatures.

Collection methods:
- **Online** via the Commission's central online collection system
- **On paper** using pre-filled statement of support forms

**Who can sign:**
- EU citizens (nationals of any Member State)
- Old enough to vote in European Parliament elections (or aged 16+ in some countries)

The initiative must gather at least **1 million signatures total**, with minimum thresholds reached in at least **7 Member States**.

## Step 4: Verification

After the collection period, organisers have **3 months** to group signatures by nationality and submit them to the competent national authorities for verification. The authorities then have **3 months** to certify the number of valid statements of support.

The Commission provides a secure file exchange service for transferring paper-collected statements.

## Step 5: Submission

With the verification certificates in hand, organisers have **3 months** to submit the initiative to the Commission, together with information on support and funding received.

## Step 6: Examination and Response

The Commission's examination proceeds on a defined timeline:

| Milestone | Deadline | Action |
|-----------|----------|--------|
| Meeting with Commission | Within 1 month | Organisers explain the issues raised in detail |
| European Parliament hearing | Within 3 months | Public hearing; possible plenary debate and resolution |
| Commission response | Within 6 months | Formal communication adopted by the College of Commissioners |

The Commission's response is published in all official EU languages and sets out:
- What action (if any) it intends to take
- The reasons for taking or not taking action

## What Comes Next

If the Commission considers legislation appropriate, it starts the standard legislative process (impact assessment, public consultation, formal proposal to Parliament and Council). However, the Commission is **not obliged to propose legislation**: it may respond with non-legislative measures or a reasoned refusal.

**Observations:**
- Animal welfare and environmental ECIs have the highest success rate
- The "Stop Destroying Videogames" initiative reached its threshold in a remarkably short time, demonstrating the power of digital-native campaigning
- "Ban Conversion Practices" reportedly collected 1 million signatures in just 10 days, setting an all-time record for speed
- Germany, Italy, and France consistently contribute the largest shares of signatures

# 5. Commission Responses and Legislative Outcomes

The Commission's track record on responding to successful ECIs is mixed:

**Positive legislative outcomes:**
- **Right2Water** (2014): led to the revision of the Drinking Water Directive ([Directive 2020/2184](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dir/2020/2184/oj)), which strengthened water quality standards and improved access to water
- **End the Cage Age** (2020): the Commission committed to phasing out caged farming by 2027 and proposed a revision of animal welfare legislation
- **Ban Glyphosate** (2017): while glyphosate was not banned, the initiative contributed to the debate that led to a restricted renewal of the substance's authorisation

**Non-legislative responses:**
- **One of Us** (2014): the Commission declined to propose new legislation, citing existing frameworks
- **Stop Vivisection** (2015): the Commission maintained its position on the existing Directive 2010/63/EU but committed to further measures to reduce animal testing
- **Minority SafePack** (2020): the Commission declined to propose new legislation, leading to criticism from organisers and the European Parliament

**Pending:**
- **Stop Destroying Videogames** (submitted January 2026): Commission response due by 27 July 2026

The pattern shows that the Commission is more likely to act on ECIs that align with existing policy priorities, particularly in environment and consumer protection. ECIs on politically sensitive topics (ethics, fundamental rights) tend to receive less favourable responses.

# 6. Practical Considerations for Organisers

## 6.1 Registration strategy

- Draft the initiative description carefully: overly broad or vague proposals are more likely to be refused
- Consider partial registration: if some elements fall outside Commission competence, a narrower scope may still be accepted
- Choose the primary language strategically: the initial submission language sets the tone for translations

## 6.2 Campaign and collection

- **Build a transnational coalition** before launching: the 7-country threshold requirement means pan-European coordination is essential from day one
- **Use the Commission's online collection system**: it handles data protection compliance, accessibility, and validation automatically
- **Collect more signatures than required**: national verification authorities may invalidate a proportion of submissions; a 20-30% buffer is recommended
- **Set the kick-off date strategically**: align with relevant news cycles, European Parliament plenary sessions, or Commission work programme announcements

## 6.3 Data protection

Organisers must comply with GDPR and the specific data protection provisions in Regulation 2019/788. The Commission's online collection system handles most compliance requirements, but paper-collected signatures require organisers to implement their own safeguards.

## 6.4 Funding and transparency

All sources of support and funding exceeding EUR 500 per sponsor must be declared. This information is published on the ECI register. Transparency on funding sources is essential for the initiative's credibility.

# 7. The ECI in the Broader EU Democratic Landscape

The ECI is one of several participatory democracy instruments available to EU citizens, but the only one with a Treaty-based right to compel a Commission response:

| Instrument | Treaty Basis | Scope | Binding? |
|-----------|-------------|-------|----------|
| **European Citizens' Initiative** | Art. 11(4) TEU | Request legislation | Commission must respond |
| **Petition to EP** | Art. 227 TFEU | Any EU matter | Non-binding |
| **European Ombudsman** | Art. 228 TFEU | Maladministration | Recommendations |
| **Have Your Say** (EC consultations) | Art. 11(3) TEU | Policy proposals | Consultative |
| **Conference on the Future of Europe** | Ad hoc | Treaty reform | Political commitment |

The ECI has been criticised for low success rates (14 out of 125+ registered initiatives), the Commission's discretion to refuse legislation even after a successful initiative, and the logistical difficulty of collecting 1 million signatures across 7 countries within 12 months. However, even unsuccessful ECIs generate public debate and may influence policy indirectly.

The European Economic and Social Committee (EESC) has been a consistent advocate for strengthening the ECI, calling at ECI Day 2025 for the instrument to "reach its full potential" through better institutional support and more meaningful Commission follow-up.

# 8. Relevance for EU Policy Professionals

For advocacy professionals, the ECI represents both a strategic tool and an intelligence source:

**As a strategic tool:**
- An ECI can be used to raise the profile of an issue on the EU agenda, even if the initiative does not reach 1 million signatures
- Successful ECIs create political pressure that the Commission and Parliament must address publicly
- The public hearing at the European Parliament provides a high-visibility platform

**As an intelligence source:**
- The ECI register provides real-time data on emerging public concerns and policy demands
- Signature counts and geographic distribution reveal where public sentiment is strongest
- Commission responses to successful ECIs signal policy direction and regulatory appetite

**Monitoring ECIs is relevant for:**
- Tracking emerging regulatory risks in specific policy areas (animal welfare, digital rights, environment)
- Identifying potential allies or coalition partners among civil society organisations
- Understanding the Commission's legislative priorities through its response patterns
- Anticipating EP debates and resolutions triggered by ECI hearings
