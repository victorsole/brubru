# Defence and Space: EDIS, EDIP, the EU Space Strategy for Security and Defence, and IRIS2

**An Analysis of the European Union's Emerging Defence Industrial Policy, Space Security Architecture, and Secure Satellite Connectivity Programme**

**Author:** Victor Sole Ferioli, Founder & Director, Beresol

**Date:** February 2026

**Location:** Brussels, Belgium

---

## Executive Summary

The European Union's defence and space policy landscape has undergone a transformation of historic proportions since Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine in February 2022. What was once a politically sensitive policy area, characterised by intergovernmental caution and industrial fragmentation, has become one of the most dynamic domains of EU legislative activity. Four interconnected initiatives now define the Union's ambitions: the European Defence Industrial Strategy (EDIS), the European Defence Industry Programme (EDIP), the EU Space Strategy for Security and Defence, and the IRIS2 secure satellite connectivity constellation.

**Key Findings:**

- **EDIS** (COM(2024) 150, 5 March 2024) is a non-legislative strategic communication setting quantitative targets for defence industrial readiness by 2030 and 2035, including 40% collaborative procurement and 50% intra-EU defence spending
- **EDIP** (Regulation (EU) 2025/2643, CELEX: 32025R2643) is the first-ever EU regulation establishing a dedicated defence industry programme, with a EUR 1.5 billion budget for 2025-2027, including EUR 300 million for Ukraine support, and introducing the Structure for European Armament Programme (SEAP) as a novel legal framework for joint procurement
- **The EU Space Strategy for Security and Defence** (JOIN(2023) 9, 10 March 2023) is the first comprehensive EU strategy addressing the security and defence dimensions of space, structured around enhancing resilience, leveraging space for security operations, and ensuring autonomous access to space
- **IRIS2** (Regulation (EU) 2023/588, CELEX: 32023R0588) establishes a EUR 10.6 billion multi-orbit secure satellite connectivity constellation of approximately 290 satellites, operated under a 12-year concession by the SpaceRISE consortium (SES, Eutelsat, Hispasat), with governmental services expected by 2030
- **Broader context:** The ReArm Europe plan (Readiness 2030) and the SAFE instrument (EUR 150 billion in loans) represent a paradigm shift in EU defence financing, accelerated by US strategic reorientation under President Trump and NATO's new 5% GDP defence spending target for 2035

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 A Paradigm Shift in EU Defence Policy

For decades, the European Union's role in defence was deliberately constrained. The Treaties preserved defence as a Member State competence, and the EU's Common Security and Defence Policy (CSDP) operated primarily through civilian missions, crisis management operations, and capacity-building in partner countries. Industrial defence policy was considered taboo at the EU level -- Member States guarded their national defence industries as strategic assets, and the European Defence Agency (EDA), established in 2004, had limited budgetary and regulatory influence.

Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine on 24 February 2022 shattered this paradigm. Within weeks, the EU activated the European Peace Facility to finance lethal military assistance for the first time in its history, mobilising over EUR 5 billion in military support for Ukraine by the end of 2024. The Strategic Compass, adopted on 21 March 2022, set a new political framework for EU security and defence, calling for a Rapid Deployment Capacity of 5,000 troops and substantially increased defence investment.

What followed was an unprecedented legislative acceleration. Emergency instruments -- the Act in Support of Ammunition Production (ASAP, Regulation (EU) 2023/1525) and the European Defence Industry Reinforcement through Common Procurement Act (EDIRPA, Regulation (EU) 2023/2418) -- were enacted in 2023 to address immediate ammunition shortages and incentivise joint procurement. By March 2024, the Commission presented the first-ever European Defence Industrial Strategy (EDIS) alongside a proposal for a permanent defence industry programme (EDIP). Within twenty months, EDIP was negotiated, adopted, and entered into force -- an extraordinarily fast legislative timeline for a regulation of this complexity and political sensitivity.

### 1.2 Four Pillars of EU Defence-Space Integration

This report analyses four distinct but deeply interconnected initiatives that collectively define the EU's emerging defence-industrial and space-security architecture:

1. **EDIS** -- the strategic vision, setting long-term objectives and industrial benchmarks
2. **EDIP** -- the legislative instrument translating EDIS into binding rules and funding
3. **The EU Space Strategy for Security and Defence** -- the doctrinal framework linking space capabilities to security requirements
4. **IRIS2** -- the flagship infrastructure programme delivering secure satellite connectivity for governmental, military, and commercial users

Together, these four initiatives represent a coherent attempt to build a European Defence Technological and Industrial Base (EDTIB) that is more integrated, more resilient, and less dependent on non-EU suppliers -- while simultaneously extending European strategic autonomy into the space domain.

## 2. Geopolitical Context

### 2.1 The Russia-Ukraine Factor

The scale of materiel consumption in the Ukraine conflict exposed critical weaknesses in European defence production. Ammunition stocks were depleted to dangerously low levels across most EU Member States within months of the invasion. Artillery ammunition production capacity, which had been rationalised and reduced after the Cold War, proved grossly inadequate for a high-intensity land war. The lesson was clear: European defence industries were configured for peacetime maintenance of small professional armies, not for the sustained production volumes required by a major conventional conflict on European soil.

This realisation drove the emergency measures of 2023 (ASAP, EDIRPA) and underpinned the strategic urgency of EDIS and EDIP in 2024-2025. The inclusion of a dedicated EUR 300 million Ukraine Support Instrument within EDIP reflects the EU's recognition that Ukrainian defence industry integration is not merely an act of solidarity but a strategic necessity -- Ukraine possesses significant defence industrial capacity, battlefield-tested innovation, and the most current operational experience in Europe.

### 2.2 The Transatlantic Defence Spending Debate

The Trump administration's return to power in January 2025 dramatically intensified the transatlantic debate on European defence burden-sharing. At the NATO summit at The Hague in June 2025, allies agreed to a new Defence Investment Commitment of 5% of GDP by 2035, with a minimum of 3.5% dedicated to core defence and an additional 1.5% for critical infrastructure protection. This represented a quantum leap from the 2% GDP target agreed at the 2014 Wales Summit, which many European allies had only recently begun to meet.

In 2024, defence spending across the EU-27 amounted to EUR 343 billion, or 1.9% of GDP. Projections indicated expenditure could reach EUR 392 billion in 2025, representing 2.1% of GDP -- a more than 30% real increase from 2021 levels. Yet even at 2.1%, the EU remains far short of the new 5% NATO target, and the gap underscores the urgency of the EU's defence financing initiatives.

President Trump's explicit scepticism about the US security guarantee to Europe, his administration's overtures toward a negotiated settlement in Ukraine, and the broader US strategic pivot toward the Indo-Pacific have collectively reinforced the political case for European defence autonomy. The EU Defence White Paper -- Readiness 2030 -- acknowledged that "the pace of traditional allies and partners, including the US, pivoting away from Europe is happening faster than many had anticipated."

### 2.3 NATO-EU Cooperation and Complementarity

The EDIS communication and EDIP regulation are careful to frame EU defence industrial policy as complementary to NATO, not duplicative. The EU does not seek to create a parallel military alliance but rather to ensure that European defence industries can produce the capabilities that both EU and NATO force planning processes require. In practice, however, tensions exist: the initial EDIP proposal's preference for European-sourced procurement drew criticism from the United States and from EU Member States with strong transatlantic procurement relationships. The final EDIP text modulates these provisions, permitting third-country participation under certain conditions while maintaining a structural preference for European content.

## 3. EDIS: The European Defence Industrial Strategy

### 3.1 Legal Framework and Identification

| Field | Value |
|-------|-------|
| **Full title** | European Defence Industrial Strategy: Achieving EU Readiness through a Responsive and Resilient European Defence Industry |
| **Short name** | EDIS |
| **Document type** | Joint Communication |
| **Reference** | JOIN(2024) 10 final, accompanied by COM(2024) 150 final (EDIP proposal) |
| **Date** | 5 March 2024 |
| **Authors** | European Commission and High Representative of the Union for Foreign Affairs and Security Policy |
| **Legal nature** | Non-legislative strategic communication (not binding) |

EDIS is not a regulation or directive; it is a strategic communication that sets the political vision and quantitative benchmarks for EU defence industrial policy through 2035. Its operational arm is EDIP, which translates selected EDIS objectives into binding legislation and EU budget commitments.

### 3.2 Diagnosis: The Fragmentation Problem

EDIS opens with a frank diagnosis of the European defence industrial landscape. Key findings include:

- **Procurement fragmentation:** Following Russia's invasion of Ukraine, 78% of defence equipment procured by EU Member States between June 2022 and June 2023 came from non-EU suppliers, primarily the United States, South Korea, and Israel. This represents a massive leakage of defence investment away from the EDTIB.
- **Duplication of systems:** The EU operates approximately 178 different weapon system types compared to 30 in the United States, leading to interoperability challenges, higher unit costs, and logistical complexity.
- **Underinvestment in collaborative procurement:** Only around 18% of defence equipment is procured collaboratively among Member States, far below the EDA benchmark of 35%.
- **Industrial consolidation gaps:** Unlike the US defence sector, which consolidated into a small number of primes (Lockheed Martin, RTX, Northrop Grumman, Boeing, General Dynamics), the European defence industry remains fragmented along national lines, with duplicative capabilities and limited cross-border integration.

### 3.3 Quantitative Targets

EDIS establishes the following headline targets:

| Target | Deadline | Baseline (2023) |
|--------|----------|-----------------|
| Procure at least **40%** of defence equipment collaboratively | 2030 | ~18% |
| Spend at least **50%** of defence procurement budget within the EU | 2030 | ~22% (post-Ukraine surge) |
| Increase intra-EU defence spending to **60%** of procurement budget | 2035 | -- |
| Achieve **35%** of defence market value in intra-EU trade | 2030 | -- |

These targets are politically ambitious but legally non-binding. Their enforcement relies on peer pressure, EDA benchmarking, and the financial incentives embedded in EDIP.

### 3.4 Strategic Pillars

EDIS is structured around several key action areas:

1. **Demand-side measures:** Incentivising collaborative procurement through EU funding (EDIP grants, VAT exemptions via SEAP), and progressively conditioning EU financial support on joint purchasing.

2. **Supply-side measures:** Strengthening EDTIB production capacity, securing supply chains for critical raw materials and components, and reducing dependencies on non-EU sources for semiconductors, energetic materials, and specialised metals.

3. **Innovation and dual-use:** Promoting civil-military synergies, integrating emerging technologies (AI, autonomous systems, quantum, hypersonics) into defence capabilities, and leveraging the European Defence Fund (EDF) for R&D.

4. **Workforce and skills:** Addressing the skilled labour shortage in European defence manufacturing through dedicated training programmes and mobility schemes.

5. **Export policy coherence:** Working toward a more common EU approach to defence exports, including through the SEAP framework's collective export decision mechanisms.

6. **Ukraine integration:** Facilitating the progressive integration of the Ukrainian defence industry into the EDTIB, including through joint ventures, technology transfer, and participation in EDIP-funded actions.

## 4. EDIP: The European Defence Industry Programme

### 4.1 Legal Framework and Identification

| Field | Value |
|-------|-------|
| **Full title** | Regulation (EU) 2025/2643 of the European Parliament and of the Council of 16 December 2025 establishing the European Defence Industry Programme and a framework of measures to ensure the timely availability and supply of defence products |
| **Short name** | EDIP |
| **CELEX number** | 32025R2643 |
| **OJ reference** | OJ L, 29.12.2025 |
| **Legal basis** | Articles 114 and 173 TFEU (internal market and industrial policy) |
| **Procedure** | 2024/0061(COD) -- Ordinary legislative procedure |
| **Commission proposal** | COM(2024) 150 final, 5 March 2024 |
| **EP committees** | Joint procedure: SEDE (Security and Defence) and ITRE (Industry, Research and Energy) |
| **EP rapporteurs** | Francois-Xavier Bellamy (EPP, France) -- ITRE; Raphael Glucksmann (S&D, France) -- SEDE |
| **Provisional agreement** | 16 October 2025 |
| **EP plenary adoption** | 25 November 2025 |
| **Council final approval** | 8 December 2025 |
| **Signature** | 17 December 2025 |
| **Entry into force** | 30 December 2025 |

EDIP is a milestone in EU integration -- the first-ever EU regulation establishing a dedicated and permanent programme for the defence industry. It builds on and replaces the emergency instruments ASAP (Regulation (EU) 2023/1525) and EDIRPA (Regulation (EU) 2023/2418), giving them a "longer-term and more structured perspective."

### 4.2 Budget

EDIP provides EUR 1.5 billion in EU budget grants for the period 2025-2027. Of this amount:

- **EUR 1.2 billion** for the core programme (joint procurement incentives, production capacity strengthening, supply chain security, interoperability)
- **EUR 300 million** for the **Ukraine Support Instrument**, dedicated to modernising Ukraine's defence industry and fostering its integration into the EDTIB

The budget is modest relative to Member States' national defence procurement budgets (collectively over EUR 100 billion per year), but EDIP's significance lies more in the legal frameworks it creates than in its direct funding volume.

### 4.3 The Structure for European Armament Programme (SEAP)

The most innovative element of EDIP is the **Structure for European Armament Programme (SEAP)** -- a new, voluntary legal framework providing a ready-made institutional vehicle for Member States to cooperate on defence equipment throughout its full lifecycle, from development through procurement to maintenance and disposal.

Key features of SEAP:

- **Legal personality:** A SEAP is endowed with legal personality, functioning as an international organisation under EU law. This is critical because it enables the SEAP to own assets, enter into contracts, and serve as the procurement entity.

- **Minimum participation:** At least three Member States are required to establish a SEAP. Ukraine and associated countries are also eligible to participate.

- **VAT exemption:** Defence equipment jointly owned through a SEAP benefits from VAT exemptions, analogous to the treatment of NATO-procured equipment. For large-scale procurement (e.g., a joint order of main battle tanks or frigates), this represents savings of 15-25% depending on the Member State's VAT rate.

- **Collective export decisions:** SEAPs establish frameworks for participating Member States to agree collectively on export policy for jointly produced or procured equipment. This addresses one of the most politically sensitive barriers to European defence cooperation -- the divergence in national export control policies.

- **Enhanced EU funding rates:** Actions carried out through a SEAP receive higher EDIP grant rates (up to 25% of contract value, compared to 15% for standard joint procurement).

### 4.4 Supply Chain Security

EDIP introduces provisions for **defence supply chain mapping**, requiring the identification of critical dependencies in European defence production. The Commission may designate "defence-relevant products in crisis" and activate supply security measures, including priority-rated orders (drawing on the model established under the Single Market Emergency Instrument).

The regulation also addresses the progressive reduction of dependence on components sourced from non-allied countries, particularly in areas such as:

- Semiconductors and advanced electronics
- Energetic materials (explosives, propellants)
- Rare earth elements and specialised metals
- Satellite and space components

### 4.5 Defence Readiness State

EDIP introduces a **Defence Readiness State** mechanism, which can be activated by the Council to trigger enhanced industrial mobilisation measures. When activated, this framework may include accelerated procurement procedures, streamlined export licensing, and priority access to critical materials -- effectively creating a "defence production emergency" toolkit for the EU.

### 4.6 Relationship to the European Defence Fund

EDIP is designed to complement the European Defence Fund (EDF), which provides EU funding for collaborative defence research (EUR 2.7 billion for the 2021-2027 MFF) and capability development. While the EDF focuses on the R&D phase, EDIP covers the subsequent stages of the capability lifecycle -- production, procurement, maintenance, and disposal. Together, they create a potential end-to-end EU funding pathway for defence capabilities, from research concept to fielded equipment.

## 5. The EU Space Strategy for Security and Defence

### 5.1 Legal Framework and Identification

| Field | Value |
|-------|-------|
| **Full title** | Joint Communication to the European Parliament and the Council on an EU Space Strategy for Security and Defence |
| **Reference** | JOIN(2023) 9 final |
| **Date** | 10 March 2023 |
| **Authors** | European Commission and High Representative |
| **Document type** | Joint Communication (non-legislative) |
| **Council conclusions** | November 2023 |

This is the first-ever EU strategy specifically dedicated to the security and defence dimensions of space. It complements the broader EU Space Programme (Regulation (EU) 2021/696), which governs Galileo, Copernicus, EGNOS, and Space Situational Awareness (SSA), and the IRIS2 Regulation for secure connectivity.

### 5.2 Threat Assessment

The strategy begins with a comprehensive threat assessment of the space domain:

- **Anti-satellite weapons (ASAT):** Russia's destructive test of a direct-ascent ASAT weapon in November 2021 (which destroyed the Cosmos 1408 satellite and created over 1,500 pieces of trackable debris) demonstrated the vulnerability of space assets. China has conducted similar tests.

- **Cyber attacks on space infrastructure:** The Viasat KA-SAT attack on 24 February 2022 -- timed to coincide with the Russian invasion of Ukraine -- disabled satellite internet terminals across Europe, affecting Ukrainian military communications and also disrupting wind turbine operations in Germany. This demonstrated the dual-use vulnerability of commercial space infrastructure.

- **Electronic warfare:** GPS/GNSS jamming and spoofing have become routine in conflict zones and along NATO's eastern flank. Russian electronic warfare capabilities have demonstrated the ability to deny or degrade satellite navigation over large areas.

- **Space debris weaponisation:** The deliberate creation of debris fields in orbits used by adversary satellites represents an emerging threat vector.

- **Dual-use proliferation:** The rapid growth of commercial space capabilities (small satellites, mega-constellations, commercial imagery) creates new intelligence and reconnaissance capabilities that may be leveraged by state and non-state actors.

### 5.3 Three Pillars

The strategy is organised around three pillars:

**Pillar I: Resilience of EU Space Systems**
- Enhance the resilience and protection of EU space infrastructure (Galileo, Copernicus, IRIS2) against physical, cyber, and electronic threats
- Develop EU Space Situational Awareness (SSA) capabilities for collision avoidance and debris tracking
- Establish rapid-response protocols for threats to space assets, including contingency communications and backup navigation
- Develop the EU SST (Space Surveillance and Tracking) system, with operational deployment from 2025

**Pillar II: Space as an Enabler for Security and Defence**
- Leverage Copernicus for military-relevant Earth observation (border surveillance, maritime monitoring, environmental intelligence)
- Use Galileo's Public Regulated Service (PRS) for secure, encrypted navigation for military and governmental users
- Develop space-based early warning capabilities for ballistic missile detection
- Integrate space data into the EU's intelligence analysis capacity (EU INTCEN, EU Military Staff)

**Pillar III: Autonomous Access to Space**
- Ensure the EU maintains sovereign access to space through European launch capabilities (Ariane 6, Vega-C)
- Reduce dependence on non-EU launch providers (particularly given that Russian Soyuz launches from Kourou ceased after the Ukraine invasion)
- Support the development of next-generation European launch vehicles, including reusable systems
- Promote a competitive European small-launcher ecosystem

### 5.4 Implementation Status

Since the strategy's adoption, several implementation actions have advanced:

- The EU SST consortium has expanded its operational capabilities for debris tracking and collision avoidance
- Galileo Second Generation (G2G) development continues, with new frequencies, higher transmitter power, and centimetre-level accuracy expected from 2025 onwards
- The IRIS2 concession contract was signed in December 2024 (see Section 6)
- The EU Space Act proposal (COM(2025) 335) was tabled in July 2025, aiming to create a comprehensive regulatory framework for the European space sector, including provisions for space traffic management and in-orbit servicing

## 6. IRIS2: Secure Satellite Connectivity

### 6.1 Legal Framework and Identification

| Field | Value |
|-------|-------|
| **Full title** | Regulation (EU) 2023/588 of the European Parliament and of the Council of 15 March 2023 establishing the Union Secure Connectivity Programme for the period 2023-2027 |
| **Short name** | IRIS2 (Infrastructure for Resilience, Interconnectivity and Security by Satellite) |
| **CELEX number** | 32023R0588 |
| **OJ reference** | OJ L 79, 17.3.2023, p. 1 |
| **Legal basis** | Article 189(2) TFEU (European space policy) |
| **Entry into force** | 20 March 2023 |
| **Application period** | 2023-2027 (programme); operational services to 2037 (concession) |

### 6.2 Programme Rationale

IRIS2 addresses a critical capability gap: the EU currently lacks a sovereign, end-to-end encrypted satellite communication system for governmental and military use. EU institutions, diplomatic services, and Member State governments rely on a patchwork of national military satellite systems (France's Syracuse, Italy's SICRAL, Spain's XTAR, the UK's Skynet -- now post-Brexit) and commercial providers. This fragmented landscape creates vulnerabilities in terms of coverage, interoperability, encryption standards, and strategic dependence.

The Viasat cyber attack of February 2022 demonstrated in real time how reliance on commercially operated satellite communications can be disrupted by state-sponsored attackers. IRIS2 is the EU's strategic response -- a sovereign, resilient, multi-orbit satellite constellation providing encrypted communications with guaranteed availability for governmental users.

### 6.3 Constellation Architecture

IRIS2 will deploy approximately **290 satellites** in a multi-orbit architecture:

| Orbit | Altitude | Satellites | Purpose |
|-------|----------|------------|---------|
| **LEO** (Low Earth Orbit) | ~1,200 km | ~264 | Low-latency broadband, high-throughput data |
| **MEO** (Medium Earth Orbit) | ~8,000 km | ~18 | Complementary coverage, resilience |
| **GEO** (Geostationary Orbit) | ~36,000 km | ~8 | Continuous coverage over fixed regions |

The multi-orbit approach provides resilience through redundancy -- if one orbital layer is degraded (whether by technical failure, debris, or hostile action), the remaining layers can maintain service continuity. It also optimises the latency-throughput trade-off: LEO satellites provide low latency (comparable to terrestrial fibre), while GEO satellites provide continuous coverage over strategic areas.

### 6.4 Funding and Concession Model

IRIS2 operates under a **public-private partnership (PPP) concession model**, distinguishing it from Galileo and Copernicus, which are fully publicly funded and operated.

| Funding Source | Amount | Percentage |
|----------------|--------|------------|
| **EU budget** | ~EUR 6.0 billion | ~57% |
| **ESA** | ~EUR 550 million | ~5% |
| **Private investment (SpaceRISE)** | ~EUR 4.0 billion | ~38% |
| **Total** | **~EUR 10.6 billion** | 100% |

The concession contract runs for 12 years and covers design, development, deployment, and operation of the constellation.

### 6.5 The SpaceRISE Consortium

The **SpaceRISE** (Space Consortium for a Resilient, Interconnected and Secure Europe) consortium was selected by the European Commission in October 2024 and signed the concession contract on **16 December 2024** in Brussels.

SpaceRISE comprises three European satellite operators:
- **SES** (Luxembourg) -- lead operator, MEO/GEO expertise
- **Eutelsat** (France/now merged with OneWeb) -- LEO/GEO expertise
- **Hispasat** (Spain) -- GEO operator

The core industrial subcontracting team includes:
- **Airbus Defence and Space** (France/Germany/Spain/UK)
- **Thales Alenia Space** (France/Italy)
- **OHB** (Germany)
- **Telespazio** (Italy/France)
- **Deutsche Telekom** (Germany)
- **Orange** (France)
- **Hisdesat** (Spain)
- **Aerospacelab** (Belgium)

In August 2025, the consortium narrowed the field of potential prime contractors for satellite manufacturing to **Airbus** (France) and **Aerospacelab** (Belgium). As of late 2025, the programme had entered the active procurement phase, with preparation of Request for Proposal (RFP) documentation for satellite hardware and launch services.

### 6.6 Service Tiers

IRIS2 will provide two tiers of service:

**Governmental Services (priority):**
- Encrypted, jam-resistant communications for EU institutions, agencies, and bodies
- Secure connectivity for Member State governments, diplomatic services, and military operations
- Sovereign ground segment infrastructure under EU control
- Integration with the European External Action Service (EEAS) and CSDP missions
- Encrypted governmental services expected to begin operating by **2030**

**Commercial Services:**
- High-speed broadband for underserved and remote areas
- Connectivity for aviation, maritime, and land transport
- Internet of Things (IoT) and machine-to-machine applications
- Revenue from commercial services helps offset public investment and sustain the PPP model

### 6.7 Integration with EuroQCI

A distinguishing feature of IRIS2 is its integration with the **European Quantum Communication Infrastructure (EuroQCI)**. EuroQCI will deploy quantum key distribution (QKD) capabilities across both terrestrial fibre networks and satellite links, providing theoretically unbreakable encryption based on the laws of quantum mechanics rather than computational complexity.

All 27 EU Member States have signed the EuroQCI Declaration, committing to building a pan-European quantum communication network. IRIS2 will carry QKD payloads on selected satellites, enabling:

- Quantum-secured key exchange between satellite ground stations across Europe
- Encryption keys that cannot be intercepted or copied, even by quantum computers
- A future-proof communication layer resilient to the threat of quantum computing to current cryptographic standards

### 6.8 Timeline

| Phase | Period | Activities |
|-------|--------|------------|
| **Design and development** | 2025-2028 | Satellite design, Critical Design Review (CDR), ground segment build |
| **Manufacturing** | 2026-2029 | First batch satellite production, launch procurement |
| **Deployment** | 2029-2030 | Phased satellite launches, commissioning |
| **Initial operational capability** | 2030 | Governmental services begin |
| **Full operational capability** | 2031 | Complete constellation, all services active |
| **Exploitation** | 2030-2037 | Full operations under concession contract |

## 7. The Wider Defence Financing Architecture

### 7.1 ReArm Europe / Readiness 2030

In March 2025, the European Commission presented the Defence White Paper -- **Readiness 2030** -- alongside the legislative proposal for the **Security Action for Europe (SAFE)** instrument. Together, these form the "ReArm Europe" plan, representing a step-change in EU defence financing ambitions.

**SAFE** provides a EUR 150 billion loan facility to Member States for defence investments, with the following characteristics:

- EU-backed bonds issued at favourable interest rates (leveraging the EU's AAA credit rating)
- Loans disbursed to Member States for defence capability investments aligned with EU priorities
- Adopted by the Council in May 2025
- As of early 2026, 19 Member States have requested SAFE loans
- Priority areas: air and missile defence, drones and counter-drone systems, cyber capabilities, ammunition and precision munitions, and naval platforms

### 7.2 The European Defence Fund

The European Defence Fund (EDF), operational since 2021, provides EUR 7.953 billion for the 2021-2027 MFF period, split between:

- **Research window:** EUR 2.651 billion for collaborative defence research
- **Development window:** EUR 5.302 billion for collaborative capability development projects

The EDF has funded over 100 projects since its launch, covering areas from next-generation fighter technologies (linked to FCAS/SCAF and Tempest/GCAP) to cyber defence, underwater unmanned vehicles, and space-based surveillance.

### 7.3 The Integrated Framework

The current EU defence financing architecture can be understood as a pipeline:

| Phase | Instrument | Budget |
|-------|-----------|--------|
| **R&D** | European Defence Fund (EDF) | EUR 8 billion (2021-2027) |
| **Production and procurement** | EDIP | EUR 1.5 billion (2025-2027) |
| **Financing/loans** | SAFE | EUR 150 billion (loans) |
| **Emergency procurement** | EDIRPA/ASAP (predecessors, now superseded) | ~EUR 1 billion |
| **External military support** | European Peace Facility (EPF) | EUR 17 billion (expanded) |
| **Space infrastructure** | IRIS2 | EUR 10.6 billion (2023-2037) |

## 8. Practical Implications for Industry

### 8.1 Defence Contractors (Primes and Tier 1)

For major European defence contractors (Airbus Defence and Space, Leonardo, Rheinmetall, KNDS, Dassault, Saab, BAE Systems' European operations), EDIS and EDIP create both opportunities and structural pressures:

- **Opportunities:** EDIP grants (up to 25% of contract value for SEAP-based procurement) create financial incentives for cross-border industrial teaming. Companies that position themselves as participants in multinational SEAP programmes will have preferential access to EU funding.

- **Scale effects:** The 40% collaborative procurement target, if achieved, would represent a fundamental shift in order volumes. A joint order for, say, infantry fighting vehicles from five Member States through a SEAP would generate far larger production runs than five separate national procurements, reducing unit costs and increasing production efficiency.

- **European content requirements:** EDIP's structural preference for European-sourced content will benefit companies with integrated European supply chains. Contractors reliant on US-sourced components (particularly in avionics, sensors, and propulsion) may face pressure to develop European alternatives.

- **Workforce implications:** EDIS identifies a skilled labour shortage in European defence manufacturing. Primes will need to invest in apprenticeships, technical training, and talent retention to meet expanded production requirements.

### 8.2 SMEs and Mid-Cap Defence Companies

EDIP includes specific provisions for small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) and mid-cap companies:

- Dedicated access provisions to ensure SMEs can participate in SEAP programmes and EDIP-funded actions
- Higher funding rates for actions involving broad supplier distribution across the EDTIB
- Supply chain mapping obligations that will increase the visibility of Tier 2 and Tier 3 suppliers
- Cross-border market access facilitation for companies currently restricted to their national defence markets

For mid-cap defence specialists (e.g., Hensoldt in sensors, Diehl Defence in munitions, Nammo in ammunition, MBDA in missiles), EDIP's cross-border procurement frameworks represent an opportunity to scale beyond national markets.

### 8.3 Space Companies and Satellite Operators

The IRIS2 programme represents the largest single EU investment in space infrastructure since Galileo. For European space companies, the implications are significant:

- **Satellite manufacturers:** Airbus Defence and Space and Aerospacelab are the shortlisted prime contractors for IRIS2 satellite manufacturing. OHB, Thales Alenia Space, and their supply chains will participate as subcontractors.

- **Launch service providers:** IRIS2 will require a sustained campaign of satellite launches over 2029-2030. Arianespace (Ariane 6), Avio (Vega-C), and potentially new European launch providers will compete for launch contracts.

- **Satellite operators:** SES, Eutelsat (merged with OneWeb), and Hispasat are the SpaceRISE consortium operators, but the commercial service tier of IRIS2 will also interact with the broader European telecommunications market.

- **Ground segment companies:** Telespazio, Deutsche Telekom, and Orange are involved in the ground segment, which includes antennas, data centres, network operations centres, and user terminals.

### 8.4 Dual-Use Technology Firms

Companies operating at the civil-military interface face particular opportunities:

- **Cybersecurity firms:** IRIS2's integration with EuroQCI creates demand for quantum key distribution hardware, post-quantum cryptography solutions, and satellite cybersecurity services.

- **AI and autonomous systems:** EDIS identifies artificial intelligence, autonomous systems, and robotics as priority capability areas. Dual-use AI companies with defence applications (surveillance, decision support, predictive maintenance, logistics optimisation) are well positioned.

- **Semiconductor companies:** Defence supply chain security provisions in EDIP will drive demand for European-sourced radiation-hardened and military-grade semiconductors. The European Chips Act (Regulation (EU) 2023/1781) and EDIP create complementary demand signals.

- **Drone manufacturers:** Both EDIP and the SAFE instrument prioritise unmanned systems. European drone manufacturers (military and dual-use) should anticipate growing demand and regulatory attention.

## 9. Implementation Challenges and Risks

### 9.1 Political Will and Sovereignty Concerns

The success of EDIS targets depends entirely on Member States' willingness to shift procurement from national to collaborative channels. Defence procurement is deeply entangled with national industrial policy, regional employment, and political sovereignty. France, Germany, Italy, and Spain each have distinct defence industrial champions, and the incentive to protect national industries often outweighs the economic logic of collaboration.

Article 346 TFEU, which allows Member States to derogate from EU internal market rules for essential security interests, remains available and frequently invoked. Unless the political culture around defence procurement changes fundamentally, the 40% collaborative procurement target by 2030 will be extremely challenging to meet.

### 9.2 Budget Adequacy

EDIP's EUR 1.5 billion budget for 2025-2027 is modest by any measure -- it represents approximately 0.5% of annual EU Member State defence procurement spending. The programme's impact depends less on direct EU funding and more on the regulatory frameworks (SEAP) and incentive structures it creates. The real question is whether the next MFF (2028-2034) will substantially increase EU defence budget allocations. Early signals suggest it will, but the budgetary negotiation will be complex.

### 9.3 IRIS2 Technical and Schedule Risks

A 290-satellite multi-orbit constellation is technically ambitious. While the individual satellite technology is mature, the integration challenges are substantial:

- **Manufacturing scale-up:** Producing 264 LEO satellites requires either a single high-rate production line (similar to OneWeb or Starlink approaches) or a distributed manufacturing model across multiple European sites. Europe has limited experience with satellite mass production at this scale.

- **Launch cadence:** Deploying the constellation by 2030-2031 requires a sustained launch campaign that will stress European launch capacity, particularly if Ariane 6 availability is constrained.

- **Ground segment complexity:** The multi-orbit architecture requires a sophisticated ground segment capable of managing handoffs between LEO, MEO, and GEO satellites seamlessly.

- **Cost overruns:** Large European space programmes have historically experienced significant cost and schedule overruns (Galileo's original 2008 operational date slipped to 2016). The PPP model creates additional complexity in managing the allocation of cost overrun risk between public and private partners.

### 9.4 Transatlantic Friction

EDIP's European content preferences and the broader "buy European" thrust of EDIS create friction with the United States. American defence companies (Lockheed Martin, RTX, Northrop Grumman) have substantial European operations and supply chain relationships. If EDIP is perceived as protectionist, it could trigger reciprocal "buy American" strengthening in US defence procurement, potentially harming European defence companies with US market exposure (BAE Systems, Airbus, Leonardo).

The balance between European strategic autonomy and transatlantic interoperability remains one of the most delicate political challenges in EDIP implementation.

---

## 10. Conclusions

The four initiatives analysed in this report -- EDIS, EDIP, the EU Space Strategy for Security and Defence, and IRIS2 -- represent a qualitative transformation of the EU's role in defence and space. For the first time, the Union has a strategic vision for defence industrial policy (EDIS), a legislative instrument to implement it (EDIP), a doctrinal framework linking space to security (the Space Strategy), and a flagship infrastructure programme delivering sovereign secure connectivity (IRIS2).

The scale of ambition is unprecedented. The combined financing architecture -- EDF for R&D, EDIP for procurement, SAFE for investment loans, IRIS2 for space infrastructure, and the EPF for external military support -- represents tens of billions of euros in EU-level defence engagement. The SEAP legal framework, if widely adopted, could fundamentally restructure how European defence procurement is conducted.

Yet the challenges are formidable. Decades of fragmentation, national industrial protectionism, Article 346 TFEU derogations, and divergent strategic cultures cannot be overcome by legislation alone. The test will come in the next two to three years: will Member States actually use SEAPs? Will collaborative procurement rise toward the 40% target? Will IRIS2 remain on schedule and within budget? Will the next MFF allocate the substantially increased defence funding that the current framework demands?

The geopolitical pressure is, however, unrelenting. Russia's war in Ukraine shows no signs of imminent resolution. The US strategic pivot toward the Indo-Pacific and the transatlantic defence spending debate make European defence autonomy not a luxury but an imperative. In this context, EDIS, EDIP, the Space Strategy, and IRIS2 are not merely policy initiatives -- they are the institutional architecture for a Europe that must learn to provide for more of its own security.

For EU policy professionals, defence contractors, space companies, and dual-use technology firms, these developments create a fundamentally new operating environment. Understanding the regulatory frameworks, funding mechanisms, and strategic direction set by these four initiatives is essential for any stakeholder seeking to engage with the emerging European defence-industrial ecosystem.
