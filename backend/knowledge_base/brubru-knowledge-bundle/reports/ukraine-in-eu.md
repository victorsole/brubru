# Ukraine's Path to EU Membership: The €136 Billion Question Reshaping Europe

**Analysis of Budgetary, Institutional, and Agricultural Implications**

**Author:** Victor Solé Ferioli, Founder & Director, Beresol

**Date:** December 2025

**Location:** Brussels, Belgium

---

## Executive Summary

Ukraine's accession to the European Union would fundamentally transform the bloc, making it the **fifth-largest member state** overnight while requiring an estimated **€110-136 billion in net transfers over seven years**—roughly 0.13% of EU GDP. With accession negotiations opened in June 2024 and a potential 2030 target date discussed by EU officials, this analysis examines the full scope of implications across political institutions, budgetary impacts, agricultural markets, the multiannual financial framework, euro adoption, and necessary institutional reforms.

**Key Findings:**

- **Budgetary impact:** €18-19 billion annually in net transfers—significant but manageable at 0.13% of EU GDP
- **Agricultural dominance:** Ukraine's 32.7 million hectares would add 21% to EU farmland, making CAP reform essential
- **Institutional challenges:** The 751 MEP cap would be breached, requiring treaty amendment or seat redistribution
- **Euro adoption:** Realistically decades away; Ukraine currently fails all five Maastricht criteria by substantial margins
- **Political obstacles:** 13 member states oppose treaty changes needed for efficient enlargement

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 Context

Ukraine's European integration has accelerated dramatically since Russia's full-scale invasion in February 2022. The European Council granted Ukraine candidate status in June 2022—an unprecedented four months after application—and opened accession negotiations in June 2024. This represents the most rapid progression in EU enlargement history.

The core finding across all dimensions is that Ukrainian membership is technically manageable but demands significant EU preparation. Multiple think tank analyses, including from Bruegel and CEPS, conclude the budgetary impact would be "relatively manageable" and would not transform current net beneficiaries into net payers. However, institutional reforms—particularly around qualified majority voting and European Parliament size—remain politically contentious.

### 1.2 Scope

This report examines: (1) institutional implications for EU decision-making; (2) budgetary impacts and transfer projections; (3) agricultural sector integration challenges; (4) multiannual financial framework provisions; (5) euro adoption timeline; and (6) institutional reform debates.

### 1.3 Methodology

This analysis synthesises primary EU institutional sources (European Commission, Council, Parliament), think tank analyses from Bruegel, CEPS, and Institut Delors, Eurostat data, and academic literature on EU enlargement. Financial projections draw primarily from Bruegel's March 2024 comprehensive assessment.

## 2. Institutional impact: a political heavyweight

### 2.1 Population and political weight

Ukraine's **population of approximately 37-38 million** (down from 43.5 million pre-war) would make it the EU's fifth-largest member state, ranking between Poland (36.8 million) and Spain (48.1 million).

**Table 1: EU population ranking with Ukraine**

| Rank | Member State | Population (millions) | Share of EU-28 |
|------|--------------|----------------------|----------------|
| 1 | Germany | 84.4 | 17.8% |
| 2 | France | 68.2 | 14.4% |
| 3 | Italy | 58.9 | 12.4% |
| 4 | Spain | 48.1 | 10.1% |
| **5** | **Ukraine** | **37.5** | **7.9%** |
| 6 | Poland | 36.8 | 7.8% |

*Source: Eurostat; World Bank (2024)*

### 2.2 European Parliament representation

Under the principle of degressive proportionality governing EP seat allocation, Ukraine would receive an estimated **52-59 MEPs**—comparable to Poland's 53 seats.

This creates an immediate institutional problem: the EU currently has 720 MEPs, and adding Ukraine would push the total to approximately 770-775, **breaching the Treaty of Lisbon's cap of 751 MEPs**. Resolving this requires either:

- Treaty amendment to raise the ceiling
- Redistribution of existing seats—politically difficult as it would require some member states to lose representation

### 2.3 Council voting dynamics

In the Council of the EU, Ukraine's population would represent approximately **7.8% of the EU total**, giving it substantial weight under qualified majority voting. The current QMV system requires 55% of member states representing 65% of the population.

Ukraine's entry would make **blocking coalitions easier to form**—just four large states including Ukraine could theoretically block decisions. For unanimity-based decisions in areas like Common Foreign and Security Policy, Ukraine would gain full veto power, fundamentally altering EU decision-making dynamics on matters directly affecting its security interests.

## 3. Budgetary reality: the cost of integration

### 3.1 Economic starting point

Ukraine would enter the EU as by far its poorest member state. Current estimates place Ukraine's **GDP per capita in purchasing power standards at roughly 30-35% of the EU average**—approximately half that of Bulgaria, currently the EU's lowest.

Ukraine's 2024 nominal GDP stands at approximately **$184 billion**, devastated by war from pre-2022 levels of approximately $200 billion.

### 3.2 Net Transfer projections

Bruegel's comprehensive March 2024 analysis projects Ukraine would be a net recipient of **€136 billion over a seven-year MFF period** under a baseline scenario (territorial integrity restored) or €110 billion under an alternative scenario assuming 20% permanent territory loss.

**Table 2: Projected EU budget allocations to Ukraine (7-year MFF)**

| Policy Area | Allocation (€ billion) | Share of Total |
|-------------|----------------------|----------------|
| Common Agricultural Policy | 85 | 62% |
| Cohesion Policy | 32 | 24% |
| Other EU Programmes | 7 | 5% |
| **Ukraine's Contributions** | **(14)** | — |
| **Net Transfers** | **110-136** | 100% |

*Source: Bruegel (2024)*

This breaks down to approximately **€18-19 billion annually in net transfers**, making Ukraine the largest recipient in EU history—roughly 2-3 times Poland's current net receipts of €7.1 billion annually.

![Ukraine EU Budget Impact: €136 Billion Over 7 Years](/open_reports/images/ukraine_budget_allocation.svg)

### 3.3 The Poland precedent

Poland's experience since 2004 offers the closest comparison. Poland received approximately **€245-250 billion gross (€161-175 billion net)** over 20 years, transforming its GDP per capita from 50% to 80% of the EU average.

Ukraine starts from a far weaker position—its GDP per capita is significantly lower than Poland's was at accession—and faces war devastation requiring separate reconstruction funding estimated at **€450-486 billion**.

## 4. Agricultural integration: the greatest challenge

### 4.1 Scale of Ukrainian agriculture

Ukraine possesses **32.7 million hectares of arable land** (26.5 million currently under government control), representing approximately **21% of an enlarged EU's total farmland**—more than France, currently the EU's largest agricultural state.

**Table 3: agricultural land comparison**

| Country | Arable Land (million ha) | Share of EU-28 |
|---------|-------------------------|----------------|
| France | 27.8 | 17.8% |
| **Ukraine** | **32.7** | **21.0%** |
| Spain | 23.8 | 15.3% |
| Germany | 16.7 | 10.7% |
| Poland | 14.3 | 9.2% |

*Source: Eurostat; FAO (2024)*

### 4.2 Production impact

Ukraine's agricultural production would dramatically reshape EU markets:

- **Maize:** 42 million tonnes in 2021, adding 36% to combined EU production
- **Sunflower:** World's largest producer, controlling 61% of global output
- **Agricultural exports:** $27.8 billion in 2021, representing 41% of Ukraine's total exports

### 4.3 CAP implications

Estimates for Ukraine's CAP allocation range from **€53 billion to €96.5 billion over seven years**, depending on whether transition periods and payment caps apply. Under current rules without modification, Ukraine would become the **single largest CAP recipient**, surpassing France (currently €9.5 billion annually).

This would require either:
- A 22-25% increase in CAP spending, or
- Significant reductions to existing recipients

### 4.4 Political sensitivity: the 2022-2024 experience

The experience with Ukrainian agricultural imports foreshadows the political sensitivity. After the EU eliminated tariffs and quotas in June 2022 to facilitate wartime exports:

- Grain accumulated in Eastern European countries rather than transiting onward
- Poland, Hungary, Slovakia, Bulgaria, and Romania imposed unilateral import bans in April 2023
- Polish farmers blocked border crossings for months and destroyed Ukrainian grain shipments
- The EU disbursed €156 million in compensation
- "Emergency brake" safeguard mechanisms were introduced in March 2024

Key concerns from EU farmers centre on competitive advantages: Ukrainian grain prices were **45% lower** than EU prices post-invasion, large Ukrainian agricultural holdings achieve economies of scale impossible for EU family farms, and Ukrainian producers currently face fewer environmental and animal welfare regulations.

## 5. The Post-2027 MFF and enlargement provisions

### 5.1 Current framework

The current MFF 2021-2027 totals **€1,074.3 billion** (2018 prices), representing approximately 1.12% of EU GDP. The framework has already been stretched by €64.6 billion in June 2023 mid-term revisions, partly for Ukraine support.

### 5.2 The 2028-2034 proposal

The European Commission's July 2025 proposal for MFF 2028-2034 represents the **largest budget in EU history at nearly €2 trillion** (1.26% of EU GNI). Crucially, it includes:

- **€100 billion mobilizable for Ukraine** outside normal MFF ceilings—explicitly anticipating potential accession
- Consolidation of Ukraine Facility, NDICI, IPA III, and Western Balkans facilities into a single €200 billion Global Europe Instrument
- Article 11 provisions allowing MFF revision for new member accession

### 5.3 Accession timeline projections

| Source | Target Date | Status |
|--------|-------------|--------|
| President von der Leyen | 2030 possible | Official statement |
| Charles Michel | 2030 target | European Council |
| Ambassador Mathernová | 2030 "quite realistic" | EU Delegation to Ukraine |
| Hungary | Blocking clusters | Current obstacle |

Ukraine completed its acquis screening process in September 2025, though Hungary currently blocks opening of negotiation clusters.

### 5.4 Transition mechanisms

Previous enlargements provide precedent for managing budgetary impact. The 2004 enlargement introduced **10-year phase-in periods for CAP direct payments**, starting at 25% of the final level—the same mechanism applied to Bulgaria, Romania (2007), and Croatia (2013).

The **Ukraine Facility already provides €50 billion (2024-2027)** in pre-accession support, with €34 billion already mobilized as of late 2025.

## 6. Euro adoption: legal requirement, distant reality

### 6.1 Legal framework

All post-Maastricht EU member states commit to eventual euro adoption upon accession—but **no mandatory timeline** exists. Countries control their own pace by choosing when to enter ERM II, the minimum two-year exchange rate mechanism participation required before euro adoption.

Several 2004 entrants (Poland, Hungary, Czechia) remain outside the eurozone 21 years later, demonstrating the indefinite deferral possibility.

### 6.2 Maastricht convergence assessment

Ukraine currently fails **all five Maastricht convergence criteria** by substantial margins:

**Table 4: Ukraine vs. Maastricht Criteria (2024)**

| Criterion | EU Threshold | Ukraine (2024) | Gap |
|-----------|--------------|----------------|-----|
| Budget Deficit | 3% of GDP | 17-24% of GDP | 5-8x |
| Public Debt | 60% of GDP | 90-101% of GDP | 1.5-1.7x |
| Inflation | ~3-4% | 9-12% | 3x |
| Long-term Interest Rate | ~5-6% | 15.5% | 3x |
| ERM II Participation | 2 years min | Not participating | — |

*Source: National Bank of Ukraine; IMF (2024)*

War conditions make convergence impossible—defence spending alone consumes 38-39% of GDP, driving deficits 5-8 times above threshold.

![Ukraine vs. Maastricht Convergence Criteria](/open_reports/images/ukraine_maastricht_criteria.svg)

### 6.3 Realistic timeline

Precedent suggests even stable economies require 5-11 years from EU accession to euro adoption:

- **Estonia:** EU 2004 → Euro 2011 (7 years)
- **Lithuania:** EU 2004 → Euro 2015 (11 years)
- **Croatia:** EU 2013 → Euro 2023 (9.5 years)
- **Bulgaria:** EU 2007 → Euro 2026 (19 years)

A realistic timeline for Ukrainian euro adoption extends into the **2040s at earliest**.

## 7. Institutional reform and the enlargement debate

### 7.1 The reform imperative

The Franco-German expert group's September 2023 report "Sailing on High Seas" established the principle that enlargement and EU reform must proceed together—the EU must become "enlargement-ready." The Granada Declaration of October 2023 confirmed this.

### 7.2 European Parliament position

The European Parliament voted **305-276 in November 2023** for a resolution containing 245 proposed treaty amendments, calling for a Convention under Article 48 TEU. Key proposals include:

- Shifting the Council's default voting to QMV
- Renaming the Commission the "European Executive"
- Expanding EU competences in health, industry, and environment

### 7.3 QMV expansion efforts

Germany and France launched the "Group of Friends on QMV" in May 2023 with 11 member states, seeking to extend qualified majority voting to Common Foreign and Security Policy decisions.

The Franco-German expert group proposed a "super-majority" compromise requiring 72% of member states representing 65% of population, with a "sovereignty safety net" allowing states to invoke vital interests.

### 7.4 Opposition to Treaty change

**Thirteen member states** signed a joint non-paper in May 2022 opposing "unconsidered and premature attempts to launch a process towards Treaty change":

Bulgaria, Croatia, Czech Republic, Denmark, Estonia, Finland, Latvia, Lithuania, Malta, Poland, Romania, Slovenia, and Sweden.

Hungary advocates rolling back QMV significantly. Small states fear losing vetoes that protect their interests against larger members.

## 8. Conclusions and recommendations

### 8.1 Summary of findings

Ukrainian EU membership would cost approximately **€18-19 billion annually in net transfers**—significant but representing only 0.13% of EU GDP. The EU has successfully integrated comparable challenges before, notably Poland's 2004 accession.

Technical mechanisms exist to manage the transition: cohesion caps, CAP phase-in periods, and the post-2027 MFF's explicit enlargement provisions.

The genuine obstacles are **political and institutional**:

1. The European Parliament seat cap requires either treaty amendment or painful redistribution
2. QMV expansion faces opposition from 13 member states defending unanimity
3. Agricultural interests in France, Poland, and other countries actively mobilize against competitive pressure from Ukrainian farming

### 8.2 Key insights

Ukraine would enter the EU as simultaneously its **poorest member and one of its largest**—a combination without precedent:

- Its population would immediately alter Council voting dynamics
- Its agricultural sector would transform CAP distribution
- Its security situation would make EU foreign policy decisions existentially consequential

### 8.3 Recommendations

**For EU institutions:**
- Advance MFF 2028-2034 negotiations with explicit enlargement provisions
- Develop CAP transition mechanisms that protect existing recipients while integrating Ukraine
- Explore passerelle clause activation for CFSP decisions

**For member states:**
- Engage constructively with institutional reform discussions
- Prepare domestic agricultural sectors for increased competition
- Support Ukraine Facility implementation as pre-accession preparation

**For Ukraine:**
- Continue acquis alignment across all negotiation clusters
- Develop agricultural sector transition plans compatible with CAP
- Maintain reform momentum regardless of accession timeline uncertainty

### 8.4 Forward-looking statement

The transformation runs both directions. An EU of 35+ members with Ukraine at its eastern border would be structurally different from today's Union—more geopolitically ambitious but potentially less institutionally cohesive. Whether the EU can resolve its internal reform debates before or alongside enlargement will determine whether Ukrainian membership strengthens or fragments European integration.

## 8b. Latest Developments (March 2026)

### EUR 1.5 Billion Investment Boost (5 March 2026)

On 5 March 2026, the EU unveiled a new **EUR 1.5 billion investment package** to drive Ukraine's recovery, reconstruction and modernisation. This is in addition to the existing support packages and reflects continued EU commitment to Ukraine's economic integration ahead of potential accession.

The investment targets:
- **Recovery and reconstruction** of war-damaged infrastructure
- **Modernisation** of Ukrainian institutions and economy
- **EU alignment** to support the accession process

This brings total EU financial support for Ukraine to well above EUR 100 billion since February 2022, combining macro-financial assistance, military aid, humanitarian support, and investment packages.

Source: DG NEAR news, 5 March 2026.
