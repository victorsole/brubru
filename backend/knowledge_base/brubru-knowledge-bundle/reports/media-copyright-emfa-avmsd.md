# Media, Copyright and Content Regulation in the EU: EMFA, AVMSD, the Copyright Directive, and DSA Media Enforcement

**A Comprehensive Analysis of the EU's Regulatory Framework for Media Freedom, Audiovisual Services, Digital Copyright, and Platform Content Moderation**

**Author:** Victor Sole Ferioli, Founder & Director, Beresol

**Date:** February 2026

**Location:** Brussels, Belgium

---

## Executive Summary

The European Union has constructed an interconnected regulatory architecture governing media services, copyright protection, and digital content moderation that represents the most comprehensive framework of its kind globally. Four legislative instruments -- the European Media Freedom Act (EMFA), the Audiovisual Media Services Directive (AVMSD), the Copyright in the Digital Single Market Directive, and the media-related enforcement provisions of the Digital Services Act (DSA) -- collectively define the rights and obligations of media companies, online platforms, news publishers, content creators, and Member States.

**Key Findings:**

- **EMFA (Regulation 2024/1083):** The first-ever EU-wide legal framework for media freedom and pluralism, fully applicable from 8 August 2025. Establishes the European Board for Media Services, bans spyware against journalists, requires media ownership transparency, and creates a privileged regime for media content moderation on very large online platforms.
- **AVMSD (Directive 2018/1808):** Extended audiovisual regulation to video-sharing platforms, imposed a 30% European works quota on VOD services, and strengthened protection of minors. The Commission launched a call for evidence in November 2025 and must complete a formal evaluation by 19 December 2026 under Article 33.
- **Copyright Directive (Directive 2019/790):** Article 15 created a new neighbouring right for press publishers; Article 17 imposed platform liability for user-uploaded content, sparking the "upload filters" controversy. Transposition has been uneven, with several Member States missing the June 2021 deadline. The CJEU upheld Article 17's validity in Case C-401/19 (Poland v Parliament) in April 2022.
- **DSA Media Enforcement:** Very large online platforms must assess systemic risks to media freedom and pluralism. EMFA Article 17 creates a special notification and dialogue procedure before platforms can restrict media service provider content, generating tensions with DSA content moderation obligations.
- **Cross-instrument interaction:** The four instruments create overlapping regulatory obligations that require careful navigation, particularly at the intersection of EMFA media privileges and DSA content moderation duties.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 Context

The regulation of media, copyright, and online content in Europe has undergone a fundamental transformation during the 2018-2025 period. Driven by the proliferation of digital platforms, the decline of traditional media business models, growing concerns about disinformation and foreign information manipulation, and the political weaponisation of media ownership, the EU has constructed a multi-layered regulatory architecture that addresses media freedom, audiovisual services, intellectual property, and platform governance as interconnected policy domains.

This regulatory evolution must be understood against the backdrop of several converging pressures: the erosion of press freedom in certain Member States (notably Hungary and Poland before 2023), the dominance of US-based technology platforms over European content distribution, the economic crisis facing news publishers in the digital economy, and the weaponisation of disinformation during the COVID-19 pandemic and Russia's war of aggression against Ukraine.

The result is four major legislative instruments that collectively define the EU's approach to media and content regulation: the EMFA as a constitutional charter for media freedom, the AVMSD as the sector-specific framework for audiovisual services, the Copyright Directive as the intellectual property backbone for the creative economy, and the DSA as the horizontal platform governance regime with specific media implications.

### 1.2 Scope

This report analyses each of these four instruments in detail, examines their interactions and potential conflicts, assesses the practical implications for different categories of stakeholders (media companies, broadcasters, streaming platforms, news publishers, content creators, and technology platforms), and identifies the key political dynamics, transposition challenges, and enforcement questions that will shape the regulatory landscape through 2027 and beyond.

## 3. The European Media Freedom Act (EMFA)

### 3.1 Legislative History and Political Context

The EMFA was proposed by the European Commission on 16 September 2022 as part of the von der Leyen Commission's "New Push for European Democracy" priority. The proposal responded to mounting evidence of media freedom deterioration within the EU itself, including the Hungarian government's systematic capture of media outlets, the murder of journalists Daphne Caruana Galizia (Malta, 2017) and Jan Kuciak (Slovakia, 2018), and revelations about the use of Pegasus spyware against journalists in multiple Member States.

The legislative process was marked by intense debate on several fronts. The European Parliament, under the lead of rapporteur Sabine Verheyen (EPP, Germany), pushed for stronger protections for journalists and more robust anti-concentration measures. The Council negotiations, conducted under the Swedish and Spanish presidencies in 2023, were particularly contentious regarding the scope of the spyware ban and the powers of the new European Board for Media Services. France resisted provisions it perceived as threatening national media regulation sovereignty, while smaller Member States generally supported stronger EU-level coordination.

A provisional agreement was reached on 15 December 2023. The European Parliament adopted the regulation on 13 March 2024, and the Council gave its final approval on 26 March 2024. The regulation was published in the Official Journal on 17 April 2024.

### 3.2 Core Provisions

#### 3.2.1 Rights of Recipients (Article 3)

Article 3 establishes, for the first time, an individual right of recipients of media services to access a plurality of editorially independent media content. This provision, which applied from 11 November 2024 -- ahead of the main application date -- is conceptually significant because it frames media plurality not merely as a public policy objective but as an individual entitlement.

#### 3.2.2 Protection of Journalistic Sources and Anti-Spyware (Article 4)

Article 4 is perhaps the most politically significant provision. It prohibits Member States from deploying spyware against journalists, their family members, and their employees. The narrow exceptions -- limited to national security threats and investigations of a closed list of serious crimes (terrorism, child sexual abuse, murder) -- represent a major constraint on state surveillance powers. Even where exceptions apply, judicial authorisation is required, and the measure must be proportionate and subject to regular review.

This provision was directly triggered by the Pegasus spyware scandal revealed by the European Parliament's PEGA Committee of Inquiry (2022-2023), which documented the use of surveillance technology against journalists and opposition politicians in Hungary, Poland, Spain, and Greece.

#### 3.2.3 Public Service Media Independence (Article 5)

Article 5 requires Member States to ensure that public service media providers are editorially and functionally independent, receive stable, adequate, and sustainable funding determined through transparent and objective procedures, and have management boards appointed through open, transparent, and non-discriminatory processes. The head of management and members of the governing board may only be dismissed on grounds established in advance through clear, objective criteria.

#### 3.2.4 Media Ownership Transparency (Articles 6-7)

Media service providers must make publicly available specified information about their ownership structure, including direct and indirect owners, beneficial owners holding more than 5% of shares or voting rights, and any public funds or state advertising revenue received. National regulatory authorities must maintain a national media ownership database.

#### 3.2.5 Media Market Concentrations (Articles 21-22)

The EMFA introduces a common EU framework for assessing media market concentrations from the perspective of their impact on media pluralism and editorial independence. While Member States retain competence over merger control, they must take into account the assessment criteria set out in the regulation. The European Board for Media Services may issue opinions on concentrations likely to affect media pluralism.

#### 3.2.6 State Advertising Transparency (Articles 24-25)

Public authorities at all levels (EU, national, regional, and local) must allocate state advertising to media service providers in a transparent, objective, proportionate, and non-discriminatory manner. Annual information on state advertising expenditure must be made public, broken down by media service provider. This provision targets the practice, documented particularly in Hungary and Poland, of channelling public advertising revenue to politically aligned media outlets.

#### 3.2.7 Content Moderation on VLOPs (Article 17)

Article 17 of the EMFA creates a special regime for the treatment of media content by very large online platforms (as designated under the DSA). Before suspending or restricting the provision of their service in relation to content provided by a media service provider that has made a declaration of editorial independence, VLOPs must take "all possible measures" to notify the media service provider prior to the suspension or restriction taking effect and provide a statement of reasons. Where the media service provider considers the restriction unjustified, both parties must engage in a meaningful dialogue, the outcome of which may be notified to the European Board for Media Services. This provision is analysed in detail in Section 6 below.

### 3.3 The European Board for Media Services

The EMFA replaces the European Regulators Group for Audiovisual Media Services (ERGA), which operated as an informal advisory body under the AVMSD, with the European Board for Media Services (the Board). The Board, officially established on 10 February 2025, is an independent EU advisory body composed of representatives of national media regulatory authorities or bodies.

The Board's mandate is substantially broader than ERGA's. Key functions include: advising the Commission on the consistent application of the EMFA and AVMSD; promoting best practices and cooperation among national regulatory authorities; issuing opinions on media market concentrations, state advertising allocation, and VLOP content moderation disputes; monitoring compliance with media ownership transparency requirements; and supporting cross-border enforcement.

## 4. The Audiovisual Media Services Directive (AVMSD)

### 4.1 From Television Without Frontiers to the AVMSD

The regulatory framework for audiovisual media services in Europe dates back to the "Television Without Frontiers" Directive of 1989 (89/552/EEC), which established the country-of-origin principle for cross-border broadcasting. The first AVMSD (Directive 2007/65/EC, codified as Directive 2010/13/EU) extended the framework beyond traditional television to on-demand audiovisual media services.

The 2018 revision (Directive 2018/1808) represented the most significant update to date, driven by the fundamental transformation of audiovisual media consumption through video-sharing platforms, streaming services, and social media.

### 4.2 Key Provisions of the 2018 Revision

#### 4.2.1 Extended Scope to Video-Sharing Platforms (VSPs)

The most consequential change was the extension of AVMSD obligations to video-sharing platforms such as YouTube, TikTok, and Dailymotion. While these platforms are not subject to the full editorial responsibility obligations of linear broadcasters or on-demand services, they must take appropriate measures to protect minors from harmful content, protect the general public from content inciting violence, hatred, or terrorism, and comply with advertising and product placement rules. Member States must designate the national regulatory authority competent for VSPs established on their territory.

#### 4.2.2 European Works Quota for VOD Services (Article 13)

On-demand audiovisual media services must ensure that European works constitute at least 30% of their catalogues and that such works are given appropriate prominence. Member States may require that services targeting audiences in their territory contribute financially to the production of European works, including through direct investment or contributions to national film funds.

This provision has had significant commercial implications for global streaming platforms such as Netflix, Amazon Prime Video, and Disney+, which have been required to invest in European content production and ensure visibility for European works in their recommendation algorithms and user interfaces.

#### 4.2.3 Country of Origin Principle with Derogation Mechanism

The country-of-origin principle remains the cornerstone of the AVMSD, meaning that a media service provider is subject to the jurisdiction of the Member State in which it is established. However, the 2018 revision introduced a strengthened derogation mechanism (Article 3(2)-(5)) allowing a receiving Member State to take measures against services from another Member State that manifestly, seriously, and gravely infringe specified provisions on the protection of minors, incitement to violence or hatred, or public security.

#### 4.2.4 Advertising Rules

The 2018 revision relaxed certain quantitative advertising restrictions while introducing new qualitative safeguards. The 12-minute-per-hour advertising limit was replaced by a 20% cap over the period between 06:00 and 18:00 and a 20% cap between 18:00 and midnight, giving broadcasters greater flexibility. Product placement rules were also liberalised, with the general prohibition replaced by a more permissive regime, though sponsorship and product placement in children's programming remain restricted.

#### 4.2.5 Protection of Minors

Strengthened provisions on the protection of minors require Member States to ensure that audiovisual media services that might seriously impair the physical, mental, or moral development of minors are only available in a manner that ensures minors do not normally hear or see them. This includes requirements for parental control tools, age verification systems, and content rating.

#### 4.2.6 ERGA and the Transition to the EMFA Board

The 2018 AVMSD formalised the role of the European Regulators Group for Audiovisual Media Services (ERGA) as an advisory body to the Commission. However, as noted above, the EMFA has now replaced ERGA with the European Board for Media Services, which has a broader mandate and enhanced powers. The transition was completed in February 2025.

### 4.3 Evaluation and Potential Revision (2025-2027)

Article 33 of the AVMSD requires the Commission to submit to the European Parliament, the Council, and the European Economic and Social Committee a report on the application of the Directive by 19 December 2022 (subsequently postponed), and, where appropriate, make proposals for its revision. The Commission also committed to a further evaluation by 19 December 2026.

In November 2025, the Commission launched a call for evidence to inform the 2026 evaluation, with the consultation period closing on 21 December 2025. The evaluation will assess the effectiveness, relevance, efficiency, coherence, and EU added value of the AVMSD, with particular attention to:

- **Scope:** Whether the current categorisation of services (linear, on-demand, VSP) remains fit for purpose given convergence trends and the rise of social media content creators and influencers.
- **Prominence:** Whether European works of general interest receive sufficient visibility on digital platforms.
- **Protection of minors:** Whether existing safeguards are adequate in the context of children's access to audiovisual content through social media and influencer content.
- **Advertising:** Whether the regulatory framework for advertising, including influencer marketing and native advertising, requires updating.
- **Country of origin:** Whether the derogation mechanism provides adequate safeguards for receiving Member States.

The Council adopted conclusions on 13 May 2025 setting out its priorities for the audiovisual media sector, feeding into the Commission's evaluation process as part of the European Democracy Shield initiative.

Three policy options are under consideration: (1) leaving the AVMSD unchanged; (2) targeted adjustments, particularly regarding influencers, prominence, advertising, and protection of minors; or (3) comprehensive revision to address fundamental structural changes in the audiovisual media landscape.

## 5. The Copyright in the Digital Single Market Directive

### 5.1 Legislative History

The Copyright Directive was proposed by the Commission on 14 September 2016 as part of the Digital Single Market strategy. It became one of the most contentious pieces of EU legislation in recent memory, generating unprecedented public engagement, street protests, and a lobbying battle that pitted technology companies against rights holders and news publishers.

The Directive was adopted on 17 April 2019 and published in the Official Journal on 17 May 2019. Member States were given until 7 June 2021 to transpose it into national law.

### 5.2 Article 15: Press Publishers' Neighbouring Right

#### 5.2.1 Scope and Mechanism

Article 15 creates a new neighbouring right for press publishers, granting them the exclusive right to authorise or prohibit the online reproduction and making available to the public of their press publications by information society service providers. This right lasts for two years from the date of publication (starting from 1 January of the year following publication).

The right specifically targets news aggregators and search engines that display snippets, headlines, and thumbnail images from press publications, requiring them to obtain licences or otherwise compensate publishers for such uses. Individual words or "very short extracts" are excluded from the right's scope, though the Directive does not define what constitutes a "very short extract."

#### 5.2.2 Relationship with Journalists

Article 15(5) requires that authors of works incorporated in press publications receive an "appropriate share" of the revenues that press publishers obtain from the neighbouring right. The mechanism for determining this share is left to national transposition, leading to significant variation across Member States.

#### 5.2.3 Industry Impact

The press publishers' right has catalysed licensing negotiations between news publishers and technology platforms globally. Google, in particular, has entered into licensing agreements with publishers in several Member States, including France (where ARCEP issued a landmark decision in 2021) and Germany. However, smaller publishers have generally struggled to leverage the right, as technology platforms have more bargaining power and can simply cease displaying snippets rather than enter into licensing agreements.

### 5.3 Article 17: Platform Liability for User-Uploaded Content

#### 5.3.1 The "Upload Filters" Controversy

Article 17 is the single most contested provision in EU copyright law. It establishes that online content-sharing service providers (OCSSPs) -- platforms whose main purpose is to store and give public access to a large amount of copyright-protected works uploaded by users -- perform an act of communication to the public and must therefore obtain authorisation from rights holders.

Where no authorisation has been obtained, the platform is liable for unauthorised content unless it demonstrates that it made "best efforts" to obtain authorisation, made "best efforts" in accordance with high industry standards of professional diligence to ensure the unavailability of specific works for which rights holders have provided relevant and necessary information, and acted expeditiously upon receiving a sufficiently substantiated notice to disable access to or remove the notified works, and made best efforts to prevent their future upload.

Critics argue that compliance with these requirements effectively necessitates the deployment of automated content recognition technologies -- commonly referred to as "upload filters" -- which risk over-blocking legitimate content, restricting freedom of expression, and creating barriers to entry for smaller platforms.

#### 5.3.2 Safeguards for Users and Fundamental Rights

Article 17(7)-(9) includes safeguards designed to balance copyright enforcement with freedom of expression. Member States must ensure that users can rely on exceptions and limitations for quotation, criticism, review, caricature, parody, and pastiche. Platforms may not impose a general monitoring obligation. Content-sharing service providers must put in place effective and expeditious complaint and redress mechanisms, and users must have access to out-of-court dispute resolution and judicial remedies.

#### 5.3.3 The CJEU Judgment in Case C-401/19 (Poland v Parliament)

Poland challenged the validity of Article 17(4)(b) and (c) before the Court of Justice, arguing that the provision violated the right to freedom of expression and information guaranteed by Article 11 of the Charter of Fundamental Rights. The Grand Chamber delivered its judgment on 26 April 2022, dismissing Poland's action.

The Court acknowledged that Article 17 constitutes a limitation on the right to freedom of expression and information, but held that the limitation is justified and proportionate, subject to the condition that automated filtering systems must be able to adequately distinguish between lawful and unlawful content. The Court emphasised that:

- Member States, when transposing Article 17, must ensure that the national measures do not lead to any obligation to carry out prior general monitoring.
- Automated tools must not filter out lawful content, particularly content benefiting from exceptions and limitations.
- If filtering cannot adequately distinguish between lawful and unlawful content for specific types of works, the use of filtering tools must not be imposed.

This judgment, while upholding Article 17's validity, effectively established that over-blocking constitutes a violation of fundamental rights, creating a tension between rights holders' expectations of proactive enforcement and the limits imposed by the Charter.

#### 5.3.4 Commission Guidance

The Commission published guidance on Article 17 on 4 June 2021, setting out its interpretation of how the provision should be transposed and applied. Key elements include the recognition that purely automated blocking without human review raises fundamental rights concerns, the recommendation that platforms should allow ex ante exceptions for content identified as potentially covered by limitations (such as quotation, parody, or review), and the emphasis on transparency and accountability in automated systems.

### 5.4 Other Key Provisions

#### 5.4.1 Text and Data Mining Exceptions (Articles 3-4)

Article 3 provides a mandatory exception for text and data mining (TDM) for the purposes of scientific research, applicable to research organisations and cultural heritage institutions with lawful access to the works. Article 4 provides a broader TDM exception for any person with lawful access, subject to a rights reservation by rights holders (the "opt-out" mechanism).

These provisions have acquired enormous significance in the context of generative AI, as the training of large language models relies heavily on text and data mining of copyrighted content. Whether and how AI companies have complied with the opt-out mechanism under Article 4, and whether the scientific research exception under Article 3 can be invoked for commercial AI research, are among the most actively debated questions in EU copyright policy.

#### 5.4.2 Out-of-Commerce Works (Articles 8-11)

Articles 8-11 establish a framework for the use of out-of-commerce works held in the collections of cultural heritage institutions. Representative collective management organisations may grant non-exclusive licences for the digital use of out-of-commerce works, subject to adequate publicity measures and an opt-out mechanism for rights holders.

#### 5.4.3 Fair Remuneration for Authors and Performers (Articles 18-23)

The Directive introduces principles of appropriate and proportionate remuneration for authors and performers (Article 18), transparency obligations requiring exploitation information (Article 19), a contract adjustment mechanism in cases of unexpectedly high revenues (Article 20), and an alternative dispute resolution mechanism (Article 21). Article 22 establishes a right of revocation allowing authors and performers to terminate licences or transfers if works are not exploited.

### 5.5 Transposition Challenges

The transposition of the Copyright Directive has been markedly uneven across Member States. Many Member States failed to meet the 7 June 2021 deadline. As of early 2026, while all Member States have formally transposed the Directive, the quality and faithfulness of national implementations vary considerably.

Key areas of divergence include:

- **Definition of "very short extracts"** under Article 15, where national laws range from permissive (allowing headlines and brief snippets) to restrictive.
- **Article 17 implementation mechanisms**, where some Member States have adopted more prescriptive approaches to automated filtering while others have emphasised human review and complaint mechanisms.
- **Remuneration models** for press publishers' authors under Article 15(5), with wide variation in the share allocated to journalists.
- **Text and data mining opt-out mechanisms** under Article 4, where the technical and legal requirements for a valid opt-out vary.

The Commission launched infringement proceedings against several Member States for late or incorrect transposition, and the process of harmonisation through preliminary references to the CJEU is expected to continue for several years.

## 6. DSA Enforcement for Media Content

### 6.1 DSA Content Moderation Framework

The Digital Services Act (Regulation 2022/2065) establishes a comprehensive framework for platform governance, including transparency obligations, content moderation procedures, and systemic risk management. While the DSA is a horizontal regulation applying to all categories of digital services, several of its provisions have particular significance for media content.

#### 6.1.1 Content Moderation and Notice-and-Action (Articles 16-17)

All hosting services, including platforms that host media content, must implement notice-and-action mechanisms allowing any person to notify the platform of content they consider illegal. Platforms must process notices diligently, in a timely, non-discriminatory, and non-arbitrary manner, and inform the notifier of their decision and available remedies.

For media content, this creates a tension: content moderation decisions by platforms can have direct implications for press freedom, editorial independence, and the right to information. The removal or restriction of legitimate journalistic content -- whether through over-zealous automated moderation or politically motivated flagging -- has been documented repeatedly across major platforms.

#### 6.1.2 Trusted Flaggers (Article 22)

The DSA establishes a "trusted flagger" mechanism, whereby entities with demonstrated expertise in detecting illegal content are granted priority processing of their notices. For media content, this raises questions about which bodies qualify as trusted flaggers in relation to media law violations, and how the system interacts with press regulation frameworks at national level.

#### 6.1.3 Out-of-Court Dispute Settlement (Article 21)

Recipients of services, including media service providers, have the right to access out-of-court dispute settlement bodies certified by Digital Services Coordinators. These bodies can issue non-binding decisions on disputes concerning content moderation decisions. For media companies, this provides an additional avenue beyond the EMFA dialogue mechanism (see Section 6.2 below).

#### 6.1.4 Researcher Access to Data (Article 40)

VLOPs and VLOSEs must provide vetted researchers with access to data necessary to undertake research contributing to the detection, identification, and understanding of systemic risks. For media researchers, this is a significant provision enabling the study of algorithmic amplification, filter bubbles, and the impact of platform design on media pluralism.

### 6.2 Systemic Risk Assessments and Media Freedom (Article 34)

VLOPs are required to conduct annual systemic risk assessments covering, inter alia, actual or foreseeable negative effects on the exercise of fundamental rights, including the freedom and pluralism of the media (Article 11 of the Charter). This means that VLOPs must specifically analyse how their services, algorithms, and content moderation practices affect media freedom, editorial independence, and the availability of pluralistic media content.

Where systemic risks are identified, VLOPs must adopt reasonable, proportionate, and effective mitigation measures (Article 35). In the media context, these might include adjusting recommendation algorithms to promote authoritative news sources, implementing media literacy features, or improving the visibility and accessibility of journalistic content.

### 6.3 EMFA Article 17: The Media Privilege on VLOPs

#### 6.3.1 The Mechanism

EMFA Article 17 introduces what has been termed a "media privilege" or "media exemption" in the context of VLOP content moderation. Under this provision, VLOPs must:

1. Take "all possible measures" to communicate to a media service provider their intention to restrict or suspend the provision of their service in relation to that provider's content, before the restriction takes effect.
2. Provide a statement of reasons for the intended action.
3. Give the media service provider a reasonable opportunity to respond.
4. If the media service provider considers the action unjustified, engage in a "meaningful dialogue" aimed at finding an amicable solution.

This mechanism applies only to media service providers that have submitted a self-declaration to the relevant national regulatory authority or the European Board for Media Services, attesting that they are editorially independent and subject to regulatory requirements for the exercise of editorial responsibility.

#### 6.3.2 Scope and Limitations

The EMFA media privilege is subject to important limitations. It does not apply where content moderation action is taken because the media content contributes to a systemic risk within the meaning of Article 34 of the DSA. In other words, if a platform determines that media content poses a systemic risk (for example, through the dissemination of disinformation that threatens public health or democratic processes), the platform may restrict the content without engaging in the EMFA dialogue procedure.

Furthermore, the provision applies only to content that is restricted or suspended for violating the platform's terms and conditions -- it does not affect the platform's obligations to address illegal content under the DSA or national law.

#### 6.3.3 Controversy and Criticism

The EMFA media privilege has attracted significant criticism from multiple directions:

- **Civil society and digital rights organisations** argue that it creates a two-tier content moderation system that privileges established media outlets over individual content creators, citizen journalists, and smaller media organisations.
- **Technology platforms** contend that the notification and dialogue requirements are operationally burdensome, particularly given the volume and velocity of content moderation decisions, and create conflicts with their DSA obligations to act expeditiously against harmful content.
- **Disinformation researchers** warn that the self-declaration mechanism is susceptible to abuse by state-controlled media outlets and propaganda operations that could use the EMFA media privilege to resist legitimate content moderation.
- **Media organisations** themselves are divided, with some welcoming the protection against arbitrary platform actions and others concerned that the mechanism is too weak to provide meaningful safeguards.

## 7. Cross-Instrument Interactions and Conflicts

### 7.1 EMFA and DSA: Complementarity and Tension

The interaction between the EMFA and the DSA is the most significant area of regulatory overlap. Both instruments impose obligations on VLOPs regarding content moderation, but they pull in different directions: the DSA requires platforms to act against illegal and harmful content, while the EMFA requires them to exercise restraint when dealing with media service providers.

The key reconciliation mechanism is the "systemic risk" carve-out in EMFA Article 17, which allows platforms to bypass the media dialogue procedure where content contributes to DSA-defined systemic risks. However, the practical application of this carve-out will depend on how platforms, national regulators, and the European Board for Media Services interpret "systemic risk" in the media context.

### 7.2 EMFA and AVMSD: Institutional Continuity

The EMFA explicitly amends Directive 2010/13/EU (the AVMSD) by replacing ERGA with the European Board for Media Services. The Board's mandate covers both the EMFA and the AVMSD, creating institutional continuity. However, the coexistence of a regulation (EMFA, directly applicable) and a directive (AVMSD, requiring national transposition) in the same policy field creates potential for divergent interpretation and application.

### 7.3 Copyright Directive and DSA: Platform Liability Regimes

The relationship between Article 17 of the Copyright Directive and the DSA's hosting liability framework (Articles 6 and 16) is complex. The Copyright Directive establishes a lex specialis for copyright-protected content on OCSSPs, while the DSA provides the general framework for content moderation. Recital 10 of the DSA explicitly provides that it does not affect the Copyright Directive.

In practice, this means that OCSSPs must simultaneously comply with the Copyright Directive's "best efforts" standard for copyright infringement and the DSA's due diligence obligations for all other categories of illegal content, using potentially different technical systems, processes, and legal standards.

### 7.4 Copyright Directive and EMFA: News Content as a Nexus

News content sits at the intersection of the Copyright Directive (Article 15 press publishers' right) and the EMFA (media freedom and platform content moderation). When a VLOP restricts a media service provider's content on copyright grounds, the interaction between Article 15 of the Copyright Directive, Article 17 of the DSA (hosting liability), and Article 17 of the EMFA (media privilege) creates a three-layer regulatory puzzle that will require careful case-by-case navigation.

## 8. Practical Implications by Sector

### 8.1 Public Service Broadcasters

Public service broadcasters (PSBs) such as the BBC, ARD/ZDF, France Televisions, and RAI are among the primary beneficiaries of the EMFA. The independence guarantees in Article 5, the funding stability requirements, and the protection against political interference in management appointments directly address documented threats to PSB independence in several Member States.

Under the AVMSD, PSBs continue to hold privileged status through the prominence provisions and the European works obligations. The potential AVMSD revision may further strengthen prominence requirements, ensuring that PSB content is easily discoverable on connected TV interfaces and streaming platforms.

### 8.2 Commercial Broadcasters and VOD Services

Commercial broadcasters face a dual regulatory framework: the AVMSD for audiovisual content regulation and the EMFA for media freedom protections. The AVMSD's advertising rules, European works quota, and protection of minors provisions impose direct operational requirements.

For VOD services, the 30% European works quota and the associated financial contribution obligations represent significant compliance costs. Netflix, Amazon Prime Video, Disney+, and other global streaming platforms have responded by increasing investment in European original content, though concerns persist about whether the prominence requirements are being effectively implemented and enforced.

### 8.3 News Publishers

News publishers are the primary beneficiaries of Article 15 of the Copyright Directive, which provides a new revenue stream through licensing agreements with platforms and aggregators. However, the practical value of the neighbouring right depends heavily on bargaining power: large publishers and publisher alliances have been more successful in securing licensing deals than smaller independent outlets.

Under the EMFA, news publishers benefit from the media content moderation protections on VLOPs (Article 17), media ownership transparency provisions, and state advertising transparency requirements. The combination of copyright remuneration (via the Copyright Directive) and distribution protection (via the EMFA) represents a significant strengthening of the regulatory framework for the news publishing sector.

### 8.4 Video-Sharing Platforms and Social Media

Video-sharing platforms and social media companies face the most complex regulatory landscape, as they are subject to obligations under all four instruments simultaneously:

- **AVMSD:** Measures to protect minors and the public from harmful content, advertising compliance.
- **Copyright Directive:** Article 17 liability for user-uploaded copyright-protected content (for platforms qualifying as OCSSPs).
- **DSA:** Full due diligence obligations, systemic risk assessments, transparency requirements, and content moderation procedural safeguards.
- **EMFA:** Media privilege notification and dialogue requirements for media service provider content.

The compliance challenge is compounded by the fact that these obligations may conflict in specific cases -- for example, when a copyright takedown targets content posted by a media service provider that claims editorial independence.

### 8.5 Content Creators and Influencers

Individual content creators and influencers occupy a regulatory grey zone. Under the current AVMSD, they are generally not classified as audiovisual media service providers unless they meet specific editorial and organisational thresholds. However, the forthcoming AVMSD evaluation is expected to address influencer content directly, potentially bringing a broader range of content creators within the regulatory framework.

Under the Copyright Directive, content creators benefit from the user safeguards in Article 17(7)-(9), including the right to rely on exceptions for quotation, parody, and pastiche. However, they also bear the burden of false-positive automated filtering, where legitimate content may be blocked or restricted by platform content recognition systems.

## 9. Political Dynamics and Institutional Positions

### 9.1 The European Parliament

The European Parliament has been the most ambitious institution in pushing for stronger media freedom protections. During the EMFA negotiations, Parliament secured stronger anti-spyware provisions, broader transparency requirements, and more robust protections for editorial independence than the Commission's original proposal. On copyright, Parliament was deeply divided, with the final vote on the Copyright Directive in March 2019 passing by a relatively narrow margin (348 to 274).

In the current legislative term (2024-2029), the Parliament's Culture and Education Committee (CULT) is the lead committee for media and copyright issues, with the Internal Market Committee (IMCO) and the Civil Liberties Committee (LIBE) as associated committees. The EPP, S&D, and Renew groups have generally supported the media regulatory framework, while elements of the Greens/EFA and The Left have pushed for stronger platform regulation and user rights.

### 9.2 The Council

Council positions on media regulation reflect fundamental tensions between Member States' media traditions. France, with its strong tradition of cultural exception (exception culturelle), has been a driving force behind the European works quota, the press publishers' right, and financial contribution obligations for streaming platforms. Germany, as the largest media market, has sought to balance copyright enforcement with innovation and fundamental rights. Nordic Member States have generally favoured lighter-touch regulation, while Central and Eastern European Member States' positions have varied depending on domestic media freedom conditions.

The unanimity requirement for cultural provisions (Article 167 TFEU) does not apply to internal market harmonisation measures (Article 114), which is the legal basis for the EMFA and the DSA. This has facilitated adoption but has also generated concerns about the legitimacy of EU action in a domain traditionally considered a national competence.

### 9.3 Industry and Civil Society

The media and technology industries have adopted contrasting positions. European media companies, particularly through lobby groups such as the European Publishers Council (EPC), the European Broadcasting Union (EBU), and News Media Europe, have strongly supported the EMFA and Article 15 of the Copyright Directive while expressing reservations about certain DSA obligations.

Technology platforms, represented by groups such as CCIA (Computer & Communications Industry Association) and Digital Europe, have generally opposed the EMFA media privilege, the press publishers' right, and Article 17 of the Copyright Directive, arguing that these provisions create legal uncertainty, stifle innovation, and fragment the digital single market.

Civil society has been divided, with press freedom organisations (European Federation of Journalists, Reporters Without Borders) supporting the EMFA while digital rights organisations (EDRi, EFF, Wikimedia) have raised concerns about the Copyright Directive's impact on freedom of expression and the EMFA media privilege's potential for abuse.

## 10. Enforcement and Compliance Challenges

### 10.1 EMFA Enforcement

As a regulation, the EMFA is directly applicable in all Member States. However, effective enforcement depends on the capacity, independence, and willingness of national regulatory authorities, which vary significantly across the EU. The European Board for Media Services has a coordination and advisory role but lacks direct enforcement powers.

Key enforcement challenges include: verifying the accuracy of media ownership declarations; monitoring compliance with state advertising transparency requirements at regional and local levels; assessing the substance and good faith of VLOP-media dialogue processes under Article 17; and preventing abuse of the media self-declaration mechanism by non-independent or propagandistic outlets.

### 10.2 AVMSD Transposition Enforcement

The AVMSD, as a directive, requires national transposition, and the Commission has pursued infringement proceedings against several Member States for late or incomplete transposition of the 2018 revision. Enforcement of the European works quota and financial contribution obligations has been particularly challenging, as monitoring catalogue composition and investment flows requires sophisticated data collection and analysis.

### 10.3 Copyright Directive Transposition Enforcement

The uneven transposition of the Copyright Directive has created significant enforcement challenges. The Commission has launched infringement proceedings against Member States that failed to transpose the Directive by the June 2021 deadline. However, even where transposition has occurred, divergent national implementations of Articles 15 and 17 create fragmentation that undermines the Digital Single Market objective.

The CJEU's role in harmonising interpretation through preliminary references is critical. The Poland v Parliament judgment (C-401/19) provided important guidance on Article 17, but many questions remain unanswered, including the precise scope of the "best efforts" obligation, the technical requirements for content recognition tools, and the threshold for "very short extracts" under Article 15.

### 10.4 DSA Enforcement for Media Obligations

The Commission has direct enforcement powers over VLOPs and VLOSEs under the DSA, including the ability to impose fines of up to 6% of annual worldwide turnover. The Commission has already opened proceedings against several major platforms, and the systemic risk assessment obligations -- including those relating to media freedom and pluralism -- are a central focus of enforcement activity.

The interaction between DSA enforcement (Commission-led for VLOPs) and EMFA enforcement (national regulatory authorities) creates a dual enforcement dynamic that requires careful coordination to avoid conflicting decisions.

## 11. Outlook and Forthcoming Developments

### 11.1 EMFA Implementation (2025-2027)

The full application of the EMFA from 8 August 2025 marks the beginning of a critical implementation phase. The European Board for Media Services must develop its institutional capacity, adopt working procedures, and begin exercising its advisory and coordination functions. National regulatory authorities must adapt to their expanded mandates. VLOPs must implement the Article 17 media dialogue mechanism. The first years of application will reveal whether the EMFA's ambitious framework translates into meaningful improvements in media freedom and pluralism.

### 11.2 AVMSD Evaluation and Potential Revision (2026-2028)

The Commission's AVMSD evaluation, due by 19 December 2026, will determine whether a legislative revision is proposed. Based on the call for evidence launched in November 2025, key areas for potential revision include: extension of regulatory scope to influencers and social media content creators; strengthened prominence requirements for services of general interest; updated advertising rules for digital-age formats; enhanced protection of minors in the online environment; and possible convergence with the EMFA framework.

### 11.3 Copyright Directive: AI and the Future of Article 4

The text and data mining exceptions in Articles 3 and 4 of the Copyright Directive are at the centre of a rapidly evolving debate about the use of copyrighted content for AI training. The Commission, in the context of the AI Act implementation and the broader AI regulatory framework, will need to clarify the scope and conditions of the TDM exceptions in relation to generative AI. This may require legislative action, Commission guidance, or interpretation through CJEU case law.

### 11.4 DSA Enforcement Maturation

As DSA enforcement matures, the Commission's approach to media-related systemic risks will become clearer. The first annual transparency reports from VLOPs, the results of systemic risk assessments, and any enforcement actions will provide empirical evidence of how the DSA framework functions in practice for media content.

---

## 12. Conclusions

The EU's regulatory framework for media, copyright, and content regulation represents a comprehensive but complex architecture that reflects the competing values and interests at stake: media freedom, cultural diversity, intellectual property protection, platform innovation, fundamental rights, and democratic resilience.

The EMFA is a landmark achievement -- the first EU-wide law dedicated to media freedom -- but its effectiveness will depend on the institutional capacity and political independence of national regulatory authorities and the European Board for Media Services. The AVMSD faces a crucial evaluation that may lead to its most significant update since 2018, driven by the convergence of audiovisual and social media and the rise of the creator economy. The Copyright Directive's most controversial provisions -- Articles 15 and 17 -- are still being absorbed into national legal systems, with ongoing litigation and unresolved questions about the balance between copyright enforcement and fundamental rights. The DSA's media-related obligations are entering their enforcement phase, with the interaction between DSA content moderation duties and EMFA media privileges remaining the most delicate area of regulatory overlap.

For media companies, broadcasters, streaming platforms, news publishers, and content creators operating in the EU, the regulatory landscape demands sophisticated compliance strategies that address multiple overlapping instruments. For policymakers, the challenge is to ensure that these instruments work together coherently to achieve their shared objectives of a free, pluralistic, innovative, and sustainable media ecosystem in Europe.
