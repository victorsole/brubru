# Digital Markets and Competition: DMA, DSA Enforcement, Foreign Subsidies Regulation, and Data Act

**A Comprehensive Analysis of the EU's New Digital and Competition Regulatory Architecture**

**Author:** Victor Sole Ferioli, Founder & Director, Beresol

**Date:** February 2026

**Location:** Brussels, Belgium

---

## Executive Summary

Between 2022 and 2023, the European Union adopted four landmark regulations that collectively reshape the rules for digital markets, online platforms, foreign-subsidised competitors, and data access across the Single Market. The Digital Markets Act (DMA), the Digital Services Act (DSA), the Foreign Subsidies Regulation (FSR), and the Data Act constitute a regulatory architecture of unprecedented breadth, touching virtually every major technology company operating in Europe and extending the EU's enforcement reach well beyond its borders.

**Key Findings:**

- **DMA enforcement has drawn first blood:** the Commission fined Apple EUR 500 million and Meta EUR 200 million in April 2025 for non-compliance, with Alphabet facing preliminary findings of self-preferencing in Google Search and anti-steering in Google Play. Seven gatekeepers now manage 24 designated core platform services.
- **DSA investigations are accelerating:** 14 formal proceedings have been opened against Very Large Online Platforms (VLOPs), with X (formerly Twitter) receiving the first DSA fine of EUR 120 million in December 2025 for deceptive blue checkmark design, dark patterns, and transparency failures.
- **The FSR is predominantly targeting Chinese and Gulf state-backed entities:** all ex officio investigations and most in-depth merger reviews have involved companies receiving subsidies from China or the UAE, creating significant geopolitical friction with Beijing.
- **The Data Act applies from September 2025**, establishing new IoT data access rights, cloud switching obligations, and B2B data sharing rules that will affect manufacturers, cloud providers, and data-intensive businesses across all sectors.
- **Compliance costs are substantial:** gatekeepers report spending hundreds of millions on DMA compliance teams; VLOPs must maintain systemic risk assessments and transparency reports; FSR notifications add months to M&A timelines; and the Data Act requires fundamental redesign of connected product data architectures.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 Context

The period from 2022 to 2025 marks a watershed in European competition and digital regulation. Frustrated by years of slow ex post antitrust enforcement against dominant technology companies -- where Google, for instance, accumulated over EUR 8 billion in fines across three cases spanning a decade without meaningful market change -- the EU legislator adopted a fundamentally different approach: ex ante regulation that imposes obligations on firms before harm occurs.

The four regulations examined in this report represent distinct but complementary pillars of this new approach. The DMA addresses the structural power of gatekeeper platforms. The DSA tackles content governance and platform transparency. The FSR closes a gap in competition law by scrutinising foreign government subsidies that EU state aid rules cannot reach. The Data Act creates the conditions for a competitive data economy by breaking data silos and reducing switching costs.

### 1.2 Scope

This report examines: (1) the legal framework of each regulation, including CELEX references, application dates, and institutional responsibilities; (2) designation and notification processes; (3) enforcement actions and decisions taken through early 2026; (4) the interactions and overlaps between the four instruments; (5) practical implications for affected businesses; and (6) the emerging enforcement trajectory.

## 3. The Digital Markets Act: Taming the Gatekeepers

### 3.1 Objectives and Architecture

The DMA establishes a regulatory framework for "gatekeepers" -- large digital platforms that serve as critical intermediaries between business users and end users. Unlike traditional competition law, which requires lengthy market definition and effects analysis, the DMA imposes pre-defined behavioural obligations on designated firms, creating what scholars have described as "regulation by designation."

The core innovation of the DMA is its shift from ex post enforcement to ex ante obligations. Rather than waiting for anticompetitive harm to materialise and then pursuing cases that can take a decade, the DMA defines categories of prohibited conduct and mandates compliance from day one of designation.

### 3.2 Gatekeeper Designation

A company is designated as a gatekeeper if it meets three cumulative criteria:

1. **Size:** annual EU turnover of at least EUR 7.5 billion or market capitalisation of at least EUR 75 billion, and provision of a core platform service in at least three Member States.
2. **Gateway position:** at least 45 million monthly active end users and at least 10,000 yearly active business users in the EU.
3. **Entrenched position:** the quantitative thresholds have been met in each of the last three financial years.

As of February 2026, seven gatekeepers have been designated:

| Gatekeeper | Designation Date | Core Platform Services | Number of CPS |
|------------|-----------------|----------------------|---------------|
| **Alphabet** | 6 September 2023 | Google Search, Google Maps, Google Play, Google Shopping, YouTube, Google Ads, Chrome, Android | 8 |
| **Amazon** | 6 September 2023 | Amazon Marketplace, Amazon Ads | 2 |
| **Apple** | 6 September 2023 | App Store, Safari, iOS | 3 |
| **ByteDance** | 6 September 2023 | TikTok | 1 |
| **Meta** | 6 September 2023 | Facebook, Instagram, WhatsApp, Messenger, Meta Ads (Facebook Marketplace undesignated April 2025) | 5 |
| **Microsoft** | 6 September 2023 | LinkedIn, Windows PC OS | 2 |
| **Booking** | 13 May 2024 | Booking.com | 1 |

The initial six gatekeepers were designated in September 2023 and had until 6 March 2024 to comply with DMA obligations. Booking.com was designated subsequently in May 2024, with a six-month compliance window. Notably, Meta's Facebook Marketplace was undesignated in April 2025, reducing Meta's CPS count from six to five.

### 3.3 Core Obligations

The DMA imposes two categories of obligations on gatekeepers:

**Article 5 -- Self-executing obligations (no further specification needed):**
- Prohibition on combining personal data from the core platform service with data from other services without user consent (Art. 5(2))
- Allowing business users to offer different prices and conditions outside the gatekeeper's platform (Art. 5(3))
- Allowing business users to promote offers and conclude contracts with end users outside the platform (Art. 5(4))
- Prohibition on requiring business users to use the gatekeeper's identification or payment services (Art. 5(7))
- Prohibition on requiring business users or end users to subscribe to other core platform services (Art. 5(8))

**Article 6 -- Obligations susceptible of further specification:**
- Prohibition on using non-publicly available data from business users to compete against them (Art. 6(2))
- Allowing end users to uninstall pre-installed apps (Art. 6(3))
- Allowing installation of third-party app stores and sideloading (Art. 6(4))
- Prohibition on self-preferencing in ranking (Art. 6(5))
- Data portability for end users (Art. 6(9))
- Fair and non-discriminatory access for business users to the gatekeeper's search engine ranking, indexing, and crawling data (Art. 6(11))

**Article 7 -- Interoperability obligations:**
- Messaging interoperability for number-independent interpersonal communication services (phased: one-to-one text and images from March 2024; group messaging and voice/video calls by March 2027)

### 3.4 Enforcement Actions and Fines

The Commission has moved with notable speed in DMA enforcement, opening the first non-compliance investigations just weeks after the compliance deadline:

**March 2024 -- Investigations Opened:**
On 25 March 2024, the Commission opened formal proceedings against Alphabet, Apple, and Meta for suspected non-compliance.

**July 2024 -- Meta Preliminary Findings:**
The Commission informed Meta that its "pay or consent" advertising model -- requiring users to either pay a monthly subscription or consent to personalised advertising -- breaches Article 5(2). The Commission's view was that forcing a binary choice between paying and consenting to full data combination does not provide the "less personalised but equivalent" alternative the DMA requires.

**March 2025 -- Alphabet Preliminary Findings:**
The Commission found that Alphabet breaches the DMA in two respects:
- **Google Search self-preferencing (Art. 6(5)):** Alphabet treats its own services (shopping, hotel booking, transport, financial results, sports results) more favourably in Google Search results than similar third-party services.
- **Google Play anti-steering (Art. 5(4)):** Alphabet prevents app developers from freely steering consumers to alternative channels for better offers and charges fees that go beyond what is justified, including high commissions over unduly long periods for digital goods purchases.

**April 2025 -- First Non-Compliance Decisions and Fines:**
On 22 April 2025, the Commission adopted its first non-compliance decisions:

| Company | Fine | Infringement | Details |
|---------|------|-------------|---------|
| **Apple** | EUR 500 million | Art. 5(4) anti-steering | Technical and commercial restrictions preventing developers from steering users to alternatives to the App Store |
| **Meta** | EUR 200 million | Art. 5(2) data combination | "Pay or consent" model from March-November 2024 forced binary choice without equivalent less personalised option |

Apple was additionally ordered to remove the restrictive conditions within 60 days. Meta's fine covered the period from 7 March 2024 (compliance deadline) to November 2024, when Meta introduced a subscription-free model with less personalised advertising.

### 3.5 Booking.com: The Hospitality Gatekeeper

Booking.com's designation in May 2024 carries particular significance for the European hospitality sector. Key obligations include:

- **Parity clause prohibition (Art. 5(3)):** Hotels and other accommodation providers are free to offer different prices and conditions on their own websites or through other channels. This directly addresses the long-standing "rate parity" dispute in online travel.
- **Data portability (Art. 6(9)):** Booking.com launched a Data Portability API enabling travellers to transfer their data to registered third parties.
- **Anti-steering prohibition (Art. 5(4)):** Accommodation providers can promote offers and direct bookings outside Booking.com.

## 4. The Digital Services Act: Platform Accountability at Scale

### 4.1 Objectives and Tiered Architecture

The DSA establishes a comprehensive framework for the responsibility of online intermediaries, with obligations calibrated to the size and societal impact of the platform. The regulation creates a four-tier system:

| Tier | Category | Obligations |
|------|----------|-------------|
| 1 | All intermediary services | Transparency reporting, single point of contact, terms of service compliance |
| 2 | Hosting services | Notice-and-action mechanisms, statement of reasons for content moderation decisions |
| 3 | Online platforms | Complaint-handling systems, trusted flaggers, ban on dark patterns, advertising transparency |
| 4 | VLOPs and VLOSEs (45M+ EU monthly users) | Systemic risk assessments, independent audits, researcher data access, crisis response protocols |

### 4.2 Designated VLOPs and VLOSEs

The Commission has designated platforms in three waves. As of February 2026, the full list comprises:

**First Designation (April 2023 -- 19 platforms):**

| Alibaba AliExpress | VLOP (marketplace) | Alibaba Group |
| Amazon Store | VLOP (marketplace) | Amazon |
| Apple App Store | VLOP (app store) | Apple |
| Booking.com | VLOP (travel) | Booking Holdings |
| Facebook | VLOP (social network) | Meta |
| Google Play | VLOP (app store) | Alphabet |
| Google Maps | VLOP (mapping) | Alphabet |
| Google Shopping | VLOP (marketplace) | Alphabet |
| Instagram | VLOP (social network) | Meta |
| LinkedIn | VLOP (professional network) | Microsoft |
| Pinterest | VLOP (image sharing) | Pinterest Inc. |
| Snapchat | VLOP (social network) | Snap Inc. |
| TikTok | VLOP (video sharing) | ByteDance |
| X (formerly Twitter) | VLOP (social network) | X Corp. |
| Wikipedia | VLOP (encyclopedia) | Wikimedia Foundation |

**Second Designation (December 2023 -- 3 platforms):**
Three adult content platforms were added: Pornhub, Stripchat, and XVideos.

**Third Wave Designations (2024):**
- Shein (April 2024)
- Temu (May 2024)
- XNXX (July 2024)

This brings the total to approximately 25 designated services. Notably, Zalando challenged its designation before the CJEU, and the designation was later overturned on appeal.

### 4.3 VLOP Obligations in Detail

Platforms designated as VLOPs or VLOSEs bear the heaviest obligations under the DSA:

**Systemic Risk Assessment (Art. 34):** VLOPs must identify, analyse, and assess systemic risks stemming from the design, functioning, and use of their services, including risks related to: the dissemination of illegal content; negative effects on fundamental rights; negative effects on civic discourse, electoral processes, and public security; negative effects on gender-based violence and the protection of minors.

**Risk Mitigation (Art. 35):** VLOPs must implement reasonable, proportionate, and effective mitigation measures tailored to the identified systemic risks, which may include adapting content moderation processes, terms of service, algorithmic systems, or advertising systems.

**Independent Auditing (Art. 37):** VLOPs must undergo at least one annual independent audit assessing compliance with DSA obligations and voluntary commitments under codes of conduct.

**Researcher Data Access (Art. 40):** VLOPs must provide access to data to vetted researchers for the purpose of conducting research contributing to the detection, identification, and understanding of systemic risks, as well as to the assessment of risk mitigation measures.

**Advertising Transparency (Art. 39):** VLOPs must maintain publicly accessible advertising repositories containing information on each advertisement displayed, including the content, the advertiser, the period of display, targeting parameters, and total reach.

### 4.4 Enforcement: Investigations and the First Fine

The Commission has opened 14 formal proceedings against VLOPs and VLOSEs as of late 2025:

**X (formerly Twitter) -- First DSA Fine:**
On 5 December 2025, the Commission fined X EUR 120 million, marking the first-ever DSA non-compliance decision. The Commission found three breaches:

1. **Deceptive blue checkmark (Art. 25 -- dark patterns):** X's paid "verified" blue checkmark system allows anyone to purchase verified status without meaningful identity verification, deceiving users about account authenticity.
2. **Advertising transparency failures (Art. 39):** X's advertising repository did not meet DSA requirements for searchability and completeness.
3. **Researcher data access failures (Art. 40):** X failed to provide adequate access to publicly available data for vetted researchers.

X was given 60 days to address the blue checkmark issue and 90 days for advertising and data access remedies.

**TikTok -- Addiction and Minors:**
In August 2024, ByteDance accepted commitments to permanently withdraw TikTok Lite from EU markets after the Commission raised concerns that its "task and reward" feature could be addictive for minors. Separate proceedings continue regarding TikTok's main service.

**Meta -- Election Integrity and Disinformation:**
The Commission initiated proceedings against Meta in April 2024, focusing on suspected failures in handling deceptive advertisements, disinformation, and political content, including concerns about the discontinuation of its election-monitoring tool CrowdTangle ahead of the 2024 European elections.

**Other Investigations:**
Proceedings have been opened against AliExpress (product safety), Temu (product safety, dark patterns), and Shein (product safety, transparency), reflecting the Commission's focus on Chinese e-commerce platforms entering the EU market.

### 4.5 Digital Services Coordinators

While the Commission directly supervises VLOPs and VLOSEs, Member States are responsible for oversight of smaller platforms through designated Digital Services Coordinators (DSCs). Each Member State must designate one DSC, which serves as the single point of contact for cross-border cooperation and handles complaints from users. The DSC network enables coordinated enforcement across borders, though implementation speed has varied significantly across Member States.

## 5. The Foreign Subsidies Regulation: Levelling the Playing Field

### 5.1 Objectives and the Enforcement Gap

Before the FSR, the EU had a significant asymmetry in its competition toolkit: EU state aid rules (Articles 107-109 TFEU) rigorously scrutinise subsidies granted by EU Member States, but no equivalent mechanism existed to address subsidies granted by non-EU governments to companies active in the Single Market. The FSR closes this gap.

The regulation addresses three categories of distortive foreign subsidies:

1. **Financial contributions by non-EU governments** (direct grants, loans, guarantees, tax benefits, or supply of goods/services) that confer a benefit on an undertaking engaging in an economic activity in the EU.
2. **Subsidies linked to concentrations** (mergers and acquisitions).
3. **Subsidies linked to public procurement procedures.**

### 5.2 The Three Enforcement Tools

The FSR establishes three distinct mechanisms:

| Tool | Trigger | Thresholds | Procedure |
|------|---------|-----------|-----------|
| **Ex officio review** | Commission initiative | None (any foreign subsidy) | Market investigation, preliminary review, in-depth investigation |
| **M&A notification** | Mandatory filing | Turnover >EUR 500M + foreign financial contribution >EUR 50M in preceding 3 years | Phase I (25 working days) + Phase II (90 working days, extendable by 15) |
| **Public procurement notification** | Mandatory filing | Contract value >EUR 250M + foreign financial contribution >EUR 4M per non-EU country in preceding 3 years | Preliminary review (20 working days) + in-depth investigation (110 working days, extendable by 20) |

### 5.3 Balancing Test

When the Commission finds a distortive foreign subsidy, it applies a balancing test: the negative distortive effects of the subsidy are weighed against any positive effects on the development of the relevant economic activity. If the negative effects outweigh the positive, the Commission may impose redressive measures, accept commitments, or prohibit the concentration or public procurement award.

### 5.4 Enforcement Cases

The FSR has generated substantial enforcement activity in its first two years, with a notable geographic concentration:

**Merger Cases:**

*e& / PPF Telecom (September 2024) -- First In-Depth Merger Decision:*
Emirates Telecommunications Group Company (e&), majority-owned by the UAE government, notified its acquisition of sole control over PPF Telecom Group (excluding the Czech business). The Commission opened an in-depth investigation in June 2024, identifying distortive foreign subsidies in the form of: (i) an unlimited state guarantee via exemptions from UAE bankruptcy law; and (ii) a term loan from banks whose actions could be attributed to the UAE, with indications it was not obtained at market conditions.

The Commission conditionally approved the transaction after e& committed for ten years to: remove the unlimited guarantee by amending its articles of association; refrain from financing PPF's EU activities through e& (except emergencies); conduct intercompany transactions on market terms; and notify the Commission of future acquisitions not otherwise notifiable under the FSR.

*ADNOC / Covestro (November 2025) -- Second In-Depth Merger Decision:*
Abu Dhabi National Oil Company (ADNOC) notified its approximately EUR 14.7 billion acquisition of Covestro. The Commission identified an unlimited state guarantee from the UAE (exemption from insolvency proceedings under UAE Bankruptcy Law) as a potentially distortive subsidy. The Commission found that the conditions offered by ADNOC, including a EUR 1.17 billion capital increase commitment, may have deterred other bidders, and that the combined entity could engage in more aggressive investment strategies to the detriment of competitors.

ADNOC committed to: (i) amending its articles of association to remove the UAE bankruptcy law exemption for ten years; and (ii) granting access to certain sustainability-related Covestro patents on transparent terms for an unlimited duration.

**Public Procurement Cases:**

*CRRC / Bulgarian Rail Tender (2024) -- First Public Procurement Investigation:*
The Commission opened its first in-depth public procurement investigation into CRRC, the Chinese state-owned rail equipment manufacturer, regarding a tender for rail equipment supply to Bulgaria. CRRC subsequently withdrew from the tender, and the investigation was closed without substantive findings.

*CRRC / Lisbon Light-Rail Tender (November 2025):*
The Commission opened a second investigation into CRRC regarding its participation in a Lisbon light-rail vehicles tender, signalling persistent scrutiny of Chinese state-owned enterprises in European public procurement.

*Nuctech / Security Equipment (December 2025):*
The Commission opened an in-depth investigation into Nuctech, a Chinese security scanning equipment manufacturer. This followed a probe launched in April 2024, accompanied by dawn raids at Nuctech's premises in Poland and the Netherlands.

**Ex Officio Investigations:**

*Temu (December 2025):*
The Commission reportedly raided Temu's premises in Ireland as part of a probe into possible non-EU subsidies received by the Chinese e-commerce platform.

### 5.5 Notification Volumes and China's Response

By 31 October 2024, the Commission had received 120 prenotification discussions, over 100 formal M&A filings, and 1,108 filings in connection with 213 public procurement tenders. The administrative burden of the notification system has attracted criticism, particularly from dealmakers who report that the parallel FSR filing adds weeks or months to transaction timelines already subject to EU and national merger control.

Since the FSR entered into force, almost all in-depth reviews and all ex officio investigations have involved Chinese or UAE-backed entities. In January 2025, the Chinese Ministry of Commerce (MOFCOM) issued a final determination concluding that the FSR and the Commission's investigations constitute "trade and investment barriers." This finding has no direct legal effect in the EU but underscores the geopolitical dimension of the regulation.

## 6. The Data Act: Unlocking the Data Economy

### 6.1 Objectives and Scope

The Data Act is the EU's most ambitious initiative to date in data governance, aiming to unlock the economic value of non-personal data generated by connected products and related services. The European Commission estimated that the data economy could contribute EUR 829 billion to EU GDP by 2028, but that most IoT data (approximately 80%) goes unused because of contractual lock-ins, technical barriers, and unclear access rights.

The regulation applies from 12 September 2025, with certain provisions (notably cloud switching fee abolition) phased in through January 2027.

### 6.2 Key Provisions

The Data Act contains six major chapters of obligations:

**Chapter II -- B2C and B2B Data Access from Connected Products (Arts. 3-7):**
Users of connected products (consumers and businesses) have the right to access and use data generated by their use of IoT devices. This includes smart home devices, connected vehicles, fitness trackers, industrial machinery, and agricultural equipment. The data holder (typically the manufacturer) must provide data in a structured, commonly used, and machine-readable format. Users may also instruct the data holder to share data with third parties.

Data holders may not use the data to develop competing products that compete with the connected product of the user. Third-party recipients face similar restrictions and must not use the data to profile individuals.

**Chapter III -- B2B Data Sharing Obligations (Arts. 8-12):**
When data sharing is mandated by EU or national law, the data holder must provide data on fair, reasonable, and non-discriminatory (FRAND) terms. Compensation is permitted but must be reasonable and may include a profit margin, except where the recipient is an SME or not-for-profit research organisation, in which case costs must be limited to the marginal cost of making the data available.

**Chapter IV -- Unfair Contractual Terms (Arts. 13-16):**
The Data Act addresses the power imbalance in data sharing contracts by declaring certain contractual terms imposed unilaterally by a stronger party as unfair and therefore not binding. Terms presumed unfair include: excluding or limiting liability; granting one party exclusive rights to determine contract compliance; and enabling unilateral termination without reasonable notice.

The Commission has been tasked with developing Model Contractual Terms (MCTs) to assist businesses in negotiating balanced data sharing agreements.

**Chapter V -- B2G Data Sharing in Exceptional Need (Arts. 14-22):**
Public sector bodies may request data from private data holders in situations of "exceptional need," which encompasses: public emergencies (pandemics, cyberattacks, natural disasters); and non-emergency situations where the data is necessary for a task in the public interest and cannot be obtained by other means. Compensation is limited to the marginal costs of making the data available, and the public body may not use the data for purposes other than those originally specified.

**Chapter VIII -- Cloud Switching and Interoperability (Arts. 23-31):**
Cloud service providers must: remove technical, contractual, and commercial barriers to switching; ensure that customers can port their data and applications to another provider within 30 days; provide functional equivalence where technically feasible; and gradually reduce and ultimately abolish switching charges (full abolition by 12 January 2027).

Providers must also ensure interoperability with open standards and implement adequate security measures during the transition period.

**Chapter X -- International Data Access Safeguards (Art. 32):**
The Data Act includes protections against unlawful international data transfers. Data processing service providers must take all reasonable technical, legal, and organisational measures to prevent non-EU government access to non-personal data held in the EU where such access would conflict with EU or Member State law.

### 6.3 Relationship with GDPR and Trade Secrets

The Data Act explicitly preserves the primacy of the GDPR: all personal data processing under the Data Act must comply with the General Data Protection Regulation, and the Data Act does not create any new legal basis for processing personal data. Where IoT data contains both personal and non-personal elements (which is often the case), mixed datasets are subject to both the GDPR and the Data Act.

Trade secrets are protected through specific provisions requiring confidentiality agreements and proportional access measures. Data holders may refuse access where disclosure would seriously undermine their trade secrets, but must demonstrate the specific risk and, where possible, offer technical solutions (anonymisation, aggregation) that preserve confidentiality while enabling access.

### 6.4 Practical Impact

The Data Act will have far-reaching effects across multiple sectors:

- **Automotive:** connected car manufacturers must share vehicle-generated data (driving patterns, diagnostics, location) with car owners and, at their direction, with independent repair shops, insurers, and fleet managers.
- **Smart home:** manufacturers of connected thermostats, lighting, and security systems must make usage data accessible and portable.
- **Industrial IoT:** machine tool and factory equipment manufacturers must share production data with the businesses operating the machinery.
- **Cloud providers:** AWS, Azure, Google Cloud, and European providers (OVHcloud, Hetzner, Scaleway) must implement switching and portability mechanisms by September 2025, with switching fees abolished by January 2027.

## 7. Interaction Between the Four Regulations

### 7.1 Overlapping Subjects

Several companies are simultaneously subject to multiple regulations. Alphabet, for instance, is: (a) a DMA gatekeeper for eight core platform services; (b) subject to DSA obligations as operator of multiple VLOPs (YouTube, Google Play, Google Maps, Google Shopping) and a VLOSE (Google Search); and (c) a major cloud provider affected by the Data Act's switching provisions. Similarly, Amazon is a DMA gatekeeper, a VLOP under the DSA, and a cloud provider (AWS) subject to the Data Act.

### 7.2 Complementary Enforcement

The four regulations address different aspects of the same market power dynamics:

- The **DMA** targets the structural power of gatekeepers through behavioural obligations.
- The **DSA** addresses the informational and content-related externalities of large platforms.
- The **FSR** ensures that foreign-subsidised competitors do not undercut firms that compete on their merits in the internal market.
- The **Data Act** reduces lock-in and promotes competition by making data portable and accessible.

Together, they create a layered regulatory environment where a single business decision -- such as a cloud provider's pricing or data access policy -- may implicate multiple regulations simultaneously.

### 7.3 Potential Tensions

Despite their complementary objectives, the four regulations can create conflicting obligations. For example:
- DMA data portability requirements may conflict with GDPR data protection obligations when personal data is involved.
- Data Act sharing mandates may clash with trade secret protections.
- DSA transparency requirements may conflict with a gatekeeper's obligation to protect business user data under the DMA.
- FSR notification timelines can delay M&A transactions already subject to EU and national merger control timetables, creating cumulative procedural burdens.

## 8. Practical Implications for Businesses

### 8.1 For Big Tech Companies

The seven DMA gatekeepers face the most comprehensive compliance challenge. Each must: restructure core platform service offerings to permit sideloading, alternative payment systems, and data portability; file detailed compliance reports; submit to continuous monitoring; and risk fines of up to 10% of worldwide turnover (approximately USD 30 billion for Alphabet) for non-compliance. The Apple and Meta fines of 2025 demonstrate that the Commission is prepared to impose significant penalties relatively quickly.

### 8.2 For Online Platforms

The DSA's VLOP obligations require substantial investment in: systemic risk assessment teams; content moderation transparency; independent audit capabilities; researcher data access infrastructure; and crisis response protocols. The compliance cost of the DSA's annual supervisory fee alone (up to 0.05% of worldwide net income) represents a significant recurring expense.

### 8.3 For Companies Involved in Cross-Border M&A

Any acquisition involving a target or acquirer with over EUR 500 million in EU turnover and over EUR 50 million in foreign financial contributions in the preceding three years triggers a mandatory FSR notification. The parallel FSR process adds time and complexity to what are already lengthy merger control procedures.

### 8.4 For IoT Manufacturers and Cloud Providers

The Data Act requires IoT manufacturers to redesign their products to be "data-sharing by design," ensuring that data generated by connected devices is accessible to users in real time and in interoperable formats. Cloud providers must invest in portability tools, implement switching protocols, and phase out switching fees -- a direct challenge to the business model of providers that have historically relied on lock-in effects to retain customers.

### 8.5 For SMEs

While the four regulations primarily target large firms, SMEs experience indirect effects: as business users of gatekeeper platforms, they benefit from DMA obligations (fairer ranking, anti-steering prohibitions, data portability); as data recipients, they enjoy preferential terms under the Data Act (cost-limited access); as suppliers, they may be caught in FSR notification processes for public procurement contracts above EUR 250 million.

## 9. Enforcement Trends and Outlook

### 9.1 The Commission as Super-Regulator

The DMA and FSR grant the Commission exclusive enforcement powers, while the DSA splits enforcement between the Commission (for VLOPs and VLOSEs) and national Digital Services Coordinators (for smaller platforms). This concentration of enforcement authority in the Commission reflects a deliberate choice to ensure uniform application but raises capacity concerns: the Commission is simultaneously enforcing traditional competition law, state aid rules, the DMA, the DSA, and the FSR.

### 9.2 First-Mover Enforcement Strategy

The enforcement pattern through early 2026 reveals a clear strategy: the Commission has chosen high-profile targets and visible infringements to establish precedent and deter non-compliance. The DMA fines against Apple (EUR 500 million) and Meta (EUR 200 million) sent a clear signal. The DSA fine against X (EUR 120 million) established that content design choices (not just content moderation failures) can constitute violations. The FSR investigations have overwhelmingly targeted Chinese and UAE entities, establishing the regulation's practical scope.

### 9.3 Anticipated Developments

Looking forward, several enforcement trends are expected:

- **DMA:** Alphabet is likely to face a formal non-compliance decision regarding Google Search and Google Play, potentially carrying the largest DMA fine to date given its worldwide turnover.
- **DSA:** investigations into TikTok, Meta (election integrity), and Chinese e-commerce platforms (product safety) are expected to yield decisions in 2026.
- **FSR:** the volume of M&A notifications is expected to grow as deal activity recovers, with continued scrutiny of sovereign wealth fund-backed acquisitions.
- **Data Act:** enforcement will begin in earnest once the regulation's full application date passes in September 2025, with early cases likely to focus on cloud switching and IoT data access.

### 9.4 The Digital Omnibus Simplification

In early 2025, the European Commission proposed a Digital Omnibus package that would simplify certain reporting and compliance obligations under the DMA and DSA, in line with the broader competitiveness agenda advocated by the Draghi and Letta reports. However, core enforcement mechanisms and substantive obligations are expected to remain intact.

## 10. Conclusions and Recommendations

The DMA, DSA, FSR, and Data Act represent the most comprehensive regulatory overhaul of European digital markets since the creation of the Single Market. Together, they create a regulatory architecture that addresses market power (DMA), content governance (DSA), subsidy-driven distortions (FSR), and data access (Data Act) in a layered but increasingly complex framework.

**For EU policymakers:**
- Ensure adequate Commission staffing and resources for simultaneous enforcement of the DMA, DSA, and FSR, as enforcement credibility depends on institutional capacity.
- Provide clear guidance on the interaction between the four regulations to reduce compliance uncertainty, particularly where obligations overlap or conflict.
- Monitor the cumulative compliance burden on businesses subject to multiple regulations and consider consolidation where appropriate.
- Pursue international dialogue to prevent retaliatory measures, particularly from China in response to FSR enforcement.

**For national governments:**
- Designate and resource Digital Services Coordinators promptly, as uneven national implementation undermines the DSA's effectiveness.
- Prepare national enforcement frameworks for the Data Act, which relies on Member State authorities for supervision.
- Coordinate with the Commission on FSR cases affecting national public procurement tenders.

**For gatekeeper companies:**
- Treat DMA compliance as a strategic priority, not a legal exercise: the first fines demonstrate that the Commission will not accept formalistic compliance that preserves the economic substance of prohibited conduct.
- Invest in compliance-by-design, embedding DMA and DSA requirements into product development processes rather than retrofitting existing services.
- Prepare for the Data Act's impact on cloud services and connected products well in advance of the application date.

**For businesses active in the EU:**
- Assess FSR exposure for any planned acquisitions or public procurement bids, particularly where non-EU government financial contributions are involved.
- Leverage DMA rights as business users of gatekeeper platforms to negotiate fairer terms and access data.
- Prepare for Data Act obligations regarding connected products and cloud services.
- Engage with the Model Contractual Terms process under the Data Act to shape fair data sharing practices.

The enforcement trajectory through 2025 and into 2026 demonstrates that these regulations are not merely legislative statements of principle. They are operational enforcement instruments with substantial penalties, and the Commission has shown willingness to use them. Businesses that treat compliance as optional face not only financial penalties but reputational damage and structural remedies that may fundamentally alter their business models in the European market.
