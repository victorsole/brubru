# EU Critical Infrastructure Resilience: The Iberian Blackout Case Study

Analysis of the April 2025 Power Grid Failure and EU Policy Implications

Author: Victor Solé Ferioli

Wednesday 30 April 2025

This is a report made for a client of Beresol that has allowed us to publish it as an open report from December 2025.

# Executive Summary

On 28 April 2025, the Iberian Peninsula experienced the most significant power grid failure in European history, affecting approximately 60 million people across Spain and Portugal. This report analyses the incident, its root causes, and the implications for EU critical infrastructure policy.

**Key Findings:**

- **Root cause identified:** Cascading voltage control failures due to insufficient synchronous generation, not cyberattack or renewable energy integration issues.
- **Regulatory gaps exposed:** 19-24 Member States face infringement proceedings for failing to transpose the CER and NIS2 Directives by the October 2024 deadline.
- **Infrastructure deficit:** The Iberian Peninsula's 2.8-3.4% interconnection rate with continental Europe-well below the 15% EU target-created systemic vulnerability.
- **Investment imperative:** €1.8+ trillion in grid modernisation investment required through 2050, with annual spending needing to double from historical levels.

# Introduction

The European Union's energy transition and the increasing complexity of interconnected critical infrastructure systems present both opportunities and vulnerabilities. The April 2025 Iberian blackout occurred just one month after the European Commission adopted its Preparedness Union Strategy, providing an immediate stress test of EU resilience frameworks.

This report examines the blackout through the lens of EU critical infrastructure policy, analysing the effectiveness of existing regulatory frameworks-particularly the Critical Entities Resilience (CER) Directive and the Network and Information Security (NIS2) Directive-and identifying lessons for policymakers, grid operators, and stakeholders across the Union.

# The Iberian Blackout: What happened

The blackout sequence began at 12:32 CEST on Monday, 28 April 2025. Generation trips in southern Spain near Granada, Badajoz, and Seville disconnected approximately 2,200 MW within 20 seconds. Each disconnection further raised voltage levels across the transmission network, triggering protective disconnections at power plants that had not yet reached their voltage thresholds-a phenomenon Red Eléctrica de España (REE) described as 'premature disconnections.'

![Timeline of the 28 April 2025 Iberian Blackout](/open_reports/images/blackout-timeline.png)

_Figure 1: Chronological sequence of the 28 April 2025 blackout, showing cascade progression from initial generation trips to full system collapse._

The ENTSO-E Expert Panel's factual report, published on 3 October 2025, confirmed this was the most significant power system event in Europe in over two decades and the first time cascading voltage increases led to a Continental European blackout. The total disconnected load reached 31 GW (25 GW in Spain, 6 GW in Portugal), affecting approximately 60 million people.

Official investigations ruled out cyberattack and renewable energy as causes. The Spanish Government's June 2025 investigation identified a critical operational gap: of ten units scheduled for voltage control (three nuclear, seven combined-cycle gas), one was declared unavailable on 27 April and not replaced. Voltage instability episodes had been recorded on 22 and 24 April, providing warning signs that went unheeded.

REE's Chief of Operations stated: 'Had conventional power plants done their job in controlling the voltage there would have been no blackout.' An independent peer-reviewed analysis from Pontifical Comillas University concluded the primary cause was insufficient dispatch of synchronous generation with dynamic voltage control combined with limited transmission network resilience.

Economic losses are estimated at €1.6 billion. Restoration required 12 hours for Portugal and approximately 16 hours for Spain, relying heavily on hydroelectric black-start capabilities, support from Morocco's grid (providing up to 900 MW emergency power), and eventual reconnection with France. At least eight deaths resulted from generator misuse, candle fires, and medical equipment failures.

# EU Critical Infrastructure Framework

## Critical Entities Resilience (CER) Directive

The CER Directive (EU) 2022/2557 establishes a framework for the resilience of critical entities across 11 sectors, including energy. Member States were required to transpose the Directive by 17 October 2024 and identify critical entities by 17 July 2026. The Directive mandates that critical entities conduct risk assessments and implement resilience measures covering physical security, personnel security, and crisis management.

As of late 2025, only approximately 10 of 27 Member States have adopted implementing legislation. The Commission sent formal notices to 24 Member States in November 2024 for failing to notify transposition measures. Countries with confirmed transposition include Ireland, Greece, Portugal, Romania, Slovenia, Estonia, and Croatia. Key economies including Germany, France, Belgium, Netherlands, and Spain remain pending.

## Network and Information Security (NIS2) Directive

The NIS2 Directive (EU) 2022/2555 strengthens cybersecurity requirements for essential and important entities, with the energy sector classified as 'essential.' The transposition deadline was also 17 October 2024. Only 4 Member States (Belgium, Croatia, Italy, Lithuania) met the deadline. By July 2025, 14 countries had fully transposed the directive, but 13 remained in legislative process.

The Commission sent letters of formal notice to 23 Member States in November 2024, then escalated to reasoned opinions for 19 Member States on 7 May 2025. Germany's implementation was particularly delayed due to political instability following the traffic-light coalition's collapse. Implementation is complicated by the need to coordinate CER (physical resilience) with NIS2 (cybersecurity resilience), as critical entities under CER automatically qualify as essential entities under NIS2.

## Regulation 2019/941 on Risk Preparedness

Spain formally complied with Regulation 2019/941 by submitting its National Risk-Preparedness Plan in 2022. The plan identified 31 crisis scenarios across 9 categories, designated Red Eléctrica de España (REE) as the responsible system operator, and established prevention, preparation, and mitigation protocols. When the blackout struck, Minister Sara Aagesen confirmed Spain declared an electricity crisis per established protocols.

However, ACER's 2025 monitoring report found that only 10% of national risk preparedness plans include joint measures to mitigate crises and assist neighbouring countries. Cross-sectoral dependencies between gas and electricity systems are routinely overlooked. A second cycle of risk preparedness plans is due by 5 January 2026.

# Lessons learned and policy implications

The Iberian Peninsula's 2.8-3.4% interconnection rate with continental Europe-against EU targets of 10% by 2020 and 15% by 2030-makes it functionally an 'electricity island.' Current Spain-France capacity stands at approximately 2,800 MW, unchanged since the 2015 commissioning of the Santa Llogaia-Baixas underground HVDC line. This isolation directly contributed to blackout severity, as limited interconnection capacity constrained both pre-event support and restoration speed.

![EU Electricity Grid Interconnections Map](/open_reports/images/eu-grid-interconnections.png)

_Figure 2: European electricity transmission network showing interconnection capacities between Member States, highlighting the Iberian Peninsula's limited connections with France._

The Bay of Biscay interconnection represents the transformative project now underway. This €2.2 billion, 400 km submarine and underground HVDC link will connect Gatika (near Bilbao) to Cubnezais (near Bordeaux), adding 5,000 MW capacity and nearly tripling Spain-France interconnection. Construction is on schedule for 2028 commissioning, with the project receiving €578 million in CEF grants and €1.6 billion in EIB financing.

Two additional Trans-Pyrenean projects-the Navarra-Landes and Aragón-Atlantic Pyrenees interconnections-were originally targeted for 2030 but have been delayed to 2036 and 2041 respectively. Following the blackout, Portugal announced a €400 million resilience package including tripling battery storage targets to 750 MW by 2026.

## Investment requirements

ENTSO-E's Ten-Year Network Development Plan identifies €800+ billion in transmission investment needed by 2050, with 108 GW additional cross-border capacity required by 2040. The European Court of Auditors estimates annual grid investment must reach €72 billion through 2030-roughly double historical levels. Total investment through 2050: €1.871 trillion.

The European Commission has proposed quintupling the CEF Energy budget from €5.8 billion (2021-2027) to nearly €30 billion (2028-2034). Eleven Member States representing 86.3% of EU wind and solar capacity, including Spain and Portugal, are projected to miss the 15% 2030 interconnection target despite ongoing investment.

# EU Preparedness Strategy Assessment

## The Preparedness Union Strategy

The European Commission adopted the Preparedness Union Strategy on 26 March 2025-exactly one month before the Iberian blackout. Built on former Finnish President Sauli Niinistö's October 2024 report calling for a 'profound change of mindset,' the strategy outlines 30 key actions and 60 concrete implementation steps to be completed between 2025 and 2028.

![EU Preparedness Union Strategy - Three Pillars](/open_reports/images/preparedness-strategy.svg)

_Figure 3: The three pillars of the EU Preparedness Union Strategy: All-hazards approach, Whole-of-government coordination, and Whole-of-society engagement._

## 4.2 Post-blackout acceleration

The blackout accelerated several strategy components. The EU Stockpiling Strategy, released 9 July 2025, explicitly references 'major energy blackouts' as a key crisis scenario. The 72-hour citizen preparedness campaign urges all EU citizens to maintain essential supplies for minimum three days, with the Commission developing harmonised guidelines alongside Member States.

## rescEU Strategic Reserves

The rescEU strategic reserves continue expanding, with Finland hosting CBRN stockpiles worth €242 million that reached full capacity in 2025. Twenty-two rescEU stockpiles are being developed across 16 Member States. The reserves now include firefighting aircraft (12 new planes to be delivered from 2027), field hospitals, mobile shelters, and power generators-though dedicated energy emergency reserves remain limited.

## PreparEU Pilot Project

The PreparEU pilot project concluded on 4 December 2025 after running for 22 months across Sweden, Norway, Belgium, and Spain with €755,839 in EU funding. It delivered final recommendations on population resilience and published guidance on communicating risks during crises.

# Conclusions

The April 2025 Iberian blackout revealed that formal regulatory compliance-maintaining updated risk preparedness plans, transposing directives, planning interconnection projects-proved insufficient when operational execution failed. Spain had its plan; it activated the correct protocols; yet cascading failures stemming from voltage control deficiencies and insufficient synchronous generation availability caused Europe's most significant grid collapse in two decades.

## Key insights for policymakers

- **Transposition vs Implementation:** the distinction between directive transposition and operational implementation matters enormously. Nineteen Member States face NIS2 infringement proceedings even as they implement the law, and operational failures at well-regulated entities caused the blackout.
- **Physical infrastructure:** the Iberian Peninsula's physical isolation from European grids creates systemic risk that only major infrastructure projects can address. The Bay of Biscay link won't arrive until 2028 and subsequent Trans-Pyrenean projects are now pushed beyond 2035.
- **Solidarity mechanisms:** solidarity mechanisms exist in regulation but proved limited in practice-Morocco's submarine cable provided more immediate support than EU mechanisms, and Portugal restricted Spanish imports for two weeks post-blackout while stabilising its own grid.

## Recommendations

- **Accelerate directive transposition** with technical assistance programmes for Member States still in legislative process, particularly Germany, France, and Spain.
- **Strengthen operational oversight** mechanisms to ensure that transposed requirements translate into effective day-to-day grid management practices.
- **Fast-track interconnection projects** with streamlined permitting and increased CEF Energy funding as proposed in the 2028-2034 MFF.
- **Include energy emergency reserves** in rescEU stockpiling strategy, with dedicated mobile generation capacity for blackout scenarios.
- **Mandate cross-border joint measures** in the second cycle of risk preparedness plans due January 2026, addressing the current 10% inclusion rate.

The EU's response architecture-Preparedness Union Strategy, enhanced rescEU reserves, proposed CEF budget increases, European Grids Package-represents appropriate long-term infrastructure. But the gap between these frameworks and operational resilience remains the central challenge, one that the next cycle of risk preparedness plans (due January 2026) and the pending ENTSO-E recommendations (expected Q1 2026) must urgently address.
