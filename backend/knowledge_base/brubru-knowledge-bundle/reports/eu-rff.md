# EU Recovery and Resilience Facility: Implementation Reaches Critical Juncture


**Author:** Victor Solé Ferioli

This is a report made for a client of Beresol that has allowed us to publish it as an open report from December 2025.

---

## Executive Summary

The EU's €650 billion Recovery and Resilience Facility faces significant absorption risks as implementation enters its final stretch toward the August 2026 deadline. With only **37% of milestones and targets** assessed as fulfilled and **€315 billion** (49%) disbursed by mid-2025, approximately 70% of scheduled deliverables remain incomplete with just 14 months remaining.

Albeit the shining numbers, there has been uneven absorption of such funds. On the winning side, France leads at 76.7% absorption, while Hungary remains frozen at 8.8% due to years-long rule-of-law conditionality between the Orbán government and the Commission. Interestingly, all over Europe, 70% of milestones were concentrated in the final 14 months (until October 2025, so far), creating substantial completion risks. The European Court of Auditors (ECA) found that the RRF is "not actually a performance-based instrument" with climate contributions overestimated by €34.5 billion.

On the good note for the geopolitical interests of the EU, the RRF has helped Russian gas imports to fall from 45% to 19% of EU supply, while solar capacity has reached 338 gigawatts. Which has also led the European Parliament to demand an 18-month extension of the RFF, something the Commission refuses since some Member States, such as Germany, oppose any tempo increase.

## 1. Introduction

The Recovery and Resilience Facility (RRF) represents the EU's most ambitious economic intervention, designed to support Member State recovery from the COVID pandemic while advancing the green and digital transitions. Established under the NextGenerationEU package, born during the long lockdowns of 2020 and 2021, the RRF channels unprecedented resources through a novel performance-based disbursement model tied to milestones and targets rather than costs.

This report examines the financial disbursement patterns across Member States; European Court of Auditors (ECA) findings on design and accountability; milestone achievement rates by policy pillar; the REPowerEU energy integration; implementation challenges; and the August 2026 deadline pressure.


---


## 2. Financial disbursements: uneven progress across Member States

The RRF's total financial envelope now stands at **between €637-650 billion**, comprising €359 billion in non-repayable grants and €278 billion in committed loans, according to official Commission data. Approximately **€95 billion** are available as loans, which remain unclaimed by Member States, permanently reducing the facility's size from its theoretical maximum of €723 billion.

The disbursement timeline in as follows:

| Reference Date | Total Disbursed | Percentage of RRF |
|----------------|-----------------|-------------------|
| August 31, 2025 | €366-367 billion | 55-57% |
| December 31, 2024 | €306.1 billion | 47.1% |
| October 2024 | €267 billion | ~40% |
| February 2024 | €225 billion | ~35% |

Pre-financing totaled **€67 billion** across two waves. The Union provided €67 billion in advance payments to Member States to help them start implementing reforms and investments before meeting formal milestones. This pre-financing was paid in two phases: first, €56.5 billion in 2021–2022, limited to a maximum of 13% of each country’s total allocation and only for national recovery plans approved in 2021, to support the immediate post-COVID recovery; and second, €10.4 billion in 2023–2024 under the REPowerEU initiative, paid to 20 Member States once their dedicated energy chapters were approved, with the specific aim of accelerating energy security, renewables, and the reduction of dependence on Russian fossil fuels.

As per who gets what and how, there are frontrunners and laggards.

**Frontrunners:**
- **Italy** leads in absolute terms with €140.4 billion received against its €194.4 billion allocation (72% absorption by August 2025)
- **France** achieves the highest absorption rate at **76.7%** with approximately €30.8 billion received of its €40.3 billion grant allocation
- **Germany** has significantly accelerated, reaching 65.2% absorption after a €13.5 billion second payment in December 2024.

**Laggards:**
- **Sweden** submitted its first payment request only on 20 December 2024, for €1.6 billion—having received no regular payments to date
- **Hungary** remains frozen at 8.8% absorption, having received only REPowerEU pre-financing while 27 "super milestones" related to rule-of-law conditions remain outstanding
- **Romania** and **Bulgaria** project absorption rates of just 33.1% and 36.8% respectively.

## 3. European Court of Auditors: systemic concerns

The ECA has produced **12+ publications** scrutinising the RRF since 2022, with findings consistently highlighting accountability gaps, performance monitoring weaknesses, and risks to EU financial interests.

In its special report 13/2024, about the absorption delays, published on 2 September 2024, the ECA examined absorption across Spain, Italy, Slovakia, and Romania. It found out that, by the end of 2023, **seven Member States** (Hungary, Netherlands, Belgium, Finland, Ireland, Poland, Sweden) had not received any funds for satisfactory milestone fulfillment. Member States submitted payment requests worth **€228 billion versus €273 billion planned**, approximately 16% less than anticipated. Roughly **50% of RRF funds** were disbursed to 15 Member States who had not yet reached final recipients.

All of this was caused by external circumstances such as inflation, supply shortages, or even geopolitical changes. Another common problem was an underestimation of how long projects would take, cited by 77% of cases, suggesting that timelines were often overly optimistic (something that happens in most projects seeking EU money, by the way…).

This was followed by public procurement difficulties (54%), reflecting delays and complexity in tendering procedures (and the tender process of the EU has never been easy). State aid rules were also a significant challenge for 42%, indicating that compliance with EU competition rules slowed down or complicated project design. Finally, insufficient administrative capacity affected 23%, meaning that a lack of staff, expertise, or institutional resources hindered effective delivery.

The Court of Auditors, through its special report 14/2024, also found that the climate impact of the RRf has been significantly overstated, by at least €34.5 billion. The method used to label spending as “green” allows projects to be counted as 100%, 40%, or 0% climate-relevant, which in practice makes it easy to exaggerate their environmental value (ergo greenwashing, oups!). Auditors found that several projects classified as climate-related had no clear or direct link to the green transition.

Moreover, the special report 22/2024 highlighted serious weaknesses in financial controls. It concluded that existing systems lack key safeguards to prevent double funding between the RRF and other EU programmes. Notably, the Commission itself identified only the first two potential cases of double funding, pointing to insufficient detection and monitoring mechanisms.

In its Review 02/2025, which brought together the findings of all previous audits, the ECA reached a particularly critical conclusion. It stated that the RRF is not genuinely performance-based, despite how it is often presented. According to the review, spending efficiency and value for money cannot be properly assessed, because there is no reliable information on actual costs. As summarised by ECA’s Ivana Maletić: ““EU policymakers must draw lessons from the RRF, and not allow any future similar instrument without having information on actual costs, final recipients and a clear answer to the question of what the citizens actually get for their money.”

## 4. Milestone achievement: concentrated in final years

Of approximately **6985 total milestones and targets** across all national plans, only **2586 (37%)** had been assessed as fulfilled by August 2025. Member States have self-reported an additional 21-34% as completed but awaiting Commission assessment.

| RRF Pillar | Milestones Completed | Total | Progress Rate |
|------------|---------------------|-------|---------------|
| Health and Resilience | 1,152 | 2,503 | **46%** |
| Smart/Sustainable Growth | 1,346 | 3,305 | **41%** |
| Gender Equality | 102 | 253 | **40%** |
| Next Generation | 259 | 665 | **39%** |
| Territorial Cohesion | 1,064 | 2,888 | **37%** |
| Digital Transformation | 877 | 2,464 | **35%** |
| Green Transition | 1,080 | 3,214 | **34%** |

![RRF Milestone Achievement by Policy Pillar](/open_reports/images/rrf_milestone_progress.svg)

A troubling pattern emerges, though: several countries show higher absorption rates than milestone fulfillment—**Greece** received over 50% of its allocation but achieved less than 33% of milestones—indicating disbursement may outpace actual implementation. Common failure areas are:

- Long-timeline infrastructure investments
- Equipment procurement delays
- Slow pass-through of funds to final beneficiaries
- Renewable energy permitting backlogs
- Digital system integration challenges.

## 5. REPowerEU: Energy Transition Integration

All **27 Member States** have now incorporated REPowerEU chapters into their recovery plans following the March 2023 regulation enabling these additions. Bulgaria became the final country with an approved chapter in July 2025. REPowerEU unlocked approximately **€300 billion** across multiple sources:

| Source | Amount |
|--------|--------|
| New ETS grants (Innovation Fund + frontloaded allowances) | €20 billion |
| Brexit Adjustment Reserve transfers | Up to €5.4 billion |
| Repurposed RRF loans | €225 billion |
| Cohesion and agricultural fund transfers | Variable |
| REPowerEU pre-financing | €10.4 billion |

Member States have collectively allocated **42% of RRP funding** to climate objectives, surpassing the mandatory 37% threshold. Total climate expenditure reaches approximately **€275 billion**.

Energy-related measures account for **€184 billion** (nearly 30% of total RRF, and I apologise for the boring set of numbers the reader will have to read from now on):
- €106.5 billion for energy efficiency (€81 billion buildings, €25.5 billion industry)
- €34.2 billion for renewable energy deployment
- €25 billion for electricity networks and smart meters
- €13.6 billion for hydrogen projects.

### Expected energy deliverables by 2026

- **100+ GW** additional renewable capacity
- **33.4 million MWh** annual energy savings
- 14,000 km of modernized transmission lines
- 13 million smart meters installed
- 2 million heating systems replaced.

### Tangible energy security outcomes

| Indicator | 2021 | 2024 | Change |
|-----------|------|------|--------|
| Russian gas imports | 150 bcm (45%) | 52 bcm (19%) | -65% |
| Russian coal imports | Active | 100% banned | Complete phase-out |
| Russian oil imports | 27% of supply | 3% of supply | -89% |
| EU gas demand | Baseline | -17% | Significant reduction |
| EU solar capacity | 138 GW | 338 GW | +145% |

2024 marked the first year solar exceeded coal's share in EU electricity generation.

## 6. Implementation challenges

The 2024 Country-Specific Recommendations (CSRs) identified administrative capacity constraints in **13 Member States**:

- Hungary and Sweden urged to "significantly accelerate implementation given prolonged delays"
- Bulgaria, Germany, Netherlands, and Romania advised to "substantially speed up efforts"
- ECA found Member States struggled to recruit specialized personnel
- IT monitoring systems in two sampled countries "did not provide sufficient information to identify delays in good time".

Even the Committee of the Regions had something to say. In a consultation, the CoR showed that participation at local and regional level has been widely problematic. Almost all respondents reported encountering barriers to involvement, while many pointed to national governments as providing an inappropriate framework for meaningful participation. Most strikingly, one third of respondents stated that national recovery plans do not contribute at all to strengthening territorial cohesion.

Similar concerns were raised by the European Court of Auditors in its special report 09/2025, which found serious weaknesses in how compliance is ensured. The report concluded that the European Commission cannot be certain that Member States have effective systems in place to comply with public procurement and state aid rules.

In practice, national audit bodies generally provided no assurance on state aid compliance at the time payment requests were submitted. Moreover, because RRF financing is not linked to actual costs, the Commission is unable to apply financial corrections for individual procurement violations. The auditors also noted a major accountability gap: there is no mechanism to recover funds in cases where formal milestones are met but the underlying measures remain incomplete.

One quarter of regional stakeholders identified potential overlaps between RRF and other EU funds as posing "high risk," pointing to a potential crowding-out effect on cohesion policy absorption.

## 7. Best Performers: successful implementation models

France exemplifies effective implementation with 76.7% absorption achieved by integrating RRF into its broader "France Reliance" €100 billion national strategy, providing policy coherence and political prioritization.

Despite its massive €194.4 billion allocation, Italy has processed six payment requests successfully and delivered multiple reforms in public administration, justice, and taxation—with 72% of planned spending frontloaded to 2024.

Germany overcame initial criticism for slow progress, significantly accelerating through late 2024 to achieve 65.2% absorption with strong digital transformation and transport reforms.

Concrete achievements from the Commission's mid-term evaluation include:

- Over **28 million MWh** in energy consumption saved
- **5.6 million additional households** with high-capacity internet access
- Almost **9 million people** protected from climate-related disasters
- **16.3 million participants** in education and training programs
- **2.1 million people** finding employment or engaging in job searches.

## 8. The August 2026 deadline

Tick, tack. The Commission has **ruled out any deadline extension**, maintaining the following schedule:

| Milestone | Deadline |
|-----------|----------|
| All milestones and targets achieved | August 31, 2026 |
| Final payment requests due | September 30, 2026 |
| All Commission disbursements completed | December 31, 2026 |
| Plan amendments deadline | August 31, 2026 |
In its June 2025 communication “NextGenerationEU – The road to 2026”, the European Commission warned that time is running short and called on Member States to take corrective action. It urged them to carry out comprehensive reviews of their recovery plans as soon as possible, to remove or scale down measures that cannot realistically be completed, and to split complex, multi-phase projects so that funding is limited to parts that can actually be delivered. Member States were also asked to revise their plans by the end of 2025 in order to avoid losing any of their allocated funds. Member States will continue as they see fit.
Within the European Parliament, concerns about the looming deadline were echoed by the committees on Budgets (BUDG) and on Economic and Monetary Affairs (ECON). In a text adopted in June 2025, MEPs warned that the expiry of the RRF in 2026 represents a serious risk to successful implementation. They suggested a limited extension, but only for projects that are already well advanced and have a high likelihood of success if given additional time. Their concerns were not even taken into consideration, since the Parliament lacks legislative initiative and is, in the end, a macrolobby within the EU institutional construct.
Not all Member States support this approach. Germany has come out clearly against any extension, arguing that the RRF was designed as a temporary and exceptional instrument and that it should therefore come to a clear and definitive end.

### High-risk Member States

| Country | Risk Factor | Status |
|---------|-------------|--------|
| Sweden | No funds received | First payment request December 2024 |
| Hungary | Rule-of-law conditionality | 27 super milestones outstanding |
| Poland | Compressed timeline | 70% of investments planned for 2026 |
| Romania | Administrative inefficiencies | Political uncertainty |
| Bulgaria | Political instability | Six elections in three years |

### Structural vulnerabilities
The ECA warned that payments under the RRF do not always correspond to the real substance of progress made. Disbursements may be released even when they do not accurately reflect the number or significance of milestones and targets achieved, raising concerns about whether funding truly matches results.

The auditors also cautioned against backloading investments, noting that postponing spending toward the end of the programme is likely to exacerbate delays and increase the risk that projects will not be completed on time.

Finally, the ECA highlighted a major accountability gap in the RRF framework: the rules contain no mechanism to recover funds in situations where milestones and targets are formally met, but the underlying measures are never fully completed.

## 9. Conclusions

The RRF represents the EU's most ambitious economic intervention, yet its final implementation phase reveals fundamental tensions between ambition and absorptive capacity. While **€366 billion** in disbursements and tangible achievements in energy transition and digital connectivity demonstrate meaningful impact, the concentration of 70% of milestones in the final 14 months creates substantial non-completion risk.

The ECA's consistent findings about accountability gaps, performance monitoring limitations, and control system weaknesses suggest systemic design flaws that will inform future EU fiscal instruments.

Three factors will determine outcomes:
1. Whether lagging Member States can overcome administrative bottlenecks in time
2. Whether political negotiations yield deadline flexibility for mature projects
3. Whether the Commission can balance strict milestone enforcement with pragmatic accommodation of implementation realities.

The resolution will shape not only €300+ billion in remaining disbursements but the credibility of performance-based EU financing for the decade ahead. Whether the RRF ultimately succeeds as a transformational instrument or serves as a cautionary tale will depend on the collective capacity of EU institutions and Member States to navigate its final, most challenging phase.
