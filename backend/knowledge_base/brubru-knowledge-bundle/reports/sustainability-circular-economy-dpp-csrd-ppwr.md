# Sustainability and Circular Economy: Digital Product Passport, CSRD, PPWR, and Ecodesign (ESPR)

**How Four Interconnected Regulations Are Reshaping Product Design, Corporate Reporting, and Packaging Across the EU Single Market**

**Author:** Victor Sole Ferioli, Founder & Director, Beresol

**Date:** February 2026

**Location:** Brussels, Belgium

---

## Executive Summary

The European Union's sustainability and circular economy agenda has crystallised into four major legislative instruments that, taken together, represent the most comprehensive overhaul of product lifecycle governance in history. These are not isolated regulations -- they form an interlocking system where corporate reporting feeds into product design requirements, which in turn drive packaging standards and digital traceability across entire value chains.

**Key Findings:**

- **ESPR (Regulation 2024/1781)** replaces the 2009 Ecodesign Directive and extends sustainability requirements from energy-related products to virtually all physical goods on the EU market, with the first delegated acts expected in 2026 and product-specific DPP obligations from 2027
- **The Digital Product Passport (DPP)**, established under ESPR, will create a decentralised digital record for each product unit, accessible via QR codes or RFID tags, with batteries the first mandatory category from February 2027 under the Battery Regulation (2023/1542)
- **CSRD (Directive 2022/2464)** requires approximately 50,000 companies to report under European Sustainability Reporting Standards (ESRS) using a double materiality approach, though the February 2025 Omnibus simplification package has dramatically narrowed the scope to companies with over 1,000 employees and EUR 450 million turnover
- **PPWR (Regulation 2025/40)** replaces the 1994 Packaging Directive with directly applicable rules including mandatory recycled content targets (30-65% for plastic packaging by 2030), reuse targets, PFAS bans, and a DPP requirement for packaging -- applying from August 2026
- **Industry readiness remains uneven:** large manufacturers and retailers are investing heavily in compliance infrastructure, while SMEs face disproportionate costs relative to their market share
- **GS1 standards** are emerging as the de facto technical backbone for DPP implementation, with the GS1 Digital Link URI syntax enabling both human-readable QR codes and machine-to-machine data exchange
- **The Omnibus package** represents a significant political recalibration, reducing CSRD scope by approximately 80% and cutting ESRS data points from 1,073 to roughly 320, reflecting industry lobbying pressure and competitiveness concerns

## 1. Introduction

Between 2022 and 2025, the European Union adopted a suite of regulations and directives that fundamentally redefine how products are designed, manufactured, reported on, packaged, and disposed of within the single market. These instruments -- the Ecodesign for Sustainable Products Regulation (ESPR), the Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive (CSRD), and the Packaging and Packaging Waste Regulation (PPWR) -- are bound together by a common thread: the Digital Product Passport (DPP), a concept that runs through all three as both an enabler and a compliance mechanism.

The logic is straightforward, even if the implementation is not. Companies must report their sustainability impacts (CSRD). Products must meet sustainability performance requirements and carry digital records of their composition and environmental footprint (ESPR and DPP). Packaging must contain minimum recycled content, be recyclable, and in many cases be reusable (PPWR). Together, these laws aim to close the loop on the EU's circular economy ambitions, first articulated in the 2015 Circular Economy Action Plan and significantly accelerated under the European Green Deal.

Yet the political landscape has shifted since these instruments were conceived. The February 2025 Omnibus simplification package, driven by competitiveness concerns and industry lobbying, has already narrowed the scope of CSRD dramatically and simplified the reporting standards. This tension -- between environmental ambition and economic pragmatism -- defines the current state of play and will shape implementation for years to come.

This report analyses each instrument in turn, examines their interconnections, assesses practical implications for different market actors, and evaluates the political dynamics that will determine whether the EU's circular economy vision delivers on its promise.

## 3. ESPR: The Ecodesign for Sustainable Products Regulation

### 3.1 From Energy Products to Everything

The original Ecodesign Directive (2009/125/EC) was a targeted instrument that set energy efficiency requirements for energy-related products: washing machines, light bulbs, boilers, and the like. It was effective within its domain -- the Commission estimated it saved consumers EUR 120 billion annually in energy costs by 2020 -- but it was limited in scope.

ESPR represents a paradigm shift. By replacing the Directive with a directly applicable Regulation, and by extending the scope from "energy-related products" to "virtually all physical products placed on the EU market" (excluding food, feed, and medicinal products), the Commission has created a framework regulation of extraordinary breadth. The key word is "framework": ESPR itself does not set product-specific requirements. Instead, it empowers the Commission to adopt delegated acts that define ecodesign requirements for individual product categories.

### 3.2 The Working Plan and Priority Product Groups

The ESPR requires the Commission to adopt a working plan identifying priority product groups for delegated acts. The ESPR Working Plan 2025-2030, due by 19 April 2025, is expected to prioritise:

| Priority Wave | Product Groups | Expected Delegated Act |
|---------------|---------------|----------------------|
| First wave | Textiles and apparel, iron and steel, aluminium | 2026 |
| First wave | Furniture, mattresses, tyres | 2026 |
| First wave | 16 energy-related products (carried over from old Directive) | 2025-2026 |
| Second wave | Electronics (smartphones, tablets, laptops) | 2027 |
| Second wave | Detergents, paints, lubricants | 2027-2028 |
| Third wave | Remaining product categories | 2028-2030 |

### 3.3 Performance Requirements

For each product group, delegated acts may set requirements across seven dimensions:

1. **Durability and reliability** -- minimum lifespan, resistance to wear
2. **Reusability** -- ease of disassembly and component reuse
3. **Upgradability and reparability** -- availability of spare parts, repairability scores
4. **Recyclability** -- material composition enabling end-of-life recycling
5. **Recycled content** -- minimum percentage of recycled materials
6. **Carbon and environmental footprint** -- lifecycle emissions limits
7. **Resource efficiency** -- energy, water, and material consumption in use

### 3.4 Ban on Destruction of Unsold Goods

One of ESPR's most politically visible provisions is the prohibition on destroying unsold consumer products. Every year in Europe, an estimated 4-9% of unsold textiles are destroyed before ever being worn, generating approximately 5.6 million tonnes of CO2 emissions. The ban applies in phases:

- **Large enterprises (>250 employees):** ban on destruction of unsold apparel, clothing accessories, and footwear from 19 July 2026
- **Medium-sized enterprises:** from 19 July 2030
- **Derogations:** permitted only for safety risks or severe damage, and must be documented and justified
- **Disclosure obligation:** companies must report quantities of unsold products discarded as waste

### 3.5 Substances of Concern

ESPR introduces enhanced transparency requirements for substances of concern present in products, building on the REACH framework and the SCIP (Substances of Concern In articles as such or in complex objects (Products)) database maintained by ECHA. Product-specific delegated acts may impose restrictions or disclosure requirements for hazardous substances, with data to be made available through the Digital Product Passport.

## 4. The Digital Product Passport

### 4.1 Concept and Architecture

The Digital Product Passport is perhaps the single most transformative element across this entire legislative package. It is not a single central database. It is a federated, decentralised data ecosystem that connects information from various systems across the supply chain, with the physical product serving as the key to unlock the data.

Each product unit (or batch, depending on the delegated act) will carry a unique identifier linked to a data carrier -- typically a QR code, RFID tag, or NFC chip -- that provides access to structured information about:

- Unique product identifier and compliance documentation
- Manufacturer identity and production facility
- Bill of materials and substances of concern
- Carbon footprint and environmental impact data
- Repairability and recyclability information
- User manuals, safety instructions, and disposal guidance
- Supply chain provenance and materials origin

The decentralised architecture means that data is not stored in one place. Instead, each actor in the value chain maintains their own data, and the DPP provides a standardised way to link and access it. This avoids a single point of failure, reduces vendor lock-in, and allows data owners to maintain control over access rights.

### 4.2 GS1 Standards as the Technical Backbone

GS1, the global standards organisation behind barcodes and identification systems, has emerged as the de facto technical standard-setter for DPP implementation. The GS1 Digital Link URI syntax serves a dual purpose: it integrates a product identifier (such as a GTIN -- Global Trade Item Number) with a web address that can resolve to different types of information depending on who is scanning.

Key technical components include:

- **GS1 Digital Link:** a URI that encodes product identification and resolves to relevant data sources
- **Data carriers:** QR codes that are both human-scannable and machine-readable
- **Resolver infrastructure:** distributed resolvers that direct queries to the appropriate data source
- **Standardised data attributes:** aligned with ESPR delegated act requirements per product category

The Commission has indicated that the DPP registry -- the central infrastructure component that indexes all passports -- must be operational by 19 July 2026.

### 4.3 Battery Passport: The First Mandatory DPP

The Battery Regulation (2023/1542) serves as the pioneer for DPP implementation. From 18 February 2027, all electric vehicle (EV) and industrial batteries with a capacity greater than 2 kWh placed on the EU market must carry a digital battery passport accessible via a QR code. The battery passport must include:

- Battery model and chemistry
- Manufacturing date and facility
- Carbon footprint declaration
- Recycled content percentages (cobalt, lithium, nickel, lead)
- State of health and expected lifetime
- Supply chain due diligence information (particularly for cobalt and lithium sourcing)

### 4.4 DPP Timeline Across Product Categories

| Product Category | Legal Basis | DPP Mandatory From | Data Carrier |
|-----------------|------------|-------------------|-------------|
| EV and industrial batteries (>2 kWh) | Battery Reg. 2023/1542 | 18 February 2027 | QR code |
| Textiles and apparel | ESPR delegated act (pending) | ~2027-2028 | QR code / NFC |
| Iron and steel | ESPR delegated act (pending) | ~2027-2028 | QR code / RFID |
| Electronics | ESPR delegated act (pending) | ~2028-2029 | QR code / NFC |
| Packaging | PPWR (Reg. 2025/40) | 2028-2029 (implementing acts) | QR code |
| Furniture | ESPR delegated act (pending) | ~2028-2029 | QR code |

### 4.5 Challenges and Industry Concerns

The DPP concept is technically elegant but operationally demanding. Key challenges include:

- **Data granularity:** product-level (or even item-level) data requires significant IT investment, especially for high-volume, low-margin products
- **Supply chain transparency:** manufacturers often lack visibility beyond their tier-1 suppliers, yet DPP requires upstream data
- **Cost allocation:** who pays for DPP infrastructure? Large brands can absorb costs; SME suppliers may not
- **Interoperability:** while GS1 standards provide a framework, ensuring cross-border, cross-sector interoperability remains complex
- **Data sovereignty:** the decentralised architecture must balance transparency with legitimate trade secrets

## 5. CSRD: Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive

### 5.1 From NFRD to CSRD

The Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive (2022/2464) replaced the Non-Financial Reporting Directive (NFRD, Directive 2014/95/EU), which applied to approximately 11,700 large public-interest entities. CSRD was designed to dramatically expand both the scope of reporting companies and the depth of required disclosures.

The original CSRD scope was ambitious: approximately 50,000 companies across the EU, including all large companies (exceeding two of three criteria: 250 employees, EUR 50 million turnover, EUR 25 million balance sheet total) and all listed SMEs. The phased implementation was structured as follows:

| Wave | Companies | Reporting From | Reports Published |
|------|-----------|---------------|-------------------|
| Wave 1 | Large public-interest entities already under NFRD (>500 employees) | FY2024 | 2025 |
| Wave 2 | Other large companies meeting size criteria | FY2025 | 2026 |
| Wave 3 | Listed SMEs (except micro-enterprises) | FY2026 | 2027 |
| Wave 4 | Non-EU companies with EUR 150M+ EU turnover | FY2028 | 2029 |

### 5.2 European Sustainability Reporting Standards (ESRS)

CSRD mandates reporting under the European Sustainability Reporting Standards developed by EFRAG (European Financial Reporting Advisory Group). The original ESRS framework comprised:

- **ESRS 1:** General requirements (materiality assessment, value chain boundaries)
- **ESRS 2:** General disclosures (governance, strategy, targets, policies)
- **ESRS E1-E5:** Environmental standards (climate change, pollution, water, biodiversity, resource use and circular economy)
- **ESRS S1-S4:** Social standards (own workforce, value chain workers, affected communities, consumers)
- **ESRS G1:** Governance standard (business conduct)

The standards originally contained approximately 1,073 mandatory and voluntary data points.

### 5.3 Double Materiality

CSRD's most conceptually significant innovation is the double materiality approach. Companies must assess and report on both:

- **Impact materiality:** how the company's activities affect people and the environment (the "inside-out" perspective)
- **Financial materiality:** how sustainability matters create risks and opportunities that affect the company's financial position and performance (the "outside-in" perspective)

A sustainability matter is material -- and must be reported on -- if it is material from either perspective. This goes significantly beyond the financial materiality standard used in US reporting frameworks (such as the SEC's climate disclosure rules) and reflects a distinctly European approach to corporate accountability.

### 5.4 The Omnibus Simplification: A Political Earthquake

On 26 February 2025, the European Commission published the Omnibus simplification package, fundamentally altering the CSRD landscape. On 9 December 2025, the Parliament and Council reached a provisional agreement. The changes are dramatic:

**Scope reduction:**
- New thresholds: only companies with >1,000 employees AND >EUR 450 million net turnover are in scope
- Listed SMEs removed entirely from the CSRD scope
- Financial holding undertakings exempted
- Estimated scope reduction: from ~50,000 companies to approximately 6,000-8,000

**Stop-the-clock mechanism:**
- On 14 April 2025, the Council adopted a two-year postponement of CSRD requirements for Wave 2 and Wave 3 companies
- Wave 1 companies (already reporting for FY2024) receive a transition exemption, with no additional requirements for FY2025 and FY2026 beyond what they reported for FY2024

**ESRS simplification:**
- Data points reduced from 1,073 to approximately 320 (a 70% reduction)
- Clearer guidance on double materiality assessment
- Greater flexibility for "undue cost or effort" exemptions
- Simplified delegated regulation expected for formal consultation in early 2026

**Expected timeline:**
- Publication in the Official Journal: approximately March 2026
- Member State transposition: 12 months from publication
- New scope applicable: from FY2027

### 5.5 Practical Implications

The Omnibus simplification has created a two-tier reality. Wave 1 companies (those already reporting) must maintain their CSRD disclosures but face no escalation in requirements until the new framework takes effect. Companies that were preparing for Wave 2 or Wave 3 may now fall outside the scope entirely. Yet many of these companies have already invested in reporting infrastructure, and institutional investors, banks, and supply chain partners may continue to demand sustainability disclosures regardless of legal obligation.

## 6. PPWR: Packaging and Packaging Waste Regulation

### 6.1 From Directive to Regulation

The Packaging and Packaging Waste Regulation (2025/40) replaces Directive 94/62/EC, which had governed EU packaging policy for three decades. The shift from a Directive (requiring national transposition) to a directly applicable Regulation is itself significant: it eliminates the patchwork of 27 different national implementations that created compliance headaches for cross-border trade.

PPWR was adopted on 19 December 2024, published in the Official Journal on 22 January 2025, entered into force on 11 February 2025, and will apply generally from 12 August 2026.

### 6.2 Packaging Waste Reduction Targets

PPWR sets binding waste reduction targets relative to 2018 baseline levels:

| Year | Reduction Target |
|------|-----------------|
| 2030 | 5% reduction in packaging waste per capita |
| 2035 | 10% reduction |
| 2040 | 15% reduction |

### 6.3 Mandatory Recycled Content for Plastic Packaging

One of PPWR's most consequential provisions is mandatory minimum recycled content for plastic packaging:

| Packaging Type | Target by 2030 | Target by 2040 |
|---------------|---------------|---------------|
| Contact-sensitive PET packaging | 30% | 50% |
| Contact-sensitive plastic packaging (non-PET) | 10% | 50% |
| Single-use plastic beverage bottles | 30% | 65% |
| Other plastic packaging | 35% | 65% |

Recycled content is measured as post-consumer recyclate (PCR), meaning material recovered from consumer waste streams, not industrial scrap. This creates significant demand for high-quality recycled plastics, particularly food-grade recycled PET.

### 6.4 Reuse Targets

PPWR introduces mandatory reuse targets for specific packaging formats:

| Packaging Category | 2030 Target | 2040 Target |
|-------------------|------------|------------|
| Transport packaging (general) | 40% reusable | 70% reusable |
| Intra-EU transport packaging (B2B) | 100% reusable | 100% reusable |
| Alcoholic and non-alcoholic beverages (excl. milk/wine) | 10% reusable | 40% reusable |
| Grouped packaging (excl. cardboard) | 10% reusable | 25% reusable |
| Takeaway food and beverages | 10% reusable | 40% reusable |

Additionally, takeaway operators must accommodate reusable containers brought by customers at no additional charge.

### 6.5 PFAS Ban

From August 2026, per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS) -- commonly known as "forever chemicals" -- will be banned from food contact packaging. This affects grease-resistant coatings used in fast food wrappers, pizza boxes, microwave popcorn bags, and similar applications. The ban is one of the regulation's earliest application dates and will require rapid reformulation by packaging manufacturers.

### 6.6 Recyclability Requirements

All packaging placed on the EU market must be recyclable by 2030. This means packaging must be designed so that the material can be collected, sorted, and reprocessed into secondary raw materials of sufficient quality to substitute virgin materials. The Commission will adopt implementing acts establishing detailed design-for-recycling criteria for each packaging material.

From 2035, packaging must be "recycled at scale," meaning that collection, sorting, and recycling infrastructure must actually exist and operate at commercial scale for the packaging type in question.

### 6.7 Harmonised Labelling

PPWR introduces harmonised labelling requirements across the EU, replacing the current fragmentation of national symbols:

- **Material identification:** standardised marking of packaging materials
- **Sorting instructions:** harmonised consumer-facing labels indicating correct waste stream
- **Deposit return scheme (DRS) marking:** clear labelling for packaging subject to deposit systems
- **Digital information:** QR code or data carrier linking to substance of concern information

Harmonised labelling implementing acts are expected in 2028-2029.

### 6.8 Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR)

PPWR strengthens EPR obligations, requiring producers to bear financial and operational responsibility for the collection, sorting, and recycling of their packaging. EPR fees must be modulated based on recyclability, recycled content, and reusability -- rewarding sustainable packaging design and penalising difficult-to-recycle formats.

## 7. Interconnections and Regulatory Coherence

### 7.1 The Disclosure Chain

These four instruments form a coherent (if complex) disclosure chain:

1. **CSRD** requires companies to report on their environmental impacts, including resource use and circular economy (ESRS E5), pollution (ESRS E2), and climate change (ESRS E1)
2. **ESPR** translates corporate sustainability commitments into product-level performance requirements
3. **DPP** makes product-level sustainability data digitally accessible throughout the value chain
4. **PPWR** applies the same logic specifically to packaging, with its own recycled content, recyclability, and labelling requirements

### 7.2 Data Flows

The DPP acts as the connective tissue. Data reported under CSRD (e.g., Scope 3 emissions, recycled content percentages) feeds into DPP disclosures at the product level. PPWR's substance of concern information for packaging is delivered via the same digital data carrier infrastructure that ESPR mandates for products. A consumer scanning a QR code on a product could, in principle, access both ESPR-mandated product information and PPWR-mandated packaging information.

### 7.3 Regulatory Overlaps and Gaps

Several areas of overlap require careful coordination:

- **Recycled content:** both ESPR (via delegated acts) and PPWR set recycled content requirements, potentially for the same product-packaging combination
- **Substances of concern:** ESPR, PPWR, and the REACH Regulation all address hazardous substances, with different disclosure mechanisms
- **DPP for packaging:** PPWR requires digital information on packaging, while ESPR mandates DPPs for products -- the relationship between the product DPP and the packaging DPP is not yet fully clarified
- **Carbon footprint:** CSRD requires corporate-level carbon reporting; ESPR delegated acts may require product-level carbon footprint declarations; the methodology must be consistent

## 8. Industry Impact and Practical Implications

### 8.1 Manufacturers

Manufacturers face the broadest compliance burden. They must:

- **Design for sustainability:** meet ESPR ecodesign requirements (once delegated acts are adopted)
- **Implement DPP infrastructure:** create and maintain digital product passports, including supply chain data collection
- **Report under CSRD:** if within scope, prepare annual sustainability reports under ESRS
- **Reformulate packaging:** ensure packaging meets PPWR recyclability, recycled content, and PFAS-free requirements
- **Invest in IT systems:** DPP compliance alone requires significant investment in product lifecycle management, data management, and GS1-compliant identification systems

Estimated compliance costs for a mid-size manufacturer (500-1,000 employees) range from EUR 500,000 to EUR 2 million in initial investment, with ongoing annual costs of EUR 200,000-500,000.

### 8.2 Retailers

Retailers, particularly those selling private-label products, face dual obligations as both producers (for their own brands) and distributors (for third-party products):

- **EPR obligations:** financial responsibility for packaging of products they place on the market
- **DPP facilitation:** ensuring DPP data carriers are maintained on products throughout the retail chain
- **Reuse infrastructure:** particularly for online retailers, who must offer reusable shipping options from 2030 under PPWR
- **Consumer information:** transmitting sustainability information to consumers at point of sale

### 8.3 Recyclers and Waste Management

The recycling industry stands to benefit significantly from PPWR's mandatory recycled content targets, which guarantee demand for post-consumer recyclate. However, challenges include:

- **Quality standards:** food-grade recycled PET and other high-quality recyclates require advanced sorting and decontamination technology
- **Feedstock availability:** current collection and sorting rates may not generate sufficient high-quality feedstock to meet 2030 targets
- **Investment requirements:** EUR 6-10 billion in new recycling capacity estimated to be needed across the EU by 2030
- **Chemical recycling debate:** whether chemically recycled material qualifies as "recycled content" under PPWR remains contested

### 8.4 Standards Bodies and Technology Providers

GS1 and other standards organisations are positioned as critical infrastructure providers:

- **GS1:** Digital Link URI standard, Global Trade Item Numbers (GTINs), serialisation standards
- **CEN/CENELEC:** European standardisation mandates for recyclability criteria, recycled content measurement, and DPP technical specifications
- **ISO:** international alignment on lifecycle assessment (ISO 14040/14044) and carbon footprint (ISO 14067) methodologies
- **Technology providers:** DPP platforms, blockchain-based traceability solutions, QR code management systems, and compliance software represent a growing market estimated at EUR 5-8 billion by 2030

### 8.5 SMEs

Small and medium-sized enterprises face a particular challenge. While the Omnibus package has removed most SMEs from CSRD scope, ESPR and PPWR apply regardless of company size -- they regulate products and packaging, not corporate entities. An SME manufacturer must still comply with ecodesign requirements and DPP obligations once the relevant delegated acts enter into force.

The Commission has indicated that SME-specific guidance, simplified DPP templates, and longer transition periods will be provided in delegated acts. Whether these accommodations are sufficient remains to be seen.

## 9. Political Dynamics and the Omnibus Recalibration

### 9.1 The Green Deal Backlash

These regulations were conceived during the height of the European Green Deal's political momentum (2019-2022), when the von der Leyen I Commission made climate and sustainability its flagship priority. By 2024-2025, the political wind had shifted. The European Parliament elections of June 2024 saw gains for centre-right and right-wing parties sceptical of regulatory burden. Farmers' protests across Europe in early 2024 highlighted the political costs of environmental regulation. Industry associations, led by BusinessEurope, mounted an increasingly effective campaign framing sustainability regulation as a competitiveness handicap vis-a-vis the United States and China.

### 9.2 The Omnibus as Political Compromise

The February 2025 Omnibus simplification package was the Commission's response to this political shift. By dramatically narrowing CSRD scope (from ~50,000 to ~6,000-8,000 companies) and simplifying ESRS (from 1,073 to ~320 data points), the Commission sought to demonstrate responsiveness to competitiveness concerns while preserving the core framework.

The provisional agreement reached on 9 December 2025 between Parliament and Council went further than the Commission's original proposal, adding a net turnover threshold of EUR 450 million. Environmental NGOs criticised the deal as gutting CSRD's ambition. The financial sector expressed concern that reduced corporate reporting would undermine the data availability needed for SFDR and Taxonomy compliance. Industry welcomed the simplification but noted that voluntary reporting would likely continue for companies seeking green finance or supply chain contracts with large corporates.

### 9.3 PPWR: Hard-Fought Compromises

The PPWR legislative process was among the most contested of the entire Green Deal package. Key battles included:

- **Reuse targets:** the European Parliament's original position favoured higher mandatory reuse targets; industry (particularly the paper and cardboard sector) argued that recycling should be treated as equivalent to reuse; the final text represents a compromise with modest reuse targets
- **Recycled content:** plastic producers supported ambitious targets (which create demand for their recycled products); virgin plastic producers opposed them; the final targets are achievable but challenging
- **PFAS ban:** unanimously supported by health and environmental groups; resisted by food packaging manufacturers who argued reformulation timelines were too short
- **Deposit return schemes:** some Member States (Germany, the Nordics) already operate DRS; others (France, Spain, Italy) resisted mandatory EU-wide harmonisation; the final text permits national flexibility

### 9.4 ESPR: The Delegated Act Battleground

Because ESPR is a framework regulation, the real regulatory substance will emerge through delegated acts. These acts will be developed through the comitology process, with significant input from industry stakeholders. The Commission's ESPR Working Plan 2025-2030 will trigger intense lobbying as sectors seek to influence performance requirements, DPP specifications, and transition timelines.

The textile sector, likely the first non-energy product group to face ESPR delegated acts, has been particularly vocal. Fast fashion retailers express concern about cost impacts; luxury brands worry about disclosing supply chain details through DPPs; textile recyclers welcome recycled content mandates. The destruction ban for unsold textiles, which takes effect in July 2026 for large enterprises, has already prompted changes in inventory management practices.

## 10. Conclusions and Outlook

The EU's sustainability and circular economy regulatory package represents an unprecedented experiment in product lifecycle governance. The ambition is clear: by mandating sustainability requirements at the product level (ESPR), creating digital traceability throughout value chains (DPP), requiring corporate-level sustainability reporting (CSRD), and setting binding targets for packaging circularity (PPWR), the EU aims to make the circular economy the default operating model for the single market.

Yet the implementation road is long and contested. The Omnibus simplification of CSRD reveals the political limits of sustainability regulation -- even within the EU, the world's most ambitious jurisdiction on these matters. The phased approach to ESPR delegated acts means that product-specific requirements will emerge gradually, creating uncertainty for manufacturers who must plan investments years in advance. PPWR's recycled content and reuse targets require massive infrastructure investment that is not yet funded.

Three factors will determine whether this legislative architecture achieves its environmental objectives:

1. **Delegated act ambition:** the substance of ESPR delegated acts will define whether ecodesign requirements drive genuine sustainability improvements or become a compliance exercise
2. **DPP interoperability:** if the Digital Product Passport ecosystem fragments across sectors, standards, and Member States, its value as a transparency tool diminishes significantly
3. **Enforcement capacity:** market surveillance authorities across 27 Member States must be resourced and empowered to verify compliance -- a perennial weakness in EU product regulation

For industry actors, the message is clear: regardless of short-term political fluctuations, the direction of travel is towards greater product transparency, higher sustainability performance requirements, and mandatory circularity. Companies that invest early in DPP infrastructure, sustainable product design, and circular packaging will be better positioned than those that wait for enforcement deadlines.
