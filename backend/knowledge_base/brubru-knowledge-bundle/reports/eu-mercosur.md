# Executive Summary


After more than 25 years of negotiations, the European Union and the Mercosur bloc (Argentina, Brazil, Paraguay, and Uruguay) concluded in December 2024 a landmark trade agreement. If totally ratified, this partnership will establish the world's largest free trade area, encompassing approximately 780 million consumers and representing nearly a quarter of global GDP. It helped that, politically, Argentina moved from Peronista governments that tended to be very critical of this FTA, to Milei’s election in 2023, with a radically different agenda than his predecessors (both in hardcore style and policies).


The agreement promises significant benefits for European industrial exports, particularly automobiles, machinery, and pharmaceuticals, with estimated annual tariff savings of €4 billion. However, it has generated substantial opposition from European agricultural sectors, particularly beef, poultry, and sugar producers, who fear competition from lower-cost South American imports produced under less stringent regulatory standards. Politically, farmers felt their voices heard with tractors filling the streets of Brussels several times, and votes with a new farmer party in the Netherlands (among other examples).


As of December 2025, the ratification process remains contentious. France, Italy, Poland, Hungary, Austria, the Netherlands, and Ireland have expressed concerns or outright opposition, potentially forming a blocking minority in the Council. The European Commission has in turn proposed agricultural safeguard mechanisms to address these concerns, and the Council and Parliament reached provisional agreement on these measures on 17 December 2025. The signing has been delayed to January 2026. And it may be delayed again if need be…


# Introduction


The EU-Mercosur Partnership Agreement represents one of the most ambitious and complex trade negotiations in the history of both blocs. Initiated in 1999, discussions have repeatedly stalled due to competing interests, changing political landscapes, and evolving concerns about environmental sustainability and agricultural competitiveness. The Argentinian corralito, the subsequent Kirchner years (2003-15, and then 2019-23), Brazil’s Lulismo and Bolsonarismo… Or other pressing economic issues such as the Great Recession of 2008 (and subsequent depression, which in some EU Member States such as Spain or Italy lasted until 2016), were all moments that pushed the EU-Mercosur PA in the far periphery of daily news.


The agreement gained renewed momentum following the conclusion of negotiations in December 2024 under the second von der Leyen Commission, driven by strategic imperatives to diversify trade relationships amid rising tensions with the United States under the second Trump administration and growing Chinese influence in Latin America. The European Commission formally submitted the agreement to the Council on 3 September 2025, initiating the ratification process.


This report provides a comprehensive analysis of the agreement's provisions, with particular focus on implications for European agri-food sectors. It examines the environmental and sustainability framework, assesses the current political landscape affecting ratification prospects, and offers conclusions on potential impacts for European stakeholders.


# 1\. Background of EU-Mercosur negotiations


Negotiations between the European Union and Mercosur formally started in 1999, following the signature of an Interregional Framework Cooperation Agreement (IFCA) in 1995. The process has been characterised by prolonged periods of stagnation, reflecting the complexity of reconciling divergent economic interests and regulatory frameworks.


The first major breakthrough occurred at the G20 Summit in Osaka on 28 June 2019, twenty years after the formal beginning of negotiations, when both parties announced an agreement in principle. However, subsequent developments-including record deforestation in the Amazon, the COVID-19 pandemic, and political changes in both regions-delayed finalisation of the agreement.


The election of Javier Milei as President of Argentina in December 2023 marked a significant shift in Mercosur's negotiating position. Unlike his predecessor (Alberto Fernández, from the Kirchnerista side of Argentinian politics, meaning what we would call socialist in Europe), Milei consistently advocated for swift conclusion of the agreement, removing a key obstacle that had hindered progress in 2023. Milei is a self-styled “anarco-libertarian”, very much in the right ideological spectrum.


For the European Union, the agreement serves multiple strategic objectives. First, it represents a significant step in trade diversification, reducing dependence on Chinese supply chains and mitigating the impact of tariffs imposed by the United States. Second, it secures preferential access to critical raw materials essential for the green and digital transitions, including lithium for battery production. Third, it positions the EU as a first-mover in Mercosur markets, given the bloc's limited existing network of free trade agreements.


For Mercosur countries, the agreement offers enhanced access to one of the world's largest consumer markets and supports economic diversification beyond primary commodities. It represents Mercosur's first major external trade agreement, signalling commitment to regional and global integration.

# 2\. Key provisions of the agreement


## Tariff elimination


The agreement provides for progressive elimination of tariffs on approximately 91-92% of goods traded between the two blocs over a period of up to 15 years. For the European Union, this translates to estimated annual savings of over €4 billion in customs duties. Key tariff reductions for EU exports include:


- Automobiles: Reduction from 35% to 0% (phased over 15 years)
- Machinery and mechanical equipment: Elimination of duties up to 20%
- Chemicals and pharmaceuticals: Progressive tariff elimination
- Wines and spirits: Removal of duties ranging from 17% to 35%
- Dairy products: New duty-free quota of 30,000 tonnes of cheese.


For sensitive agricultural products, the agreement establishes tariff-rate quotas (TRQs) rather than full liberalisation. These quotas allow limited quantities of imports at reduced or zero tariffs, with standard duties applying above the quota threshold.

## Geographical indications and public procurement


The agreement provides protection for over 350 European geographical indications (GIs) in Mercosur markets. This includes iconic products such as Parmigiano Reggiano, Prosciutto di Parma, Champagne, Rioja, and numerous other regional specialities. This protection prevents the use of these names for non-authentic products and represents significant value for European food and beverage producers.


For the first time in a trade agreement with Mercosur, European companies will be able to bid for public procurement contracts in Argentina, Brazil, Paraguay, and Uruguay on equal terms with local suppliers. This opens access to government contracts at federal and regional levels, with limited exceptions for Brazil.


# 3\. Impact on European Agri-Food sectors


## Beef and poultry sectors


The beef sector has emerged as the most contentious aspect of the agreement. Under the TRQ provisions, 99,000 tonnes of Mercosur beef will gain access to the EU market at a preferential tariff of 7.5%, compared to standard duties of 40-45%. The quota is divided between fresh/chilled beef (55%) and frozen beef (45%).


While the Commission emphasises that this volume represents only 1.5% of EU beef production and is less than half of current Mercosur imports (206,000 tonnes in 2024), European beef farmers-particularly in France, Ireland, and Poland-contend that any additional imports will exert downward pressure on prices and undermine the viability of family farms.


Academic analysis, including by Professor Alan Matthews of Trinity College Dublin, suggests that the actual market impact may be more modest than critics suggest, as the new quota will partially substitute for existing over-quota imports rather than representing entirely additional volumes.


The poultry sector faces a duty-free quota of 180,000 tonnes from Mercosur, representing approximately 1.3% of EU consumption. This quota is below current import levels from Mercosur (293.000 tonnes in 2024) and is offset by EU global poultry exports of 2.1 million tonnes.


Nevertheless, concerns persist regarding differences in production standards, particularly relating to the use of antibiotics and animal welfare conditions in Brazilian poultry production. European poultry associations have called for stronger enforcement of mirror clauses requiring imported products to meet equivalent standards.


## Sugar and ethanol


The agreement does not create a new sugar quota for Brazil; rather, it converts an existing WTO quota of 180,000 tonnes of raw cane sugar for refining to duty-free status. A small additional quota of 10,000 tonnes is provided exclusively for Paraguay. Together, these volumes account for approximately 1.1% of EU sugar production.


For ethanol, a duty-free quota of 450,000 tonnes is established for industrial use (primarily in the chemical sector), with an additional 200,000 tonnes for fuel ethanol subject to phased tariff reductions over five years. European ethanol producers have expressed concerns about competition from Brazilian sugarcane-based ethanol, which benefits from lower production costs.


## Wine and dairy


European wine producers stand to benefit significantly from the agreement, with elimination of Mercosur tariffs ranging from 17% to 35%. Similarly, spirits including Cognac, Scotch whisky, and Irish whiskey will gain duty-free access.


The dairy sector presents a mixed picture. The EU has secured a duty-free quota of 30,000 tonnes of cheese exports to Mercosur, while Mercosur gains limited quotas for milk powders and infant formula. The protection of European GIs for cheese products (including Parmigiano Reggiano, Gorgonzola, and Roquefort) represents a significant offensive interest for EU dairy producers.

# 4\. Environmental and sustainability concerns


## Paris Agreement as essential element


A distinctive feature of the agreement is the inclusion of the Paris Agreement on climate change as an "essential element." This legal designation means that failure to remain a party to, or act in good faith under, the Paris Agreement could trigger partial or full suspension of the trade agreement.


The agreement’s “sustainable development” chapter (a new feature of FTAs since Canada’s CETA) includes, also in the EU-Mercosur PA, the provisions of the Paris Agreement, which includes commitments from both parties to move towards climate neutrality by 2050, with the EU pledging to reduce domestic emissions by at least 55% by 2030 compared to 1990 levels. Brazil, in its nationally determined contribution (NDC), has pledged to halt illegal deforestation, including in the Brazilian Amazon.


## Deforestation commitments


The Trade and Sustainable Development (TSD) Chapter includes a binding commitment to combat illegal logging and tackle deforestation. An annexed set of commitments provides for cooperation on preventing further deforestation and enhancing efforts to stabilise or increase forest cover from 2030.


Critically, the EU Deforestation Regulation (EUDR) continues to apply to products imported under the agreement. This means that commodities linked to deforestation-including beef, soy, palm oil, wood, cocoa, coffee, and rubber-must demonstrate they were not produced on land deforested after December 2020 to enter the EU market.


## Criticisms and limitations


Environmental organisations and academic commentators have criticised the sustainability provisions as inadequate. Key concerns include:


- The TSD Chapter lacks enforceable trade sanctions for non-compliance with environmental commitments
- Deforestation provisions are considered vague and non-binding by critics
- The "rebalancing mechanism" allows Mercosur countries to challenge EU regulations (including environmental measures) that affect their exports
- Increased trade in agricultural commodities may accelerate land conversion and associated emissions.


The European Ombudsman criticised the Commission for concluding negotiations without completing a comprehensive sustainability impact assessment, raising questions about procedural adequacy.


# 5\. Current political landscape and ratification prospects


## Ratification process


The EU-Mercosur Partnership Agreement follows a dual-track ratification process. The Commission has proposed splitting the agreement into two instruments: the full Partnership Agreement (EMPA), which requires ratification by all 27 Member States under mixed competence, and an Interim Trade Agreement (iTA) covering provisions of exclusive EU competence, which could be provisionally applied following approval by the European Parliament and Council.


This approach would allow the trade provisions to enter into force more quickly, potentially within months of Council approval, while the broader political and cooperation elements await national ratification processes that could take several years.

## Blocking minority dynamics


Under qualified majority voting rules, a blocking minority requires at least four Member States representing 35% of the EU population. The current coalition of France, Italy, Poland, Hungary, Austria, and Ireland exceeds this threshold, potentially blocking the Council vote on signing the agreement.


The December 2025 delay to January 2026 reflects Commission efforts to secure additional safeguards that could satisfy Italian concerns and break the blocking coalition. Italy's Prime Minister Giorgia Meloni has indicated conditional support, suggesting that with adequate agricultural protections, Italy may ultimately support the agreement. I am personally very sceptical about this.


The European Parliament has adopted a nuanced position. In its October 2025 resolution, Parliament welcomed the binding clause on the Paris Agreement and recognised the agreement's potential to strengthen bi-regional cooperation and diversify critical raw material supplies. However, Parliament has historically expressed concerns about environmental and food safety standards.


On 17 December 2025, the negotiators from the co-legislators (Parliament and Council) reached a provisional agreement on the agricultural safeguard regulation, establishing mechanisms for rapid response to market disruptions from Mercosur imports. The European Parliament's final consent will be required before the agreement can enter into force.


## Safeguard mechanisms


In response to agricultural concerns, the Commission has proposed enhanced safeguard mechanisms, including:


- Bilateral safeguard clauses allowing temporary suspension of tariff preferences if imports cause or threaten serious injury to EU producers
- Reinforced monitoring of sensitive agricultural products (beef, poultry, eggs, citrus, sugar)
- Trigger threshold of 8% increase in import volume (reduced from initial 10% proposal) as evidence of serious injury
- Semi-annual Commission reports on agricultural import monitoring
- €1 billion financial reserve for farmers and a €6.3 billion crisis fund in the future EU budget.


# Conclusions


The EU-Mercosur Partnership Agreement represents a defining moment for European trade policy. After a quarter-century of negotiations, the agreement offers substantial opportunities for European industrial exporters while presenting genuine challenges for certain agricultural sectors.


**Key findings of this analysis:**


- **Strategic value:** the agreement provides the EU with enhanced access to a market of 260 million consumers, critical raw materials for the energy transition, and first-mover advantages in a region increasingly courted by China.
- **Agricultural impact:** while quotas for sensitive products are limited relative to EU production (1.5% for beef, 1.3% for poultry), concerns about price pressure and standard asymmetries remain legitimate and must be addressed through robust safeguard mechanisms.
- **Environmental provisions:** the inclusion of the Paris Agreement as an essential element represents an important precedent, though enforcement mechanisms remain weaker than environmental advocates demand.
- **Ratification outlook:** the agreement faces significant political headwinds. Success in January 2026 will depend on the Commission's ability to offer satisfactory assurances on safeguards and standards to wavering Member States, particularly Italy.


**Recommendations for stakeholders:**


- **Agricultural producers** should engage constructively with the safeguard mechanism design process to ensure effective protection against market disruption
- **Industrial exporters** should prepare market entry strategies for Mercosur, particularly for automotive, machinery, and pharmaceutical sectors
- **Wine and spirits producers** should leverage GI protections and tariff elimination to expand presence in South American markets
- **All stakeholders** should monitor the ratification process closely, as implementation timelines will determine when market access improvements become available.


The coming months will be decisive for the EU-Mercosur Partnership Agreement. The outcome will shape not only bilateral trade relations but also the EU's credibility as a negotiating partner in an increasingly contested global trade landscape.
