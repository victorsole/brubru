# Automotive and Platform Economy: Euro 7, AFIR, Platform Work Directive, and AI in Autonomous Vehicles

**A Regulatory Analysis of the EU's Converging Transport, Labour, and Digital Frameworks**

**Author:** Victor Sole Ferioli, Founder & Director, Beresol

**Date:** February 2026

**Location:** Brussels, Belgium

---

## Executive Summary

The European Union's legislative output during the 2019-2024 mandate has produced a dense web of regulations reshaping the automotive industry, the gig economy, and the emerging autonomous vehicle sector. Four distinct but interconnected legislative instruments stand at the centre of this transformation: Euro 7 (Regulation (EU) 2024/1257), the Alternative Fuels Infrastructure Regulation (Regulation (EU) 2023/1804), the Platform Work Directive (Directive (EU) 2024/2831), and the AI Act's provisions on autonomous vehicles (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Annex III).

**Key Findings:**

- **Euro 7** introduces the first-ever regulation of non-exhaust emissions (brake particles, tyre abrasion) alongside battery durability requirements for electric vehicles, but was significantly weakened from the Commission's original proposal following intense lobbying by ACEA and automotive-producing Member States. Application dates run from November 2026 (new light-duty vehicle types) to May 2029 (all new heavy-duty vehicles).
- **AFIR** replaces the former voluntary approach of Directive 2014/94/EU with binding deployment targets: fast chargers every 60 km on the TEN-T core network, hydrogen refuelling every 200 km by 2030, and mandatory ad-hoc payment at all public charging points. Implementation is uneven, with the Netherlands, France, and Germany hosting 61% of EU charging points while covering only 22% of EU territory.
- The **Platform Work Directive** establishes a rebuttable presumption of employment for platform workers, affecting an estimated 28 million workers across the EU (projected to reach 43 million by 2025). Platforms like Uber and Deliveroo must now prove workers are genuinely self-employed. The transposition deadline of 2 December 2026 is creating a scramble across Member States.
- **AI in autonomous vehicles** falls under a dual regulatory architecture: the AI Act classifies autonomous driving systems as high-risk AI, but defers substantive requirements to existing type-approval frameworks (Regulations 2018/858 and 2019/2144) and UN-ECE standards. A landmark UNECE global regulation on Automated Driving Systems is expected for adoption in June 2026.

These four instruments, when considered together, reveal the EU's attempt to manage a triple transition: decarbonising transport, protecting workers in new economic models, and governing AI in safety-critical applications. The practical implications for automakers, charging infrastructure operators, platform companies, and gig workers are profound and, in several areas, contradictory.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 Context

The European automotive sector is undergoing the most significant regulatory transformation in its history. The convergence of climate policy (European Green Deal), digital regulation (AI Act, Data Act), and social policy (Platform Work Directive) is creating a regulatory environment of unprecedented complexity. The sector directly employs 13.8 million people in the EU (6.1% of total employment) and generates 7% of EU GDP.

The 2019-2024 European Commission, under President Ursula von der Leyen, pursued an explicitly transformative agenda through the "Fit for 55" package and the digital strategy. The resulting legislation does not merely update existing standards but fundamentally redefines what a vehicle is, how it is powered, who maintains the infrastructure around it, who delivers goods from it, and what data it generates.

### 1.2 Scope

This report examines four legislative instruments that collectively reshape the automotive and platform economy landscape:

1. **Regulation (EU) 2024/1257** (Euro 7) -- emission standards and battery durability
2. **Regulation (EU) 2023/1804** (AFIR) -- alternative fuels infrastructure deployment
3. **Directive (EU) 2024/2831** -- platform work and algorithmic management
4. **Regulation (EU) 2024/1689** (AI Act) -- provisions applicable to autonomous vehicles

The analysis covers legal requirements, political dynamics, industry reactions, implementation challenges, and practical implications for affected stakeholders.

## 2. Euro 7: Emission Standards for the Post-Combustion Era

### 2.1 Legal Identification

| Element | Detail |
|---------|--------|
| **Full title** | Regulation on type-approval of motor vehicles and engines and of systems, components and separate technical units intended for such vehicles, with respect to their emissions and battery durability (Euro 7) |
| **CELEX** | 32024R1257 |
| **OJ reference** | OJ L, 8 May 2024 |
| **Legal basis** | Article 114 TFEU (internal market) |
| **Procedure** | 2022/0365(COD) |
| **Replaces** | Regulation (EC) No 715/2007 (Euro 6 for light-duty) and Regulation (EC) No 595/2009 (Euro VI for heavy-duty) |
| **Entry into force** | 1 July 2024 |

### 2.2 Genesis and Political Dynamics

The Commission adopted its Euro 7 proposal on 10 November 2022 (COM(2022) 586). From the outset, the proposal became a battleground between environmental ambition and industrial competitiveness concerns.

The original Commission proposal sought to:
- Unify emission standards for cars, vans, buses, trucks, and trailers under a single regulation
- Tighten NOx limits for light-duty vehicles from 60 mg/km to 60 mg/km (lab) but with far stricter RDE enforcement
- Introduce particle number limits covering particles down to 10 nm (PN10), capturing ultrafine particles
- Set ambitious non-exhaust emission limits for brakes and tyres
- Require on-board monitoring (OBM) systems for real-time emission tracking

The legislative process saw significant weakening of the proposal. In the European Parliament, the ENVI Committee initially supported a stronger position, but the plenary vote on 9 November 2023 sided with a more industry-friendly approach. The Council's general approach, reached on 25 September 2023 under the Spanish Presidency, further diluted key provisions.

**Key political fault lines:**

- **Pro-ambition:** France (initially), the Netherlands, Denmark, and Nordic Member States, backed by environmental NGOs (Transport & Environment, European Environmental Bureau)
- **Pro-industry:** Germany, Italy, Czech Republic, Slovakia, and other automotive-producing states, supported by ACEA and national automotive associations
- **Commission:** Commissioner Thierry Breton (Internal Market) publicly contradicted his own institution's proposal, calling it unrealistic -- an extraordinary act of institutional incoherence

The trilogues concluded on 18 December 2023. The final text was formally adopted by the Council on 12 April 2024.

### 2.3 Key Provisions

#### 2.3.1 Exhaust Emission Limits

Euro 7 maintains largely the same exhaust emission limits as Euro 6 for light-duty vehicles, which was the single most criticised aspect by environmental organisations. The primary change lies not in the limit values themselves but in how compliance is measured and enforced:

- **Elimination of conformity factors:** Under Euro 6, Real Driving Emissions (RDE) testing allowed a "conformity factor" multiplier (initially 2.1x, later 1.43x) above lab limits. Euro 7 eliminates this margin, requiring vehicles to meet limits under real-world conditions without adjustment.
- **Extended environmental conditions:** RDE testing must now cover a wider range of temperatures (-7C to 45C, expanded from -2C to 38C under Euro 6d), altitudes (up to 1,800 m), and driving conditions.
- **On-Board Monitoring (OBM):** All vehicles must be equipped with sensors that continuously monitor emission levels and alert the driver when exceedances occur. This represents a paradigm shift from periodic testing to continuous compliance.
- **Extended in-service conformity:** Vehicles must demonstrate compliance for 200,000 km or 10 years (whichever comes first) for light-duty vehicles, significantly extended from Euro 6 requirements.

#### 2.3.2 Non-Exhaust Emissions: A Regulatory First

Euro 7's most novel contribution is the regulation of non-exhaust emissions -- particles generated by brake wear and tyre abrasion. These sources now account for the majority of particulate matter from road transport as exhaust aftertreatment has improved:

**Brake particle emissions:**

| Vehicle Category | PM10 Limit (mg/km) |
|-----------------|---------------------|
| Light-duty vehicles (ICE/hybrid) | 7 mg/km |
| Battery electric vehicles | 3 mg/km |

The lower limit for EVs reflects the reduced brake wear from regenerative braking. The limits apply under standardised test cycles and will require brake manufacturers to redesign friction materials or adopt enclosed brake systems.

**Tyre abrasion:**
The Regulation mandates tyre abrasion testing in accordance with UN-ECE test procedures. While specific numerical limits for tyre particulate emissions were not set in the initial regulation, the Commission is empowered to adopt delegated acts establishing binding limits. The tyre industry (represented by the European Tyre and Rubber Manufacturers' Association, ETRMA) has been working on standardised measurement methodologies, which remain a technical challenge given the variability of road surfaces, driving styles, and tyre compositions.

#### 2.3.3 Battery Durability Requirements

For the first time, EU vehicle type-approval legislation includes minimum battery performance standards for electric and plug-in hybrid vehicles:

| Milestone | Minimum State of Health (SOH) |
|-----------|-------------------------------|
| 5 years or 100,000 km | 80% of original capacity |
| 8 years or 160,000 km | 72% of original capacity |

These requirements serve a dual purpose: protecting consumers from premature battery degradation and ensuring that EVs maintain their environmental credentials over their useful life. An EV with a severely degraded battery operates at reduced efficiency and range, potentially undermining the emissions savings that justified its purchase.

The battery durability requirements also interact with the Battery Regulation (Regulation (EU) 2023/1542), which establishes broader rules on battery sustainability, recycling, and due diligence. Manufacturers must navigate both frameworks simultaneously.

Industry consensus suggests these thresholds are achievable with current battery technology -- most modern lithium-ion battery packs are engineered to retain 80%+ capacity well beyond 100,000 km. However, the requirements will compel manufacturers to guarantee performance formally and may influence warranty terms.

### 2.4 Application Timeline

| Date | Obligation |
|------|------------|
| 1 July 2024 | Regulation enters into force |
| 29 November 2026 | New type-approvals for M1/N1 (cars/vans) must comply |
| 29 November 2027 | All new M1/N1 vehicles sold must comply |
| 29 May 2028 | New type-approvals for M2/M3/N2/N3/O3/O4 (buses, trucks, trailers) must comply |
| 29 May 2029 | All new heavy-duty vehicles sold must comply |

### 2.5 Assessment

Euro 7 represents a paradox. On one hand, it introduces genuinely novel regulatory concepts (non-exhaust emissions, battery durability, on-board monitoring) that reflect the evolving nature of transport pollution. On the other, it fails to meaningfully tighten exhaust emission limits for internal combustion engines, effectively accepting the industry argument that further tightening is unnecessary given the transition to zero-emission vehicles mandated by the 2035 CO2 targets.

Transport & Environment described the final text as "Euro 6.5" -- an incremental update dressed in transformative rhetoric. ACEA, while welcoming the weakening from the Commission proposal, continued to warn that even the reduced requirements would "add complexity and uncertainty" and estimated a cost increase of up to EUR 2,000 per vehicle. This figure has been contested by independent analysts, who put the additional cost at EUR 80-200 for passenger cars in the final, diluted version.

## 3. Alternative Fuels Infrastructure Regulation (AFIR)

### 3.1 Legal Identification

| Element | Detail |
|---------|--------|
| **Full title** | Regulation on the deployment of alternative fuels infrastructure, and repealing Directive 2014/94/EU |
| **CELEX** | 32023R1804 |
| **OJ reference** | OJ L 234, 22 September 2023 |
| **Legal basis** | Article 91(1) TFEU (transport policy) |
| **Procedure** | 2021/0223(COD) |
| **Replaces** | Directive 2014/94/EU (Clean Fuels Directive) |
| **Applicable from** | 13 April 2024 |

### 3.2 From Directive to Regulation: Why It Matters

The shift from the 2014 Directive (which allowed Member States discretion in setting targets through National Policy Frameworks) to a directly applicable Regulation reflects the EU's frustration with uneven infrastructure deployment. Under the old framework, Member States had broad latitude in how -- and whether -- they pursued alternative fuels infrastructure. The result was a patchwork: the Netherlands boasted 114 public charging points per 100 km of road in 2023, while Romania had fewer than 2.

AFIR eliminates this discretion by imposing mandatory, quantifiable deployment targets with specific deadlines. Failure to meet these targets exposes Member States to infringement proceedings before the Court of Justice.

### 3.3 Key Deployment Targets

#### 3.3.1 Electric Vehicle Charging

**Light-duty vehicles (cars and vans):**

| Target | Deadline | Specification |
|--------|----------|---------------|
| Fast charging every 60 km on TEN-T core | End 2025 | Min. 150 kW per charging point, min. 400 kW total power per pool |
| Fast charging every 60 km on TEN-T core | End 2027 | Min. 150 kW per charging point, min. 600 kW total power per pool |
| Fast charging every 60 km on TEN-T comprehensive | End 2030 | Same specifications as core |
| Fleet-based target | Ongoing | Min. 1.3 kW of total publicly accessible charging power per BEV registered; 0.8 kW per PHEV registered |

**Heavy-duty vehicles (trucks and buses):**

| Target | Deadline | Specification |
|--------|----------|---------------|
| Charging pools every 120 km on TEN-T core | End 2025 | Min. 1,400 kW total power (15% of core network covered) |
| Charging pools every 120 km on TEN-T core | End 2030 | Min. 1,800 kW total power (100% of core network) |
| Charging at urban nodes | End 2030 | Min. 900 kW per pool |

#### 3.3.2 Hydrogen Refuelling

| Target | Deadline | Specification |
|--------|----------|---------------|
| Hydrogen stations every 200 km on TEN-T core | End 2030 | Min. 1 tonne/day capacity, 700 bar gaseous hydrogen |
| Urban node coverage | End 2030 | At least one station per urban node |

#### 3.3.3 LNG for Maritime and Road

AFIR also sets requirements for liquefied natural gas (LNG) bunkering at TEN-T core maritime ports by 2025 and TEN-T core inland waterway ports by 2030, as well as shore-side electricity supply at maritime ports.

### 3.4 Consumer Protection: Ad-Hoc Payment

A particularly significant provision is the requirement for ad-hoc payment at all publicly accessible charging points. Operators must allow payment via contactless card terminals or devices with an internet connection (from April 2024 for new stations above 50 kW). This eliminates the frustrating situation where drivers needed multiple apps, RFID cards, or subscriptions to charge at different networks.

Price transparency is also mandated: operators must display prices per kWh, clearly indicate any additional charges, and make pricing data digitally available. From 14 April 2025, operators must provide static and dynamic data (availability, pricing, connector types) through open interfaces, with full DATEX II standard compliance required from April 2026.

### 3.5 Implementation Gaps

Despite the Regulation's ambition, implementation is proving challenging:

**Geographic concentration:** The Netherlands, France, and Germany together host approximately 61% of all EU public charging points while representing only 22% of EU territory. Southern and eastern Member States lag significantly behind.

**Grid capacity:** High-power charging hubs (1,400+ kW for heavy-duty vehicles) require substantial grid connections. In rural areas along the TEN-T network, grid reinforcement may take years and cost millions per connection point. The European Court of Auditors flagged grid capacity as a critical bottleneck in its 2024 special report on EV infrastructure.

**Permitting delays:** Deploying a single charging hub can require permits from multiple authorities (land use, building, electrical, environmental). The EU has proposed streamlining measures under the Net-Zero Industry Act, but implementation varies dramatically across Member States.

**Investment gap:** The European Commission estimates that EUR 35 billion in investment is needed for publicly accessible charging infrastructure by 2030. Current investment rates, while accelerating, remain insufficient to meet AFIR targets, particularly for heavy-duty vehicle charging and hydrogen refuelling.

## 4. The Platform Work Directive

### 4.1 Legal Identification

| Element | Detail |
|---------|--------|
| **Full title** | Directive on improving working conditions in platform work |
| **CELEX** | 32024L2831 |
| **OJ reference** | OJ L, published 11 November 2024 |
| **Legal basis** | Article 153(1)(b) TFEU (working conditions), Article 16(2) TFEU (data protection) |
| **Procedure** | 2021/0414(COD) |
| **Entry into force** | 1 December 2024 |
| **Transposition deadline** | 2 December 2026 |

### 4.2 The Problem Being Addressed

The platform economy has created a new category of work that sits uncomfortably within traditional labour law categories. An estimated 28 million people in the EU perform work through digital labour platforms, a figure projected to reach 43 million. The vast majority are classified by the platforms as self-employed independent contractors, yet many exhibit characteristics traditionally associated with employment: they work according to platform-determined schedules, use platform-provided equipment or follow platform-mandated procedures, wear platform branding, and have their performance monitored and rated by platform algorithms.

This classification has significant practical consequences:

- **No minimum wage** protections
- **No paid leave** (sick, parental, or annual)
- **No social security** contributions by the platform
- **No protection against dismissal** (account deactivation functions as de facto dismissal without recourse)
- **No collective bargaining** rights in most jurisdictions
- **Workers bear** occupational health and safety costs

The Commission estimated that 5.5 million of the 28 million platform workers were incorrectly classified as self-employed -- deprived of rights they would otherwise enjoy under national labour law.

### 4.3 Key Provisions

#### 4.3.1 Rebuttable Presumption of Employment

The Directive's centrepiece is Article 5, which establishes a legal presumption of an employment relationship when facts indicating direction and control are present, as assessed under national law, collective agreements, and the case law applicable in each Member State.

Critically, the burden of proof is reversed: the platform, not the worker, must demonstrate that the relationship is genuinely one of self-employment. Member States must ensure that the presumption can be invoked by the worker, their representatives, or competent authorities (labour inspectors, social security bodies).

The Directive gives Member States latitude in how they implement the presumption. This reflects the tortuous negotiation history: the Commission's original proposal included a list of five control indicators (with two triggering the presumption), but this approach was abandoned after fierce opposition from both platforms and several Member States (notably France, whose "macroniste" economic philosophy favoured platform flexibility).

The final text relies on a "facts-based" assessment under existing national frameworks, which critics argue may reproduce the very ambiguity the Directive was intended to resolve. Spain, which already enacted its "Riders' Law" (Ley Rider, 2021), serves as a laboratory for what implementation might look like -- though compliance and enforcement there have been mixed.

#### 4.3.2 Algorithmic Management Transparency

Articles 7-11 constitute the Directive's second pillar: regulating automated monitoring and decision-making systems that platforms use to manage their workforce.

Platforms must inform workers about:
- The use of automated monitoring systems (GPS tracking, task timing, customer ratings) and what data they collect
- The use of automated decision-making systems (task allocation, pricing, performance evaluation, account restriction or deactivation)
- The main parameters of such systems, including how worker behaviour affects algorithmic decisions
- The grounds for decisions to restrict, suspend, or terminate worker accounts

**Human review requirement:** Workers have the right to obtain human review of significant automated decisions, particularly those affecting their earnings, contractual status, or account standing. This echoes (and in some respects exceeds) the AI Act's requirements for human oversight of high-risk AI systems.

**Prohibited data processing:** Platforms may not process personal data relating to workers' emotional or psychological state, private conversations, data used to predict trade union activity, or biometric data for identification purposes (beyond verification).

#### 4.3.3 Data Protection and Platform Obligations

The Directive requires platforms to conduct Data Protection Impact Assessments (DPIAs) for their algorithmic management systems, in consultation with workers' representatives. These assessments must be shared with workers and with competent authorities upon request.

Platforms must also ensure that automated systems do not create unjustified adverse effects on workers' health and safety, including excessive performance pressure or psychological stress from gamification mechanisms.

### 4.4 Political Dynamics

The Platform Work Directive had one of the most fraught legislative histories of the 2019-2024 mandate:

- **Commission proposal** (December 2021): Included specific control indicators for the employment presumption
- **Parliament position** (February 2023): Adopted a robust position strengthening the presumption (adopted 376-212-15)
- **Council:** Negotiations stalled repeatedly. France, under heavy lobbying from platforms, blocked successive compromise texts. Estonia, Ireland, and Greece also expressed reservations
- **Near-collapse** (December 2023): A qualified majority in Council appeared impossible. The Belgian Presidency was tasked with rescuing the file
- **Breakthrough** (March 2024): The Belgian Presidency brokered a revised text removing the explicit control indicators, replacing them with the "facts-based" approach. France withdrew its objections
- **Final adoption:** Parliament approved on 24 April 2024 (554-56-24); Council formally adopted on 14 October 2024

**Lobbying intensity:** Uber was the dominant industry voice throughout, reportedly spending millions on lobbying and engaging former officials as advisors. The company's arguments -- that automatic employment classification would destroy the flexibility workers valued and cost jobs -- were echoed by multiple MEPs and Council representatives. The European Trade Union Confederation (ETUC) fought tenaciously for the employment presumption, calling the final text "a milestone" while acknowledging that "accompanying measures" remained weaker than hoped.

### 4.5 Transposition Challenges

With the 2 December 2026 deadline approaching, Member States face significant transposition challenges:

- **Defining the presumption trigger:** Since the Directive does not prescribe specific indicators, each Member State must determine when "facts indicating direction and control" activate the presumption. This will inevitably produce divergent national approaches.
- **Existing national frameworks:** Spain (Riders' Law), France (loi d'orientation des mobilites), the Netherlands (pending legislation), and Belgium (law on the nature of working relationships) already have platform-specific rules that must be reconciled with the Directive.
- **Enforcement capacity:** Labour inspectorates across the EU are under-resourced. Enforcing employment status determinations against well-funded platforms requires technical expertise (to understand algorithmic management systems) and legal capacity that many national authorities lack.
- **Cross-border complexity:** Platforms operate across borders, workers may be in different jurisdictions from the platform's establishment, and algorithmic systems are typically centralised. Determining which national law applies in specific cases will generate considerable litigation.

## 5. AI in Autonomous Vehicles

### 5.1 Regulatory Architecture

The governance of AI in autonomous vehicles sits at the intersection of three regulatory layers:

| Layer | Instrument | Scope |
|-------|-----------|-------|
| **Horizontal AI regulation** | AI Act (Regulation 2024/1689) | Risk classification, transparency, human oversight |
| **EU vehicle type-approval** | Regulation 2018/858 (Framework), Regulation 2019/2144 (General Safety) | Market access, vehicle safety requirements |
| **International standards** | UNECE WP.29 regulations | Technical provisions, automated driving systems |

### 5.2 AI Act: High-Risk Classification

Autonomous vehicle AI systems are classified as high-risk under the AI Act's Annex III, Point 2 (safety components of products covered by Union harmonisation legislation listed in Annex I). However, the AI Act adopts a deliberately deferential approach: rather than imposing the full suite of high-risk AI requirements directly, it creates a "bridge" to existing sectoral legislation.

Under Article 6(1), AI systems that are safety components of products falling under EU harmonisation legislation (including the vehicle type-approval framework) are classified as high-risk. However, under Article 6(4), the standard obligations for high-risk AI systems (conformity assessment, quality management, post-market monitoring, etc.) do not directly apply. Instead, these systems must comply with the essential requirements through their sectoral frameworks.

This means that for autonomous vehicles, the practical compliance pathway runs through:
- **Regulation (EU) 2019/2144** (General Safety Regulation): which already mandates advanced safety systems and provides the legal basis for automated driving type-approval
- **Delegated and implementing acts** under that Regulation: specifying technical requirements for specific automation levels
- **UNECE regulations:** providing internationally harmonised technical standards

### 5.3 UNECE WP.29: The Global Standard-Setter

The United Nations Economic Commission for Europe (UNECE) World Forum for Harmonization of Vehicle Regulations (WP.29) is the de facto global standard-setter for vehicle technology. Its Working Party on Automated/Autonomous and Connected Vehicles (GRVA) has been developing regulations for automated driving systems.

**Key developments:**

- **UNECE Regulation No. 157** (Automated Lane Keeping Systems, ALKS): adopted in 2021, permitting Level 3 automation at speeds up to 130 km/h on motorways. This was the world's first binding international regulation for automated driving.
- **Draft global regulation on Automated Driving Systems (ADS):** a comprehensive framework for Level 4+ vehicles (no supervision by a driver required). This regulation, developed by GRVA, will be submitted to WP.29 for adoption at its session on 23-26 June 2026. If adopted, it would for the first time allow deployment of fully autonomous vehicles on public roads under a harmonised international framework.

### 5.4 Connected Vehicle Data: The Data Act Intersection

The EU Data Act (Regulation (EU) 2023/2854), applicable from 12 September 2025, introduces data-sharing obligations that profoundly affect connected and autonomous vehicles.

Modern vehicles generate enormous quantities of data: sensor readings, driving patterns, location data, battery status, diagnostics, and -- for autonomous vehicles -- continuous environmental mapping. Under the Data Act:

- **Vehicle owners and users** gain the right to access data generated by their vehicles and to share it with third parties (independent repairers, insurers, fleet managers)
- **Manufacturers** must design vehicles to make data accessible by default and provide it in a "commonly used and machine-readable format"
- **Third-party access:** independent service providers (repair shops, telematics companies, insurers) can request access to vehicle-generated data, breaking the data monopolies that OEMs have traditionally maintained

The Commission published specific guidance on vehicle data sharing in September 2025, clarifying that in-scope data includes sensor signals, vehicle speed, battery levels, and liquid levels, while advanced driver-assistance system (ADAS) algorithms and crash severity analysis data are deemed out of scope.

ACEA has expressed concern that mandatory data sharing could compromise cybersecurity and intellectual property, publishing a position paper in May 2025 on connected vehicle data sharing that called for a "balanced approach" protecting safety-critical systems.

### 5.5 Competitive Landscape: EU vs US vs China

The EU's regulatory approach to autonomous vehicles contrasts sharply with its competitors:

| Aspect | EU | United States | China |
|--------|-----|---------------|-------|
| **Regulatory approach** | Precautionary, type-approval based | Permissive, state-level variation | State-directed, rapid deployment |
| **Deployment status** | Limited pilots (Germany, France) | Commercial robotaxi services (Waymo in multiple cities) | Commercial services (Baidu Apollo, Pony.ai in 10+ cities) |
| **Data regime** | GDPR + Data Act + AI Act | Sectoral, limited federal rules | Centralised state access, data localisation |
| **Liability framework** | Product Liability Directive revision pending | State tort law, no federal framework | Manufacturer liability emerging |

The EU's multi-layered regulatory approach -- while arguably more comprehensive in protecting fundamental rights and ensuring safety -- risks creating a deployment gap. Waymo and Baidu are already operating commercial autonomous vehicle services at scale, while European pilots remain limited and highly constrained by permitting requirements.

## 6. Cross-Cutting Analysis: Regulatory Interactions

### 6.1 Euro 7 and AFIR: The Complementary Pair

Euro 7 and AFIR were designed as complementary instruments within the "Fit for 55" package. Euro 7 addresses the emissions of individual vehicles; AFIR addresses the infrastructure ecosystem enabling zero-emission vehicles. However, tensions exist:

- **Battery durability (Euro 7) vs charging infrastructure (AFIR):** Euro 7's battery durability requirements protect consumers but may indirectly slow the transition to ultra-fast charging, as repeated high-power charging can accelerate battery degradation. Manufacturers must balance compliance with both frameworks.
- **Timeline misalignment:** AFIR's end-2025 deadline for fast charging on the TEN-T core network runs well ahead of Euro 7's November 2026/2027 application dates. This sequencing is intentional -- infrastructure should precede vehicle mandates -- but implementation delays in AFIR risk creating a chicken-and-egg problem.

### 6.2 Platform Work Directive and Autonomous Vehicles

As autonomous delivery vehicles emerge, a question arises: does the Platform Work Directive's framework apply to the supervisory operators who monitor autonomous fleets remotely? If platforms shift from human riders to autonomous delivery robots, the Directive's relevance to the delivery sector may diminish -- though it will remain critical for ride-hailing and other person-delivered services.

### 6.3 AI Act, Platform Work Directive, and Algorithmic Management

The Platform Work Directive's provisions on algorithmic management overlap with -- but are not identical to -- the AI Act's requirements for high-risk AI in employment contexts. Both instruments require:
- Transparency about automated decision-making
- Human oversight of significant decisions
- Data protection safeguards

However, the Platform Work Directive goes further in some respects (prohibiting specific categories of data processing, such as emotional state detection) while the AI Act applies more broadly (covering all AI in employment, not just platform work). Platforms must navigate both frameworks simultaneously, which may create compliance complexity but also ensures layered protection for workers.

## 7. Industry Reactions and Stakeholder Positions

### 7.1 Automotive Industry (ACEA and National Associations)

ACEA's engagement with Euro 7 was among the most successful lobbying campaigns of the legislative term. The association argued that the Commission's original proposal would:

- Add EUR 2,000 to the average price of a new vehicle
- Divert R&D resources from the electrification transition
- Create implementation costs disproportionate to environmental benefits, given the 2035 ICE phase-out

The European Parliament's vote, which largely aligned with ACEA's position, was celebrated by the industry. However, ACEA continued to express reservations about the final text, describing it as a "step in the right direction" while cautioning that "cost pressure remains high."

Beyond Euro 7, ACEA has raised broader competitiveness concerns. EU car production fell by 6.2% in 2024, and the association has warned that the cumulative regulatory burden (Euro 7 + CO2 targets + Battery Regulation + CSRD + Data Act) risks making European manufacturing uncompetitive against Chinese and American rivals.

### 7.2 Platform Companies

The platform industry's response to the Platform Work Directive has been multifaceted:

- **Uber** lobbied intensively against the employment presumption, arguing it would destroy flexible work opportunities. The company has signalled compliance in principle but is expected to challenge national transposition measures that adopt broad presumption triggers.
- **Deliveroo** withdrew entirely from several European markets (Spain, the Netherlands, Germany) before and during the legislative process, citing unfavourable regulatory environments.
- **Bolt, Glovo, and Wolt** have adopted varying strategies, from resistance (Bolt) to pre-emptive reclassification in select markets (Glovo in Spain, following court rulings and the Riders' Law).

### 7.3 Trade Unions

The European Trade Union Confederation (ETUC) and its sectoral affiliates (UNI Europa for services, ETF for transport) were the primary advocates for the employment presumption. ETUC described the final Directive as "a milestone towards innovation that delivers for all" but acknowledged that the implementation and enforcement provisions were weaker than hoped.

National trade unions have begun preparing for the transposition phase, pushing for robust presumption triggers and adequate resourcing of labour inspectorates.

### 7.4 Environmental Organisations

Transport & Environment, the European Environmental Bureau, and national environmental groups expressed deep disappointment with Euro 7, describing it as a missed opportunity. On AFIR, environmental organisations have been broadly supportive but critical of implementation delays and the geographic concentration of infrastructure investment in wealthy Western European states.

## 8. Practical Implications by Sector

### 8.1 Automakers and OEMs

| Obligation | Impact | Timeline |
|-----------|--------|----------|
| Euro 7 OBM systems | Hardware/software integration for continuous monitoring | By November 2026 (new types) |
| Non-exhaust emissions | Brake system redesign, supplier management | By November 2026 |
| Battery durability certification | Testing, warranty implications, quality control | By November 2026 |
| Data Act compliance | In-vehicle data access architecture | By September 2025 (already applicable) |
| AI Act (for ADAS/AV) | Conformity assessment through type-approval | Ongoing (August 2026 for AI Act high-risk) |

### 8.2 EV Manufacturers

EV manufacturers face the unique position of being both beneficiaries (AFIR creates their market infrastructure) and subjects (Euro 7 imposes battery durability requirements) of the new regulatory framework. Tesla, BYD, Volkswagen Group, Stellantis, and others must:

- Ensure battery management systems deliver the mandated 80%/72% SOH thresholds
- Prepare for Data Act obligations on vehicle-generated data sharing
- Navigate AFIR's interoperability requirements for charging protocols

### 8.3 Charging Infrastructure Operators

AFIR creates a new class of regulated entities. Operators (ChargePoint, Ionity, Fastned, national utilities, and new entrants) must:

- Deploy infrastructure meeting specific technical standards (150 kW minimum, 350 kW ultra-fast)
- Accept ad-hoc payment without subscription requirements
- Provide real-time data on availability, pricing, and connector types
- Transition to DATEX II data standards by April 2026
- Meet accessibility requirements for persons with reduced mobility

### 8.4 Platform Companies

Companies operating digital labour platforms must prepare for:

- Potential reclassification of their workforce as employees in multiple jurisdictions
- Increased costs from social security contributions, minimum wage obligations, and paid leave
- Algorithmic management transparency requirements (disclosure of automated systems)
- Human review mechanisms for significant automated decisions
- Data Protection Impact Assessments for algorithmic management systems

The financial impact is substantial: Uber estimated in its filings that reclassification of all EU drivers as employees could increase costs by 20-30%.

### 8.5 Gig Workers

For the 28 million platform workers across the EU, the Directive's impact depends heavily on national transposition:

- Workers in countries that adopt broad presumption triggers (following the Spanish model) may gain employee status relatively quickly
- Workers in countries with narrow triggers or weak enforcement mechanisms may see limited practical change
- All workers gain algorithmic transparency rights regardless of employment status classification
- Access to social protection (pensions, unemployment, sick pay) depends on reclassification outcomes

## 9. Conclusions and Outlook

### 9.1 Assessment

The four instruments analysed in this report reveal both the ambition and the contradictions of EU regulatory policy:

1. **Euro 7** innovates (non-exhaust emissions, battery durability, OBM) but retreats on its core mission (tightening exhaust limits), producing a regulation that is simultaneously forward-looking in scope and conservative in substance.

2. **AFIR** represents the EU at its regulatory best: clear, measurable targets with binding deadlines. However, implementation capacity -- grid reinforcement, permitting, investment -- remains the binding constraint. The Regulation sets the destination but does not pave the road.

3. **The Platform Work Directive** addresses a genuine social deficit but does so through a framework (national transposition of a directive with broad discretion) that may reproduce the very fragmentation it seeks to eliminate. The employment presumption is a significant conceptual victory for organised labour, but its practical impact will be determined country by country.

4. **AI governance for autonomous vehicles** is the least resolved of the four areas. The AI Act's deferral to sectoral legislation is pragmatic but creates a governance gap that the forthcoming UNECE ADS regulation (June 2026) and Commission delegated acts must fill. Meanwhile, EU-based deployment trails significantly behind the US and China.

### 9.2 Emerging Issues

Several developments will shape the evolution of these frameworks:

- **Commission simplification agenda:** The von der Leyen II Commission has signalled a "25% regulatory burden reduction" target. Euro 7 and parts of AFIR may be candidates for revision under the Omnibus simplification initiative, though weakening environmental or social standards would face resistance from Parliament.
- **Chinese EV competition:** The imposition of provisional anti-subsidy tariffs on Chinese EVs (up to 45.3% from October 2024) interacts with AFIR and Euro 7 by affecting the composition of the EU EV fleet and, consequently, charging infrastructure demand patterns.
- **Autonomous vehicle deployment window:** If the UNECE ADS regulation is adopted in June 2026, the EU has a narrow window to create a favourable deployment framework. Failure to act decisively will cement US and Chinese first-mover advantages.
- **Platform economy evolution:** Generative AI and autonomous delivery technologies may reshape the platform economy faster than the Directive's transposition timeline, potentially rendering some provisions outdated before they take effect.

### 9.3 Recommendations

**For EU institutions:**
- Accelerate AFIR implementation support, particularly grid reinforcement and permitting reform in under-served Member States
- Provide transposition guidance for the Platform Work Directive to reduce divergent national interpretations of the employment presumption
- Adopt a coherent autonomous vehicle deployment strategy bridging the AI Act, General Safety Regulation, and UNECE frameworks

**For Member States:**
- Prioritise charging infrastructure deployment on TEN-T corridors to meet 2025/2027 AFIR deadlines
- Resource labour inspectorates to enforce platform worker rights effectively
- Engage with UNECE ADS regulation to enable timely domestic deployment of autonomous vehicles

**For industry:**
- Begin Euro 7 compliance programmes immediately for the November 2026 type-approval deadline
- Invest in interoperable charging infrastructure ahead of AFIR deadlines
- Platform companies should proactively review employment practices in anticipation of national transposition measures
- Automakers should design data architectures that comply simultaneously with the Data Act, GDPR, and AI Act
