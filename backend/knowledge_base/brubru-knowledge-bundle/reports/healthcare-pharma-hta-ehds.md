## Executive Summary

The European Union is undergoing the most significant transformation of its healthcare regulatory framework in over two decades. Three interconnected legislative instruments -- the revision of the pharmaceutical legislation (the "Pharma Package"), the Health Technology Assessment (HTA) Regulation, and the European Health Data Space (EHDS) -- are collectively reshaping how medicines are developed, assessed, priced, and how health data flows across Europe. Together, they represent a paradigm shift towards greater EU-level coordination in health policy, an area historically dominated by national competence.

**Key Findings:**

- **Pharma Package (deal reached 11 December 2025):** The most comprehensive overhaul since 2004, replacing Directive 2001/83/EC and Regulation (EC) No 726/2004. Introduces a new "8+1(+1)(+1)" regulatory data protection formula (replacing the previous "8+2(+1)"), creates transferable exclusivity vouchers (TEVs) for antimicrobial resistance, mandates shortage prevention plans, and reforms the orphan and paediatric medicines frameworks. Still awaiting formal adoption.
- **HTA Regulation (applying since 12 January 2025):** Regulation (EU) 2021/2282 establishes joint clinical assessments at EU level, beginning with oncology medicines and advanced therapy medicinal products (ATMPs). Orphan medicines join in January 2028; all centrally authorised products by January 2030. Does not cover pricing or reimbursement.
- **EHDS (entered into force 26 March 2025):** Regulation (EU) 2025/327 creates the first domain-specific data space under the EU Data Strategy, with two pillars: primary use (patient access, cross-border health data exchange via MyHealth@EU) and secondary use (health data for research, innovation, and policy-making through Health Data Access Bodies). Full application by 2029-2031.

**Practical impact:** These three instruments will fundamentally alter the operating environment for pharmaceutical companies, generic manufacturers, medical device firms, hospitals, national HTA bodies, health data researchers, and patient organisations across all 27 Member States.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 Context

Health policy in the European Union occupies a unique constitutional position. Under Article 168 TFEU, the EU's role is to complement and coordinate national health policies, with primary responsibility for healthcare organisation and delivery remaining firmly with Member States. Yet the internal market dimension of pharmaceuticals, medical devices, and increasingly health data has driven a steady expansion of EU-level regulation, from the first pharmaceutical harmonisation directives in the 1960s to the comprehensive regime in place today.

By the early 2020s, the existing legislative framework -- built primarily on Directive 2001/83/EC (the Union code relating to medicinal products for human use) and Regulation (EC) No 726/2004 (centralised authorisation procedures) -- was widely recognised as outdated. The COVID-19 pandemic exposed critical vulnerabilities in medicine supply chains, accelerated digitalisation of healthcare, and underscored the need for faster regulatory procedures. Simultaneously, antimicrobial resistance (AMR) was identified as one of the gravest long-term threats to public health globally, with the EU lacking effective "pull" incentives for antimicrobial development.

### 1.2 The Three-Pillar Transformation

The EU's response has been comprehensive, delivered through three interconnected legislative instruments:

1. **The Pharma Package** -- restructuring how medicines are authorised, incentivised, and supplied across Europe.
2. **The HTA Regulation** -- creating EU-level joint clinical assessments to reduce duplication and improve evidence-based decision-making.
3. **The EHDS** -- establishing the legal and technical infrastructure for health data to flow across borders for both clinical care and research.

These three instruments share common objectives: improving patient access to innovative treatments, reducing duplication and fragmentation across 27 national systems, strengthening EU competitiveness in the life sciences sector, and building resilience against future health crises.

### 1.3 Scope of This Report

This report provides a comprehensive analysis of each instrument, covering legal identification, substantive provisions, stakeholder positions, implementation timelines, and practical implications. It is written from a Brussels-based EU policy perspective, aimed at professionals engaged in legislative monitoring, advocacy, and compliance in the healthcare and pharmaceutical sectors.

## 2. Part I: The EU Pharmaceutical Legislation Revision

The revision of the EU pharmaceutical legislation -- commonly referred to as the "Pharma Package" -- represents the most substantial reform of the EU's medicines framework since the adoption of Regulation (EC) No 726/2004 and the codification of Directive 2001/83/EC. The Commission published its proposals on 26 April 2023, and after intensive negotiations, a provisional political agreement was reached between the European Parliament and the Council of the EU in the early hours of 11 December 2025.

## 4. Regulatory Data Protection: The New Formula

### 4.1 The Core Debate

The most politically contentious element of the entire reform has been the duration of regulatory data protection (RDP) for innovative medicines. RDP prevents generic and biosimilar manufacturers from referencing the clinical data of an originator product to obtain their own marketing authorisation. The current system provides for "8+2(+1)" years -- eight years of data exclusivity, two years of market protection, and a potential additional year for new therapeutic indications.

The Commission's original proposal in COM(2023) 192 sought to reduce the baseline to **six years**, with the possibility of extensions through various incentive triggers. This provoked intense opposition from the innovative pharmaceutical industry and several Member States with strong R&D-based pharmaceutical sectors.

### 4.2 The Agreed Formula: 8+1(+1)(+1)

The provisional agreement reached on 11 December 2025 establishes a new formula of **8+1(+1)(+1)**:

| Period | Duration | Condition |
|--------|----------|-----------|
| **Data exclusivity** | 8 years | Automatic for all new medicines |
| **Market protection** | +1 year | Automatic (guaranteed) |
| **Extension 1** | +1 year | For medicines addressing high unmet medical needs |
| **Extension 2** | +1 year | For medicines meeting additional innovation criteria |
| **Maximum total** | **11 years** | With all extensions |

This represents a significant departure from the Commission's original proposal and reflects the influence of the Council, which pushed back strongly against any reduction below eight years of data exclusivity. The restoration of the eight-year baseline was a key demand of Member States such as Germany, Denmark, Belgium, and Ireland -- all home to significant pharmaceutical R&D operations.

### 4.3 Comparison With Current and Proposed Regimes

| Element | Current (2001/83) | Commission Proposal (2023) | Final Agreement (2025) |
|---------|-------------------|---------------------------|----------------------|
| Data exclusivity | 8 years | 6 years (base) | 8 years |
| Market protection | 2 years | Variable (incentive-based) | 1 year (base) |
| Maximum extension | +1 year (new indication) | Up to 12 years total | Up to 11 years total |
| **Total maximum** | **11 years** | **12 years** | **11 years** |

The agreed formula retains the same maximum protection period (11 years) as the current system, but restructures the incentive triggers. The Commission's ambition to use shorter base protection as leverage for steering R&D towards unmet medical needs has been partially preserved through the extension criteria, even as the baseline was restored to eight years.

## 5. Antimicrobial Resistance and the Transferable Exclusivity Voucher

### 5.1 The AMR Crisis

Antimicrobial resistance is one of the most pressing global public health challenges. The EU estimates that AMR-related infections cause approximately 35,000 deaths per year in the EU/EEA alone. The economic pipeline for new antimicrobials has been in decline for decades, with the antibiotics market failing to provide adequate commercial returns to justify R&D investment.

### 5.2 The TEV Mechanism

The most innovative element of the Pharma Package is the introduction of the **Transferable Exclusivity Voucher (TEV)** -- a "pull" incentive designed to reward companies that develop priority antimicrobials addressing critical resistance threats.

Key features of the TEV:

- **Duration:** Grants a 12-month extension to the regulatory data exclusivity period.
- **Transferability:** The voucher can be used by the antimicrobial developer on another centrally authorised product, or transferred (sold) to a different marketing authorisation holder for use on one of their products.
- **Blockbuster clause:** The TEV cannot be applied to products with annual gross sales exceeding EUR 490 million in the preceding four years. This cap was introduced to limit the potential fiscal impact on national healthcare budgets.
- **Priority criteria:** Only antimicrobials addressing priority pathogens identified by the WHO or equivalent EU-level list are eligible.

### 5.3 Environmental and Stewardship Requirements

Companies seeking marketing authorisation for antimicrobial products must additionally provide:

- An **antimicrobial stewardship plan** to ensure responsible use and limit the development of resistance.
- An **evaluation of AMR risk** as part of the mandatory environmental risk assessment (ERA).
- An **antimicrobial study plan** outlining post-authorisation surveillance commitments.

## 6. Orphan and Paediatric Medicines Reform

### 6.1 Merger Into the Pharma Package

One of the structural innovations of the reform is the merger of the Orphan Regulation (EC) No 141/2000 and the Paediatric Regulation (EC) No 1901/2006 into the main pharmaceutical legislation. This consolidation aims to simplify the regulatory landscape and increase coherence between the general and specialised frameworks.

### 6.2 Orphan Medicines

The revised framework maintains strong incentives for orphan (rare disease) medicines whilst introducing differentiation based on unmet medical need:

| Category | Market exclusivity | Condition |
|----------|-------------------|-----------|
| Standard orphan | 9 years | Designated orphan product |
| Orphan with high unmet need | 10 years | Addresses disease with no existing treatment or significant clinical improvement |
| "Breakthrough" orphan | Up to 11 years | Addresses disease with no currently available medicinal treatment |

The reform also introduces more robust criteria for orphan designation, including updated thresholds for prevalence and a requirement to demonstrate "significant benefit" more rigorously.

### 6.3 Paediatric Medicines

The reform addresses long-standing concerns about the efficiency and effectiveness of paediatric investigation plans (PIPs):

- **Iterative PIPs:** Formalisation of a new iterative process for PIPs, already piloted by the EMA, allowing for greater flexibility in the timing and design of paediatric studies.
- **Better integration with adult development:** Paediatric development obligations are more closely aligned with the overall product lifecycle, reducing delays in adult medicine availability caused by PIP compliance requirements.
- **Waivers and deferrals:** Streamlined procedures for waivers and deferrals, with clearer criteria for when paediatric studies are not scientifically justified.

## 7. Medicine Shortages and Supply Chain Obligations

### 7.1 Background

Medicine shortages have become an increasingly frequent and serious problem across the EU, affecting patient care and public confidence. The COVID-19 pandemic dramatically exposed the fragility of global pharmaceutical supply chains and the EU's dependence on active pharmaceutical ingredient (API) manufacturing concentrated in Asia.

### 7.2 New Obligations

The Pharma Package introduces comprehensive shortage prevention and management provisions:

- **Shortage prevention plans:** Marketing authorisation holders (MAHs) are required to develop and maintain shortage prevention plans for prescription medicines and for medicines identified by the Commission as requiring such plans.
- **Early notification:** MAHs must notify anticipated shortages at least **six months in advance**, unless duly justified circumstances prevent compliance.
- **Critical medicines list:** The EMA is tasked with establishing and regularly updating a **list of critical medicines** at EU level, taking into account therapeutic importance, substitutability, and supply chain vulnerability.
- **Monitoring architecture:** Shortages are to be monitored at both national and EU levels, with enhanced coordination between the EMA, national competent authorities, and the European Commission.
- **Supply obligations:** Strengthened obligations on MAHs to ensure continuous supply of authorised medicines, with requirements for multi-source supply chains for critical products.

---

## 8. Environmental Risk Assessment

The Pharma Package significantly strengthens environmental risk assessment (ERA) requirements for medicinal products:

- **Mandatory ERA:** Environmental risk assessment becomes mandatory for all marketing authorisation applications, including for generics and biosimilars where previously it was often waived.
- **ERA for existing products:** A mechanism is introduced for requiring ERA for products already on the market where new environmental risks have been identified.
- **Antimicrobial-specific ERA:** For antimicrobial products, the ERA must specifically address the risk of antimicrobial resistance development through environmental pathways (e.g., manufacturing discharge, human excretion reaching water systems).
- **Post-authorisation ERA updates:** MAHs may be required to update ERA data post-authorisation based on new scientific evidence.

---

## 9. Faster Market Authorisation and Regulatory Modernisation

### 9.1 Accelerated Timelines

The reform introduces measures to speed up the regulatory process:

- **Reduced assessment timelines:** Target timelines for centralised marketing authorisation assessment are reduced, with the goal of bringing new medicines to patients faster.
- **Rolling review:** Provisions for rolling review of applications for medicines addressing public health emergencies, building on the experience gained during the COVID-19 pandemic.
- **Regulatory sandboxes:** Provisions enabling the EMA to support innovation through regulatory sandboxes for novel therapeutic approaches.

### 9.2 Digital and Administrative Modernisation

- **Electronic product information:** Transition towards electronic formats for product information (SmPC, package leaflet), reducing printing costs and enabling faster updates.
- **Electronic submissions:** Full digitalisation of marketing authorisation applications and post-authorisation procedures.

## 10. Stakeholder Positions on the Pharma Package

### 10.1 Innovative Pharmaceutical Industry (EFPIA)

The European Federation of Pharmaceutical Industries and Associations (EFPIA), representing the innovative pharmaceutical industry, has taken a cautiously critical stance on the final agreement. Whilst acknowledging that the retention of eight years of data exclusivity is "an improvement on earlier drafts which sought to reduce RDP to six years," EFPIA maintains that the deal "is not strong enough to move the needle" on attracting and retaining global pharmaceutical R&D investment in Europe.

Specific EFPIA concerns include:

- The expansion of the Bolar exemption, which EFPIA characterises as "an unnecessary move that will frustrate IP enforcement and create legal uncertainty, further eroding competitiveness within the innovative sector."
- The blockbuster clause limiting TEV applicability, which EFPIA argues reduces the incentive's commercial attractiveness.
- The overall framing of the reform as prioritising affordability over innovation, at a time when the US and China are increasing incentives for pharmaceutical R&D.

### 10.2 Generic and Biosimilar Industry (Medicines for Europe)

Medicines for Europe, representing the generic, biosimilar, and value-added medicines industry, has broadly supported the reform's direction. Key positions include:

- **Support for Bolar harmonisation:** The organisation has called for the Bolar exemption to be harmonised across Member States to encourage API development in Europe and prevent anti-competitive delays to generic and biosimilar competition at loss of exclusivity.
- **Shorter exclusivity periods:** Medicines for Europe originally advocated for the Commission's proposed reduction to six years of data exclusivity, arguing that faster generic and biosimilar entry improves affordability and access.
- **Supply chain resilience:** Support for provisions encouraging domestic API manufacturing and supply chain diversification.

### 10.3 Patient Organisations

Patient organisations, including the European Patients' Forum (EPF) and EURORDIS (Rare Diseases Europe), have generally welcomed the reform's emphasis on unmet medical needs and faster access, whilst expressing concerns about:

- The adequacy of incentives for rare disease medicines.
- The risk that data protection periods may still delay generic and biosimilar entry, affecting affordability.
- The need for meaningful patient involvement in EMA decision-making processes.

### 10.4 Member State Divisions

The Council negotiations revealed significant divisions:

- **Pro-innovation bloc** (Germany, Denmark, Belgium, Ireland, Sweden): Pushed for retention of strong data protection to preserve pharmaceutical R&D investment in Europe.
- **Access-focused bloc** (Southern and Eastern European Member States): Favoured shorter exclusivity periods to accelerate generic availability and reduce healthcare expenditure.
- **The compromise:** The 8+1(+1)(+1) formula and the blockbuster clause on the TEV reflect a carefully calibrated balance between these positions.

## 13. Joint Clinical Assessments: Scope and Process

### 13.1 What JCAs Cover

Joint clinical assessments (JCAs) provide a scientific analysis of the clinical evidence on the **relative effects** of a health technology on health outcomes, compared to existing treatments. Critically, JCAs cover only the **clinical domain** -- they do not address:

- Economic evaluation
- Pricing decisions
- Reimbursement decisions
- Budget impact analysis
- Ethical, organisational, or social considerations

These aspects remain exclusively within Member State competence, a fundamental principle that was essential to securing political agreement.

### 13.2 The Assessment Process

The JCA process operates as follows:

1. **Notification:** When a health technology developer submits a marketing authorisation application to the EMA for an eligible product, it must simultaneously notify the HTA Secretariat (hosted by the European Commission) by providing the summary of product characteristics and the clinical overview.
2. **Scoping:** The scope of the JCA is determined based on the clinical data submitted, defining the comparators, patient populations, and outcomes to be assessed.
3. **Assessment:** A sub-group of the Coordination Group on Health Technology Assessment (HTACG) conducts the assessment, drawing on expertise from national HTA bodies.
4. **Joint Clinical Assessment Report:** The report presents the clinical evidence and its assessment, without making a recommendation on pricing or reimbursement.
5. **National uptake:** Member States must **duly consider** the JCA report in their national HTA processes and may not repeat the clinical assessment already covered by the JCA.

### 13.3 "Duly Consider" Obligation

The phrase "duly consider" has been the subject of significant debate. It does not mean that Member States must automatically accept JCA conclusions. Rather, Member States must:

- Take the JCA report into account in their national HTA procedures.
- Not request from health technology developers the same clinical data and evidence already covered by the JCA.
- Complement the JCA with their own national assessments on non-clinical domains (economic, ethical, organisational).
- Document how the JCA was considered in their national decision.

## 14. Phase-In Schedule and Medical Devices

### 14.1 Medicinal Products

| Phase | Date | Scope |
|-------|------|-------|
| Phase 1 | 12 January 2025 | New active substances for oncology and ATMPs |
| Phase 2 | January 2028 | Orphan medicinal products |
| Phase 3 | January 2030 | All new centrally authorised medicinal products |

### 14.2 Medical Devices and IVDs

The HTAR also covers selected **high-risk medical devices** and **in vitro diagnostic medical devices (IVDs)**, though the phase-in is separate and later:

- From **2026**, specific high-risk medical devices and IVDs will be eligible for JCAs. The HTACG was expected to publish its first recommendation for the selection of qualifying devices in Q1 2026.
- The selection criteria focus on devices classified in the highest risk categories under the Medical Device Regulation (MDR, (EU) 2017/745) and the IVD Regulation (IVDR, (EU) 2017/746).

### 14.3 Joint Scientific Consultations

In addition to JCAs, the HTAR provides for **joint scientific consultations (JSCs)**, allowing health technology developers to seek advice from HTA bodies at EU level during the clinical development phase. This mechanism aims to align evidence generation with the requirements of both regulators (EMA) and assessors (HTA bodies), reducing the risk of evidence gaps at the time of marketing authorisation.

## 15. The Coordination Group and Institutional Architecture

### 15.1 The HTACG

The Coordination Group on Health Technology Assessment (HTACG) is the central governance body of the HTAR. It is composed of representatives designated by each Member State, typically from national HTA bodies or health ministries.

Key functions:

- Oversight of joint clinical assessments and joint scientific consultations.
- Adoption of annual work programmes.
- Development of methodological guidelines for JCAs.
- Coordination with the EMA and the European Commission.

The HTACG adopted its first operational work programme for 2025 at its meeting on 28 November 2024, published on 3 December 2024.

### 15.2 The HTA Secretariat

The European Commission provides the **HTA Secretariat**, which delivers administrative, technical, and IT support for:

- The HTACG and its sub-groups.
- Joint clinical assessments and joint scientific consultations.
- Stakeholder engagement (patient, clinical, and industry consultations).
- The IT platform for submission, assessment, and publication of JCA reports.

### 15.3 Stakeholder Involvement

The HTAR mandates stakeholder involvement at multiple stages:

- **Patient and clinical expert input** in JCA scoping and assessment.
- **Health technology developer engagement** through the submission process and factual accuracy checks.
- **Public consultation** on JCA reports before finalisation.

## 16. National Implementation Approaches

The implementation of the HTAR has revealed different national strategies, reflecting the diversity of existing HTA systems across Europe.

### 16.1 Germany (G-BA)

The Gemeinsamer Bundesausschuss (G-BA), Germany's Federal Joint Committee, has indicated that if a JCA report is not available at the time a company submits its national benefit assessment dossier, the G-BA will **pause the national process for up to three months** to wait for its publication and allow for its incorporation. This approach reflects a commitment to using JCA evidence but may extend overall timelines.

### 16.2 France (HAS)

The Haute Autorite de Sante (HAS) has taken a different approach: the **national process will not be delayed** if the JCA report is unavailable. Evaluations will proceed based on nationally submitted evidence, with the JCA incorporated if and when it becomes available. This reflects France's established and autonomous HTA tradition.

### 16.3 Integration of JCA Evidence

Encouragingly, both France, Germany, and the Netherlands have confirmed that manufacturers undergoing JCA will **not need to resubmit** the same clinical data at the national level but can instead reference the JCA dossier and reports. This is a significant practical benefit, reducing the administrative burden on manufacturers and representing a concrete step towards reducing duplication.

### 16.4 Industry Concerns

EFPIA and individual pharmaceutical companies have raised several implementation concerns:

- **Methodological alignment:** Differences between JCA methodology and established national approaches may create uncertainty in evidence planning.
- **Timelines:** The risk that JCA reports may not be available sufficiently early to inform national procedures has been highlighted, particularly given that some national processes operate on tight statutory deadlines.
- **Scope limitations:** The restriction to clinical evidence only, without economic or cost-effectiveness analysis, means that the JCA addresses only part of what national bodies need for their decisions.

## 19. Primary Use: Patient Empowerment and Cross-Border Access

### 19.1 Core Principles

The primary use pillar of the EHDS establishes the right of natural persons to access, control, and share their electronic health data. It builds on and significantly expands the MyHealth@EU infrastructure (formerly the eHealth Digital Service Infrastructure) established under the Cross-Border Healthcare Directive 2011/24/EU.

### 19.2 European Electronic Health Record Exchange Format (EEHRxF)

The EHDS mandates the development and implementation of a **European Electronic Health Record Exchange Format** to ensure interoperability of health data across Member States. This format covers priority categories of health data, implemented in two groups:

**Group 1 (applicable from March 2029):**

- Patient summaries
- ePrescriptions and eDispensations

**Group 2 (applicable from March 2031):**

- Medical images and related reports
- Laboratory results
- Hospital discharge reports

### 19.3 Rights of Natural Persons

Under the primary use framework, individuals have the right to:

- Access their electronic health data in a common, interoperable format.
- Share their data with healthcare providers across the EU.
- Restrict access to specific categories of data or to specific healthcare providers.
- Obtain their data in a portable, machine-readable format.
- Request rectification of incorrect data.

### 19.4 Obligations on Healthcare Providers and EHR Systems

- Healthcare providers must ensure that electronic health data is recorded in interoperable formats.
- Electronic health record (EHR) system manufacturers must ensure their products comply with the EEHRxF and interoperability requirements.
- A certification and labelling scheme for EHR systems is established to ensure compliance.

## 20. Secondary Use: Research, Innovation, and Policy-Making

### 20.1 The Secondary Use Framework

The secondary use pillar creates a legal framework for accessing electronic health data for purposes beyond individual healthcare, including:

- **Scientific research** in the health and care sectors.
- **Innovation**, including the development of AI and machine learning algorithms for healthcare.
- **Policy-making** and public health monitoring.
- **Regulatory activities**, including pharmacovigilance and medical device safety monitoring.
- **Personalised medicine** and biomarker development.
- **Training and education** of health professionals and AI systems.

### 20.2 Data Categories

The secondary use provisions cover a wide range of electronic health data categories:

- Electronic health records (primary care, hospital, specialist).
- Clinical trial data.
- Data from medical devices and in vitro diagnostics.
- Data from wellness applications and wearable devices.
- Genetic and genomic data.
- Health-related administrative data (claims, registries).
- Data from research cohorts and biobanks.
- Pathology data (images and reports).

### 20.3 Permitted and Prohibited Uses

**Permitted uses** (with appropriate safeguards):

- Health-related scientific research.
- Development and innovation of health products and services.
- Public health surveillance and crisis preparedness.
- Regulatory activities (pharmacovigilance, post-market surveillance).
- Official statistics.

**Prohibited uses:**

- Decisions detrimental to the data subject (e.g., insurance underwriting, employment decisions).
- Advertising or marketing of products to identified individuals.
- Use by law enforcement (unless specifically authorised under other EU law).

---

## 21. Health Data Access Bodies and Governance

### 21.1 Health Data Access Bodies (HDABs)

Each Member State must establish one or more **Health Data Access Bodies** to serve as gatekeepers for secondary use of health data. These bodies are responsible for:

- Receiving and evaluating applications for access to health data.
- Issuing data permits specifying the scope, conditions, and safeguards for data access.
- Ensuring that data is provided in secure processing environments.
- Monitoring compliance with permit conditions.
- Imposing sanctions for non-compliance.

Member States were required to designate their HDABs by **June 2025**.

### 21.2 HealthData@EU

**HealthData@EU** is the decentralised EU-wide infrastructure connecting national HDABs, enabling cross-border access to health data for secondary use. It allows researchers and innovators to apply for access to data from multiple Member States through a single access point, whilst actual data processing occurs within the secure environments provided by each national HDAB.

### 21.3 Secure Processing Environments

A key safeguard of the secondary use framework is the requirement that health data accessed under a data permit must be processed within **secure processing environments** managed or approved by the HDAB. Data users may not download or extract individual-level data from these environments; only aggregated results or outputs that do not allow identification of individuals may be exported.

## 22. Opt-Out Rights and Data Protection Safeguards

### 22.1 The Opt-Out Mechanism

The EHDS establishes opt-out rights for natural persons, allowing individuals to refuse the use of their electronic health data for secondary purposes. Key features:

- **Distinct mechanisms:** The opt-out for primary use and the opt-out for secondary use are **distinct and independent**. A natural person may exercise one without the other.
- **Accessible process:** Member States must provide an accessible and easily understandable opt-out mechanism.
- **Effect of opt-out:** Once an individual has opted out, their data must not be shared with data users, even in anonymised form. This is a significant provision that goes beyond the GDPR's approach to anonymised data.
- **Reversibility:** The opt-out is reversible -- individuals may opt back in at any time.

### 22.2 Relationship With the GDPR

The EHDS operates as a **lex specialis** in relation to the General Data Protection Regulation (Regulation (EU) 2016/679). It builds on GDPR principles but introduces sector-specific provisions:

- The legal basis for secondary use of health data under the EHDS is primarily Article 6(1)(e) GDPR (public interest) and Article 9(2)(j) GDPR (scientific research), as further specified by the EHDS itself.
- The EHDS does not create a new legal basis for processing but specifies conditions under which existing GDPR legal bases apply in the health data context.
- National GDPR supervisory authorities cooperate with HDABs on data protection matters.

## 24. Interconnections Between the Three Instruments

The Pharma Package, HTA Regulation, and EHDS are not isolated instruments -- they form a mutually reinforcing framework:

### 24.1 Pharma Package and HTA

- **Evidence requirements:** The revised pharmaceutical legislation's emphasis on faster market authorisation intersects with the HTA Regulation's need for robust clinical evidence at the time of marketing authorisation. Joint scientific consultations under the HTAR should help align evidence generation with both regulatory and HTA requirements.
- **Orphan and paediatric assessment:** The HTA Regulation's Phase 2 (orphan medicines from 2028) directly complements the Pharma Package's reformed orphan framework, as HTA bodies will assess the clinical added value of orphan medicines incentivised under the new system.
- **AMR products:** Novel antimicrobials developed under the TEV incentive will eventually be subject to JCAs, creating an assessment pathway for products where traditional comparative evidence may be limited.

### 24.2 HTA and EHDS

- **Real-world evidence:** The EHDS's secondary use framework will provide HTA bodies with access to real-world data from electronic health records across the EU, enabling more robust post-market assessments of clinical effectiveness.
- **Relative effectiveness studies:** Cross-border health data access through HealthData@EU could facilitate the multi-country comparative effectiveness studies needed for JCAs.

### 24.3 Pharma Package and EHDS

- **Pharmacovigilance:** The EHDS explicitly lists regulatory activities, including pharmacovigilance, as permitted secondary uses of health data, supporting the Pharma Package's strengthened safety monitoring requirements.
- **Shortage monitoring:** Health data on prescribing patterns and medicine utilisation, accessible through the EHDS, could inform the Pharma Package's shortage prevention and monitoring mechanisms.
- **Environmental risk assessment:** Epidemiological data accessible through the EHDS could support post-authorisation environmental risk assessments mandated by the Pharma Package.

## 25. Practical Implications by Sector

### 25.1 Innovative Pharmaceutical Companies

- **Regulatory data protection:** Plan product life cycles around the new 8+1(+1)(+1) formula, ensuring development programmes target the extension criteria (unmet medical needs, additional innovation).
- **JCA readiness:** Prepare for simultaneous submission of clinical data to EMA and the HTA Secretariat. Invest in evidence planning that aligns with both regulatory and HTA requirements from early clinical development.
- **Shortage prevention:** Develop and maintain shortage prevention plans for all prescription medicines; ensure notification systems for anticipated supply disruptions.
- **Health data strategy:** Engage with HDABs and HealthData@EU for real-world evidence generation to support both regulatory and HTA submissions.
- **AMR opportunities:** For companies with antimicrobial pipelines, the TEV creates a potentially valuable commercial instrument, though the blockbuster clause limits its applicability.

### 25.2 Generic and Biosimilar Manufacturers

- **Market entry planning:** The 8+1(+1)(+1) formula provides greater predictability for planning generic and biosimilar launches, though the variable extensions introduce some uncertainty.
- **Bolar exemption:** The harmonised Bolar exemption enables earlier preparatory activities for generic and biosimilar launch across all Member States.
- **JCA utilisation:** Generic and biosimilar manufacturers can reference JCA reports in their own national submissions, potentially reducing evidence generation costs.
- **Supply chain obligations:** Enhanced obligations on continuous supply and shortage prevention also apply to generic manufacturers.

### 25.3 Medical Device and Medtech Companies

- **HTA readiness:** From 2026, high-risk medical devices will be subject to JCAs. Manufacturers should begin preparing clinical evidence packages aligned with JCA methodology.
- **EHDS compliance:** Medical device data is included in the EHDS's secondary use framework, creating both opportunities (access to real-world performance data) and obligations (data availability requirements).
- **EHR interoperability:** Devices that generate electronic health data must ensure compatibility with the European Electronic Health Record Exchange Format.

### 25.4 Hospitals and Healthcare Providers

- **EHR system upgrades:** Healthcare providers will need to ensure their electronic health record systems comply with the EEHRxF and interoperability requirements, potentially requiring significant IT investment.
- **Shortage management:** Enhanced reporting obligations and engagement with EMA/national authorities on medicine shortages.
- **Data holder obligations:** Under the EHDS, healthcare providers are data holders with obligations to make health data available for secondary use (subject to patient opt-out and HDAB governance).

### 25.5 National HTA Bodies

- **Procedural adaptation:** National HTA bodies must integrate JCA reports into their existing assessment processes, adapting procedures to comply with the "duly consider" obligation.
- **Resource reallocation:** As clinical assessment work is partially taken up by JCAs, national bodies may redirect resources towards economic evaluation, budget impact analysis, and other non-clinical domains.
- **EHDS integration:** Access to cross-border health data through HealthData@EU could significantly enhance the evidence base for national pricing and reimbursement decisions.

### 25.6 Health Data Researchers

- **Unprecedented access:** The EHDS creates a legal framework for accessing health data for research across all 27 Member States, potentially transforming the scale and quality of health research in Europe.
- **Secure processing:** Researchers must adapt to working within secure processing environments, without the ability to download individual-level data.
- **Application process:** Researchers must apply to HDABs for data permits, justifying the purpose, scope, and safeguards of their proposed research.
- **Opt-out impact:** Research datasets may be affected by patient opt-out decisions, requiring careful consideration of representativeness and potential bias.

### 25.7 Patient Organisations

- **Access and affordability:** The Pharma Package's incentive structure aims to balance innovation with access, though patient groups will need to monitor whether the new system delivers faster access to needed medicines.
- **HTA participation:** The HTAR mandates patient involvement in JCA processes, creating new opportunities for patient organisations to influence evidence assessment at EU level.
- **Data rights:** The EHDS strengthens patients' rights over their health data, including cross-border portability, but patient organisations should engage in the implementation phase to ensure opt-out mechanisms are genuinely accessible.

---

## 26. Conclusions

The convergence of the Pharma Package, HTA Regulation, and EHDS represents a structural transformation of the EU healthcare regulatory landscape. Several conclusions emerge:

**First**, the EU is moving decisively towards greater centralisation in healthcare regulation, even whilst respecting Member State competence in healthcare delivery, pricing, and reimbursement. The JCA framework, the EHDS's cross-border data infrastructure, and the harmonised pharmaceutical rules all point towards a more unified European health market.

**Second**, the Pharma Package deal of 11 December 2025 reflects a pragmatic compromise. The restoration of eight years of data exclusivity acknowledges the pharmaceutical industry's competitiveness concerns, whilst the incentive-based extensions and TEV mechanism preserve the Commission's original ambition to steer R&D towards areas of greatest unmet need. Whether this compromise is sufficient to maintain Europe's attractiveness as a pharmaceutical R&D location, in the face of aggressive incentive policies in the US and China, remains to be seen.

**Third**, the HTA Regulation's success will depend critically on implementation quality. The phase-in schedule provides time for national systems to adapt, but the risk of parallel national and EU assessment processes, methodological divergence, and capacity constraints must be actively managed. The first year of application (2025) for oncology and ATMPs will provide crucial evidence of the system's viability.

**Fourth**, the EHDS is perhaps the most ambitious of the three instruments, creating an entirely new infrastructure for health data governance across Europe. Its success depends on massive investment in interoperability, IT infrastructure, HDAB capacity, and public trust. The opt-out provisions, whilst necessary for patient confidence, could create challenges for data representativeness if exercised at scale.

**Fifth**, the interconnections between these three instruments create both opportunities and implementation challenges. The potential for real-world evidence from the EHDS to inform HTA assessments and pharmacovigilance is significant, but realising this potential requires coordination across multiple governance structures, IT systems, and stakeholder communities.

For EU policy professionals, these three instruments will define the healthcare advocacy landscape for the decade ahead. The transition from legislation to implementation -- through delegated acts, technical standards, institutional capacity-building, and stakeholder engagement -- is where the practical impact will be determined.
