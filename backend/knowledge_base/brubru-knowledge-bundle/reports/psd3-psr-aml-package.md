**Analysis of the Payment Services Directive 3, Payment Services Regulation, AML Regulation, AMLA, and the 6th Anti-Money Laundering Directive -- Reshaping EU Financial Crime Prevention and Payment Markets**

## Executive Summary

The European Union is simultaneously overhauling two foundational pillars of its financial services framework: payment services regulation and anti-money laundering rules. The PSD3/PSR package and the AML package together represent the most comprehensive reform of EU financial crime prevention and payment market rules in over a decade. While the AML package was adopted in May 2024 and published in the Official Journal on 19 June 2024, the PSD3/PSR package reached a provisional political agreement on 27 November 2025 and is expected to be formally adopted in 2026.

**Key Findings:**

- **PSD3/PSR:** A provisional political agreement was reached on 27 November 2025, restructuring EU payment services law for the first time as a directly applicable regulation (PSR) complemented by a directive (PSD3) for authorisation and supervision. Key innovations include mandatory IBAN/name verification, enhanced fraud liability rules, a consent dashboard for open banking, and the merger of payment institution and electronic money institution licensing regimes.
- **AML Regulation (EU) 2024/1624:** For the first time, AML/CFT rules are set out in a directly applicable EU regulation, harmonising customer due diligence, beneficial ownership, and an EU-wide cash payment limit of EUR 10,000. Applies from 10 July 2027.
- **AMLA (Regulation (EU) 2024/1620):** A new EU authority based in Frankfurt am Main, operational since 1 July 2025, will directly supervise up to 40 highest-risk cross-border financial entities from 1 January 2028 and coordinate national AML supervisors and FIUs. Approximately 430 staff at full capacity.
- **6th AML Directive (EU) 2024/1640:** Replaces AMLD4/AMLD5, strengthening FIU cooperation, beneficial ownership register requirements, and national supervisory frameworks. Transposition deadline: 10 July 2027.
- **Expanded scope:** The AML package brings crypto-asset service providers, professional football clubs and agents, and traders of luxury goods (precious metals, art, luxury vehicles, yachts) within the scope of AML obligations for the first time.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 Context

The European Union's financial services regulatory landscape is undergoing two parallel but interconnected transformations. On one side, the payment services framework -- originally established by the Payment Services Directive (PSD1, 2007) and significantly expanded by PSD2 (2015) -- is being restructured through the PSD3/PSR package to address persistent issues with payment fraud, fragmented open banking implementation, and regulatory arbitrage between Member States. On the other side, the anti-money laundering regime -- built over three decades through successive directives from AMLD1 (1991) to AMLD5 (2018) -- is being fundamentally reformed through a package that introduces, for the first time, a directly applicable AML regulation and a dedicated EU-level supervisory authority.

These reforms share a common legislative strategy: the shift from directives to regulations as the primary instrument for harmonisation. Both the PSR and the AML Regulation represent the EU legislature's recognition that directives alone -- which require national transposition and inevitably produce divergent implementation -- are insufficient for creating a genuine single market in financial services. The choice of regulations as the legal instrument reflects lessons learned from PSD2's uneven implementation and AMLD4/AMLD5's transposition gaps, which allowed criminals and fraudsters to exploit regulatory inconsistencies between Member States.

The interconnection between these two packages is substantive as well as strategic. Payment service providers are obliged entities under AML rules. Open banking infrastructure creates new channels that require AML controls. Fraud prevention measures in the PSR complement the broader financial crime prevention objectives of the AML package. Understanding both packages together is essential for any financial institution, fintech, or policy professional operating in the EU market.

### 1.2 Scope

This report provides a comprehensive analysis of five legislative instruments across two reform packages:

**PSD3/PSR Package (proposed June 2023, trilogue agreement November 2025):**
- Directive on payment services and electronic money services (PSD3) -- COM(2023) 366
- Regulation on payment services in the internal market (PSR) -- COM(2023) 367

**AML Package (adopted May 2024, published June 2024):**
- Anti-Money Laundering Regulation -- Regulation (EU) 2024/1624
- AMLA Regulation -- Regulation (EU) 2024/1620
- 6th Anti-Money Laundering Directive -- Directive (EU) 2024/1640

For each instrument, the report examines the legal framework, key provisions, implementation timeline, enforcement mechanisms, and practical implications. It also analyses the political dynamics that shaped the final texts and identifies areas where implementation challenges are likely to arise.

## 3. The PSD3/PSR Package: Structure and Rationale

### 3.1 Why PSD2 Needed Reform

PSD2 (Directive (EU) 2015/2366) was a landmark regulation when adopted, introducing open banking through account information services (AIS) and payment initiation services (PIS), mandating strong customer authentication (SCA), and establishing a framework for third-party provider (TPP) access to payment accounts. However, its implementation revealed several structural weaknesses that the PSD3/PSR package aims to address.

First, as a directive, PSD2 was transposed differently across 27 Member States, creating a patchwork of national rules that complicated cross-border operations for payment service providers. The European Banking Authority (EBA) documented persistent divergences in how Member States interpreted key provisions, particularly around SCA exemptions, TPP access rights, and the scope of payment account information. Second, PSD2's fraud prevention mechanisms proved insufficient against the rapid evolution of payment fraud, particularly authorised push payment (APP) fraud and social engineering scams that exploit the speed of instant payments. Third, the open banking framework, while conceptually sound, suffered from inconsistent API quality, obstructive practices by account-servicing payment service providers (ASPSPs), and a lack of user-friendly tools for managing data access permissions.

The Commission's evaluation of PSD2, published alongside the legislative proposals in June 2023, confirmed these shortcomings. It found that only 11% of EU consumers had used open banking services, that fraud losses had increased despite SCA, and that the fragmented regulatory landscape continued to impede the creation of a genuine single market in payments.

### 3.2 The Directive-Regulation Split

The Commission's most significant structural decision was to split the payment services framework into a directly applicable regulation (PSR) and a directive (PSD3). This approach mirrors the architecture used in MiFID II/MiFIR and in the AML package itself, separating conduct-of-business rules (which benefit from direct applicability) from authorisation and supervisory arrangements (which require national institutional integration).

The PSR contains all rules governing the relationship between payment service providers and their users: transparency, information requirements, rights and obligations for payment transactions, SCA, fraud prevention, open banking access rules, and surcharging prohibitions. As a regulation, these rules will apply uniformly across all Member States without transposition, eliminating the divergences that plagued PSD2.

PSD3, as a directive, covers the authorisation and supervision of payment institutions and electronic money institutions, including capital requirements, passporting rules, and the institutional framework for national competent authorities. This allows Member States to integrate these requirements into their existing national supervisory structures while ensuring a harmonised baseline.

### 3.3 Merger of Payment and Electronic Money Regimes

One of the most consequential changes in PSD3 is the merger of the payment institution and electronic money institution licensing regimes. Under the previous framework, payment institutions were regulated under PSD2 while electronic money institutions were governed by the Electronic Money Directive 2 (EMD2, Directive 2009/110/EC). This dual-track system created confusion, regulatory arbitrage, and unnecessary compliance burdens for firms offering both payment and e-money services.

PSD3 repeals EMD2 and integrates electronic money services into a single authorisation framework. Firms that were previously licensed as electronic money institutions will be brought under the payment institution regime, with appropriate transitional provisions. This simplification reduces the number of EU-level regulatory frameworks for payment services from three (PSD2, EMD2, and the Settlement Finality Directive) to two, and creates a more coherent supervisory landscape.

## 4. Key Provisions of the PSR

### 4.1 Fraud Prevention and Liability

Fraud prevention is the centrepiece of the PSR. The regulation introduces several mechanisms that go significantly beyond PSD2's approach:

**Verification of Payee (VoP):** The PSR mandates that payment service providers implement IBAN/name matching for all credit transfers, not just euro-denominated SEPA transfers. Before executing a credit transfer, the payer's PSP must request verification from the payee's PSP that the payee's name and IBAN correspond. If a mismatch is detected, the payer must be warned before confirming the transaction. This obligation extends to RTGS (high-value) payments and money remittances terminating at an IBAN, closing a loophole that previously allowed fraudsters to exploit unverified transfers.

**Fraud liability sharing:** Under PSD2, liability for unauthorised transactions fell almost entirely on the payer's PSP. The PSR introduces a more balanced framework. If a PSP fails to implement adequate anti-fraud mechanisms -- including VoP -- it bears liability for the customer's losses. Critically, the provisional agreement extends liability to online platforms: major platforms and search engines may only advertise financial services if the provider is authorised in the relevant Member State, and platforms that fail to remove fraudulent content after being notified can be held liable to PSPs that have reimbursed defrauded customers.

**Refund timelines:** Customers are entitled to refunds within 14 business days of notification for unauthorised transactions, provided the payer is not at fault. This tightened timeline reflects the urgency of fraud remediation in an era of instant payments.

**Fraud data sharing:** PSPs are required to share fraud-related information with each other, creating an industry-wide intelligence network that can identify patterns and prevent repeat offences. This provision addresses the previously siloed approach to fraud data, where individual PSPs could not benefit from the broader ecosystem's collective knowledge.

### 4.2 Strong Customer Authentication (SCA)

The PSR refines and strengthens the SCA framework established by PSD2 while introducing greater flexibility for low-risk transactions:

**Risk-based approach:** SCA must be applied whenever a payer accesses a payment account online or initiates an electronic payment transaction, but PSPs are required to apply exemptions in a manner compatible with the risk level. PSPs must conduct initial fraud risk assessments and maintain up-to-date records of fraud rates.

**Enhanced exemptions:** The Transaction Risk Analysis (TRA) exemption allows PSPs to forego SCA for low-risk transactions, provided their fraud rates remain below specified thresholds. The PSR reinforces this mechanism while making PSPs responsible for fraud losses if their risk engines fail to maintain acceptable fraud rates. Low-value contactless payments under EUR 30 remain exempt, subject to cumulative caps (five consecutive transactions or EUR 100 total).

**Biometric and behavioural elements:** The PSR explicitly recognises that the inherence element of SCA may include environmental and behavioural characteristics, such as location, time of transaction, and device fingerprinting. This expansion reflects technological advances since PSD2 and allows PSPs to use more sophisticated, less intrusive authentication methods.

### 4.3 Open Banking and the Consent Dashboard

The PSR addresses PSD2's open banking shortcomings through several targeted measures:

**Prohibited obstacles:** The regulation explicitly lists practices that account-servicing PSPs may not use to obstruct TPP access, including adding unnecessary friction to authentication journeys, degrading API performance, or requiring additional authorisation steps beyond those imposed on the account holder's own access.

**Consent dashboard:** A mandatory user-facing "permission dashboard" requires ASPSPs to provide customers with a clear interface for monitoring and managing the permissions they have granted to TPPs. This addresses a significant consumer protection gap under PSD2, where many users were unaware of which third parties had ongoing access to their account data.

**Extended scope:** The surcharging prohibition, which under PSD2 applied only to euro-denominated credit transfers and direct debits, is extended to all EU currencies. This ensures that non-eurozone Member States' consumers receive the same protections against hidden fees.

### 4.4 Access to Payment Systems

The PSR strengthens the rights of non-bank PSPs to access payment systems, addressing a longstanding complaint from fintechs and payment institutions. Under PSD2, payment institutions often relied on banks for access to clearing and settlement infrastructure, creating dependency and potential competitive bottlenecks. The PSR provides a clearer legal framework for direct access, while maintaining appropriate safeguards for system stability.

## 5. Key Provisions of PSD3

### 5.1 Unified Authorisation Framework

PSD3 creates a single authorisation regime for payment institutions, replacing the dual-track system of PSD2 and EMD2. Key changes include:

**Calibrated initial capital:** Rather than a one-size-fits-all capital requirement, initial capital is calibrated to the PSP's risk profile and the range of payment services it provides. This approach is more proportionate and reduces barriers to entry for smaller, lower-risk providers.

**Simplified authorisation process:** The directive streamlines the authorisation procedure, reducing the administrative burden while maintaining robust prudential requirements. This reflects the Commission's objective of supporting innovation without compromising financial stability.

**Transitional provisions:** Existing electronic money institutions will be given a defined period to transition to the new unified framework, ensuring continuity of operations and avoiding disruption to the e-money market.

### 5.2 Supervisory Cooperation

PSD3 strengthens cooperation mechanisms between national competent authorities, addressing gaps identified in PSD2's implementation. The directive establishes clearer procedures for information sharing between supervisors, particularly for cross-border payment institutions operating through passporting arrangements. It also clarifies the allocation of supervisory responsibilities between home and host Member States, reducing the "supervisory gaps" that allowed some institutions to operate with insufficient oversight.

### 5.3 Safeguarding Requirements

The directive updates safeguarding requirements for client funds held by payment institutions and electronic money institutions, ensuring that customer funds are protected in the event of the PSP's insolvency. These rules are particularly important given the growth of fintech payment providers that hold significant volumes of client funds without the full prudential framework applicable to banks.

## 6. The AML Regulation: A Single Rulebook

### 6.1 From Directive to Regulation

The adoption of Regulation (EU) 2024/1624 marks the most significant structural change in EU AML/CFT policy since the first directive in 1991. By casting the core AML/CFT obligations as a directly applicable regulation rather than a directive, the EU legislature has eliminated the transposition divergences that allowed money launderers to exploit the weakest national implementation. This "single rulebook" approach mirrors the strategy used in banking regulation (CRR/CRD) and capital markets (MiFIR/MiFID II).

The regulation exhaustively harmonises the obligations that apply to "obliged entities" -- the firms and professionals required to implement AML/CFT controls. These include customer due diligence (CDD) requirements, beneficial ownership identification, record-keeping obligations, suspicious transaction reporting, and internal controls and governance. Where previous directives left significant discretion to Member States in implementing these requirements, the regulation prescribes detailed, uniform rules.

### 6.2 Expanded Scope of Obliged Entities

The AML Regulation significantly broadens the categories of entities subject to AML/CFT obligations:

| Category | Status |
|----------|--------|
| Credit institutions and banks | Retained from AMLD4/5 |
| Insurance undertakings and intermediaries | Retained |
| Payment service providers | Retained |
| Investment firms | Retained |
| Lawyers, notaries, accountants, tax advisors | Retained |
| Real estate agents | Retained |
| **Crypto-asset service providers** | **New** -- all categories treated as financial institutions |
| **Crowdfunding service providers** | **New** |
| **Traders of luxury goods** (precious metals, stones, jewellery, luxury cars, aircraft, yachts, artworks) | **New** |
| **Professional football clubs and agents** | **New** (applies from 10 July 2029; Member State flexibility for low-risk clubs) |

The inclusion of crypto-asset service providers reflects the parallel development of MiCA (Regulation (EU) 2023/1114) and ensures that the crypto sector is subject to the same AML obligations as traditional financial institutions. The inclusion of professional football clubs and agents, while politically controversial, reflects documented money laundering risks in the football transfer market, including opaque agent fee structures and complex multi-jurisdictional transactions.

### 6.3 Customer Due Diligence

The regulation harmonises CDD requirements with greater granularity than previous directives:

**Standard CDD threshold:** Obliged entities carrying out occasional transactions exceeding EUR 10,000 must conduct CDD. This represents a reduction from the EUR 15,000 threshold under AMLD4. For occasional cash transactions of at least EUR 3,000, limited CDD measures are required.

**Enhanced CDD:** Obliged entities must apply enhanced due diligence in situations presenting higher money laundering or terrorist financing risk, including cross-border correspondent banking relationships, transactions involving high-risk third countries, and relationships with politically exposed persons (PEPs).

**Beneficial ownership identification:** The regulation refines the definition of beneficial ownership, clarifying the framework for identifying individuals who ultimately own or control legal entities. The ownership threshold remains at 25% or more of shares, voting rights, or other ownership interests in a corporate entity. Where no natural person is identified as the beneficial owner, senior management must be identified.

### 6.4 Cash Payment Limit

For the first time, the EU imposes a harmonised limit on cash payments: EUR 10,000 or the equivalent in national currency. This limit applies to any cash payment for goods or services, whether completed as a single operation or through linked transactions. Member States retain the discretion to apply lower thresholds but may not exceed the EU-level ceiling. The cash limit is one of the regulation's most visible measures and addresses the significant money laundering risks associated with large cash transactions, particularly in real estate, luxury goods, and professional services.

## 7. AMLA: The Anti-Money Laundering Authority

### 7.1 Establishment and Mandate

Regulation (EU) 2024/1620 establishes the Authority for Anti-Money Laundering and Countering the Financing of Terrorism (AMLA) as a decentralised EU agency based in Frankfurt am Main, Germany. The co-legislators' joint decision on the seat location was unprecedented -- this was the first time that the European Parliament and the Council of the EU jointly agreed on the location of a new decentralised agency.

AMLA's mandate comprises three core functions: direct supervision of the highest-risk financial sector entities, coordination and oversight of national AML supervisors across the non-financial sector, and support for Financial Intelligence Unit (FIU) cooperation. This three-pillar mandate is designed to address the systemic weaknesses identified in the EU's AML/CFT framework, particularly the uneven quality of national supervision and the inadequate coordination between FIUs in cross-border cases.

### 7.2 Direct Supervision

AMLA's most significant power is the direct supervision of selected obliged entities in the financial sector. This direct supervisory mandate will apply to up to 40 financial entities that meet specific criteria:

- **High risk:** The entity is assessed as presenting a high risk of money laundering or terrorist financing based on its inherent risk profile and the quality of its internal controls.
- **Cross-border operations:** The entity operates on a cross-border basis in at least six Member States, reflecting the focus on entities whose risk profile transcends national supervisory capacity.

Direct supervision will be conducted through joint supervisory teams composed of AMLA staff and personnel from the relevant national competent authorities. Over 200 of AMLA's projected 430 staff members will work on direct supervision. AMLA will have the power to conduct on-site inspections, require the production of documents and information, interview staff, and ultimately impose supervisory measures on non-compliant entities.

### 7.3 Indirect Supervision and Coordination

Beyond the 40 or so directly supervised entities, AMLA will exercise indirect supervision over the broader financial sector through its coordination of national AML supervisors. This includes:

- Developing regulatory technical standards (RTS) and implementing technical standards (ITS) under the AML Regulation
- Conducting peer reviews of national supervisory authorities
- Issuing guidelines and recommendations
- Mediating disagreements between national supervisors in cross-border cases
- Intervening directly as a last resort where national supervision is assessed as inadequate

### 7.4 FIU Support Mechanism

AMLA will provide operational support to FIUs across the EU, including facilitating joint analyses in cross-border cases, managing the upgraded FIU.net system for secure information exchange, and setting deadlines for responses to cross-border information requests. This coordination role is intended to address the documented delays and inconsistencies in FIU cooperation that have hampered cross-border money laundering investigations.

## 8. The 6th Anti-Money Laundering Directive

### 8.1 Scope and Purpose

Directive (EU) 2024/1640, known as the 6th Anti-Money Laundering Directive (AMLD6), complements the AML Regulation by addressing matters that require national transposition: the institutional framework for national AML supervisors, the powers and cooperation mechanisms of Financial Intelligence Units, and the establishment and management of beneficial ownership registers. AMLD6 repeals and replaces both AMLD4 (Directive (EU) 2015/849) and AMLD5 (Directive (EU) 2018/843).

### 8.2 National Supervisory Framework

AMLD6 strengthens requirements for national AML supervisory authorities, ensuring they have adequate powers, resources, and independence. The directive establishes minimum standards for supervisory powers, including the ability to conduct inspections, require information production, and impose administrative sanctions. It also clarifies the relationship between national supervisors and AMLA, establishing clear channels for cooperation and information sharing.

### 8.3 Financial Intelligence Units

The directive significantly enhances the framework for FIU cooperation:

**Response deadlines:** AMLD6 establishes concrete deadlines for FIUs to respond to cross-border information requests, addressing the chronic delays that have undermined the effectiveness of the EU's FIU network.

**FIU.net upgrade:** The directive provides a clear legal basis for the functioning and upgrading of the FIU.net system, the EU's secure communication network for FIU-to-FIU information exchange. AMLA will manage the upgraded system.

**Joint analyses:** AMLD6 enables joint analyses between FIUs in cross-border cases, providing the legal framework for AMLA's operational support role. This addresses a key weakness identified in the Commission's 2019 assessment, where cross-border money laundering investigations were hampered by the inability of FIUs to conduct joint analysis.

### 8.4 Beneficial Ownership Registers

The directive imposes more granular requirements on Member States' central beneficial ownership registers:

**Scope:** Registers must cover beneficial ownership information for legal entities (companies, partnerships) and legal arrangements (trusts, foundations). The information must be adequate, accurate, and current.

**Access:** AMLD6 establishes a tiered access framework. Competent authorities, FIUs, and obliged entities have full access. Members of the public with a "legitimate interest" -- defined to include journalists, civil society organisations, competent authorities, and supervisory bodies -- also have access. This framework reflects the European Court of Justice's November 2022 ruling (Cases C-37/20 and C-601/20) that struck down AMLD5's provision for unrestricted public access, requiring a legitimate interest test.

**Early transposition deadline:** The provisions on beneficial ownership registers had an accelerated transposition deadline of 10 July 2025, reflecting the urgency of restoring public access to beneficial ownership information following the ECJ ruling. Several Member States have already missed this deadline.

### 8.5 Real Estate Registers

AMLD6 introduces a new requirement for Member States to establish centralised registers providing access to information relevant to the identification of owners of real estate. This provision, with a later transposition deadline of 10 July 2029, addresses the opacity of real estate markets that has been repeatedly identified as a key vulnerability for money laundering.

## 10. Enforcement and Penalties

### 10.1 PSR Enforcement

The PSR, as a regulation, will be directly enforceable by national competent authorities. While the final text is not yet published, the provisional agreement is expected to include powers for national supervisors to impose administrative sanctions and remedial measures on PSPs that fail to comply with fraud prevention, SCA, open banking, and consumer protection obligations. The liability framework for fraud -- particularly the extension of liability to online platforms -- represents a novel enforcement mechanism that operates through civil law rather than administrative sanctions.

### 10.2 AML Regulation Enforcement

The AML Regulation provides for significant administrative sanctions. National competent authorities must have the power to impose:

- Administrative fines of at least twice the amount of the benefit derived from the breach, or at least EUR 1 million (EUR 2 million for credit and financial institutions)
- Temporary bans on exercising management functions
- Orders to cease and desist from the conduct constituting the breach
- Public statements identifying the entity and the nature of the breach

### 10.3 AMLA Enforcement Powers

AMLA has direct enforcement powers over the entities it supervises:

- Periodic penalty payments for non-compliance with supervisory requirements
- Power to require specific remedial action
- Power to restrict or limit the business activities of supervised entities
- Administrative pecuniary sanctions for breaches of directly applicable AML requirements

The combination of AMLA's direct supervisory and enforcement powers with the national enforcement framework creates a dual-layer enforcement architecture comparable to the ECB's role under the Single Supervisory Mechanism for banking supervision.

## 11. Practical Implications for the Financial Sector

### 11.1 For Banks and Traditional Financial Institutions

Banks face compliance obligations under both packages simultaneously. On the payment services side, the PSR mandates investment in Verification of Payee infrastructure, enhanced fraud monitoring systems, open banking consent dashboards, and updated SCA mechanisms. On the AML side, the directly applicable regulation eliminates national implementation discretion, meaning banks must review their entire AML/CFT framework against the regulation's uniform requirements by 10 July 2027. Banks operating in six or more Member States must additionally prepare for the possibility of AMLA direct supervision from 1 January 2028, including readiness for joint supervisory team inspections and the more intensive supervisory engagement that direct AMLA oversight entails.

The convergence of compliance deadlines -- PSR application and AML Regulation application both expected in 2027-2028 -- creates a significant "compliance cliff" that requires integrated programme management. Banks that treat these as separate compliance exercises risk duplicating effort and missing synergies, particularly in areas where payment fraud prevention and AML controls overlap.

### 11.2 For Payment Institutions and Fintechs

The PSD3/PSR package is particularly consequential for payment institutions and fintech firms. The merger of the payment institution and electronic money institution regimes requires current EMI licence holders to transition to the new unified framework. The calibrated capital requirements may benefit smaller, lower-risk providers while potentially increasing requirements for larger, higher-risk firms.

The PSR's fraud liability provisions fundamentally change the economics of payment processing. PSPs that fail to implement VoP or adequate anti-fraud mechanisms face direct financial exposure for customer losses, creating a strong incentive for investment in fraud prevention technology. Conversely, the extended surcharging prohibition and enhanced open banking access rights create commercial opportunities for innovative payment providers.

Fintechs should also note that the AML Regulation's expansion to crypto-asset service providers means that firms operating across payment services and crypto will face AML obligations on both sides of their business, requiring integrated compliance frameworks.

### 11.3 For Crypto-Asset Service Providers

The inclusion of all crypto-asset service providers as obliged entities under the AML Regulation, treated as financial institutions with full CDD, record-keeping, and suspicious transaction reporting obligations, represents a step change in compliance requirements for the crypto sector. Combined with MiCA's authorisation requirements, this means that crypto firms operating in the EU will be subject to a regulatory framework comparable in stringency to traditional financial services. Firms must implement full CDD procedures, conduct ongoing monitoring of business relationships, file suspicious transaction reports, and maintain internal AML/CFT governance structures -- all by 10 July 2027.

### 11.4 For Professional Services and Non-Financial Obliged Entities

Lawyers, notaries, accountants, tax advisors, and real estate agents remain subject to AML obligations, but the shift from directive to regulation changes the practical compliance landscape. These professionals will need to apply uniform EU-level rules rather than national transpositions, which may require adjustments to existing compliance procedures. The addition of luxury goods traders, professional football clubs, and football agents creates entirely new compliance populations that may lack AML expertise and infrastructure.

### 11.5 For Technology and RegTech Providers

Both packages create significant market opportunities for technology providers. The PSR's VoP mandate, enhanced SCA requirements, and consent dashboard obligation will drive demand for payment authentication, identity verification, and API management solutions. The AML package's uniform rulebook will create demand for harmonised compliance solutions that work identically across all Member States, favouring RegTech providers that can offer pan-European platforms over national-specific tools.

## 12. Political Dynamics and Negotiation History

### 12.1 PSD3/PSR: The Road to Trilogue

The Commission published its PSD3/PSR proposals on 28 June 2023 as part of a broader financial services reform package. In the European Parliament, the ECON Committee appointed Marek Belka (S&D, Poland) as rapporteur for the PSR and Ondrej Kovarik (Renew, Czechia) as rapporteur for PSD3. The Parliament adopted its first reading position in April 2024, introducing several amendments that strengthened fraud protection provisions and expanded consumer rights.

The Council's general approach, adopted in November 2024, diverged from the Parliament on several key points, including the scope of online platform liability, the specifics of the consent dashboard, and the treatment of SCA exemptions. The trilogue negotiations, conducted throughout 2025, focused on resolving these differences. The provisional political agreement of 27 November 2025 represented compromises on all major outstanding issues, with the Parliament largely prevailing on fraud liability and the Council securing its preferred approach on certain authorisation requirements.

Industry stakeholders were broadly supportive of the reform's objectives but raised concerns about implementation complexity, particularly the VoP mandate and the compressed timeline for compliance. The European Payments Council, representing payment service providers, emphasised the need for adequate transition periods and clear technical standards.

### 12.2 AML Package: A Three-Year Negotiation

The AML package had a longer and more contentious legislative history. Proposed by the Commission in July 2021, the package faced significant debate on several fronts. The European Parliament pushed for broader scope, stronger enforcement, and greater transparency, while the Council -- reflecting Member States' varied national AML frameworks -- sought to preserve national discretion where possible.

The most politically contentious issues included the cash payment limit (with some Member States opposing any EU-level cap), the scope of beneficial ownership register access (following the ECJ's 2022 ruling), the seat location of AMLA (with multiple cities competing), and the inclusion of professional football clubs (which attracted intense lobbying from the football industry). The political agreement reached on 18 January 2024 was followed by formal adoption on 30 May 2024, with the package published in the Official Journal on 19 June 2024.

The selection of Frankfurt am Main as AMLA's seat was a significant political decision, reflecting Germany's role as the EU's largest financial market and the existing AML supervisory infrastructure centred around BaFin and the Bundesbank. The decision was also influenced by Frankfurt's established ecosystem of EU financial regulatory agencies, including the ECB and EIOPA.

### 12.3 Interconnections Between the Two Packages

While the PSD3/PSR package and the AML package were negotiated separately and on different timelines, the interconnections between them are substantive. Payment service providers are obliged entities under the AML Regulation. The PSR's fraud prevention measures complement the AML Regulation's suspicious transaction reporting obligations. Open banking data flows raise AML/CFT considerations around data sharing and customer screening. The convergence of application dates (both packages becoming applicable in 2027-2028) means that the financial sector will be implementing both simultaneously, requiring coordinated compliance strategies.

## 13. Conclusions and Recommendations

### 13.1 Assessment

The PSD3/PSR package and the AML package together represent the EU's most ambitious reform of financial services regulation since the post-2008 regulatory overhaul. The shift from directives to regulations as the primary legislative instrument reflects a mature recognition that harmonisation through minimum standards has reached its limits. The creation of AMLA as a dedicated EU-level supervisor addresses a structural gap that has undermined AML/CFT effectiveness for decades.

The PSD3/PSR package's focus on fraud prevention, VoP, and open banking improvement is well-calibrated to the most pressing challenges in the payment services market. The AML package's single rulebook approach, combined with AMLA's direct supervisory powers, creates a framework that is, in principle, capable of addressing the EU's documented weaknesses in money laundering prevention.

### 13.2 Implementation Challenges

The primary risk to both packages lies in implementation. The compressed timeline -- with both packages becoming applicable within a similar 2027-2028 window -- creates a significant compliance burden for the financial sector, particularly for smaller institutions with limited compliance resources. AMLA's ambitious staffing plan (from 120 in 2025 to 430 by 2027) and the establishment of direct supervision over 40 entities represent an unprecedented organisational build-up that will test the EU's capacity for institutional development.

For the PSD3/PSR package, the technical standards that will flesh out the regulation's high-level requirements have not yet been developed, and their timely publication will be critical for giving the industry adequate preparation time. The VoP mandate in particular requires significant infrastructure investment across the entire payment ecosystem.

### 13.3 Recommendations for Stakeholders

**For payment service providers:** Begin impact assessment now for both the PSR and AML Regulation. Map overlapping obligations, particularly around fraud prevention and suspicious transaction reporting. Invest early in VoP infrastructure and consent dashboard development. Monitor the development of technical standards under the PSR.

**For banks operating cross-border:** Assess exposure to AMLA direct supervision by reviewing the selection criteria. Ensure that AML/CFT frameworks are aligned with the regulation's uniform requirements, not merely with the most lenient national transposition of AMLD4/AMLD5.

**For crypto-asset service providers:** Treat the AML Regulation's application date (10 July 2027) as a firm compliance deadline. Build AML/CFT frameworks that integrate with MiCA compliance obligations.

**For policy professionals:** Monitor the technical trilogue for PSD3/PSR final text publication, expected summer 2026. Track AMLA's early activities and the first round of entity selection for direct supervision. Watch for Member State transposition of AMLD6, particularly the beneficial ownership register provisions.
