The Markets in Crypto-Assets Regulation

Regulation (EU) 2023/1114 (MiCA) is the EU's first comprehensive framework for crypto-assets outside existing financial-services law, covering issuers, service providers and market abuse across three asset types and ten licensed services.

Overview

From legal grey zone to harmonised single market

Why MiCA exists

Before MiCA, most crypto-assets in the EU sat in a legal grey zone. Exchanges, wallet providers and token issuers were largely unregulated except for anti-money-laundering checks, while some Member States had introduced their own national frameworks, creating fragmentation across the single market. MiCA fills that gap with a single harmonised regulation, built on Article 114 TFEU (internal market approximation), the same legal basis as MiFID II and GDPR.

The organising principle is "same activities, same risks, same rules": an entity providing crypto-asset services faces the same authorisation, capital and conduct requirements as comparable financial-services firms. This principle extends to technology neutrality: the regulation applies to any distributed ledger technology or similar technology without singling out particular protocols.

Environmental disclosure and sustainability

Recital 7 requires white papers to disclose the environmental impact of the consensus mechanism used by the crypto-asset (for example whether it uses proof-of-work or proof-of-stake). ESMA was tasked with developing technical standards on the content of this disclosure. This requirement reflects the EU's broader push to align financial regulation with sustainability objectives, though MiCA does not restrict particular consensus mechanisms.

What MiCA protects

The four objectives named in the regulation are: protecting retail holders of crypto-assets; safeguarding market integrity; ensuring financial stability (particularly against systemic risks from large stablecoins); and preserving monetary sovereignty (by ensuring that widely-used stablecoins do not undermine currencies). Recital 16 also connects MiCA to the international fight against money laundering, through the transfer-of-funds regulation and the sixth anti-money-laundering directive.

Nine Titles, one framework

MiCA is structured in nine Titles: Title I (subject matter, scope and definitions); Title II (other crypto-assets and the white paper); Title III (asset-referenced tokens); Title IV (e-money tokens); Title V (crypto-asset service provider authorisation and operating conditions); Title VI (prevention and prohibition of market abuse); Title VII (competent authorities, EBA and ESMA); Title VIII (delegated acts); and Title IX (transitional and final provisions, including amendments to four other instruments).

What it covers

Five thematic pillars of Regulation (EU) 2023/1114

White paper and disclosure
Offerors of Title II crypto-assets must publish a standardised white paper with mandatory disclosures: issuer, project, offer, rights, technology, risks and environmental impact. Civil liability attaches. Retail holders get 14 days to withdraw.
Asset-referenced tokens (ARTs)
Stablecoins referencing a basket or non-currency asset. Issuers need authorisation, own funds, a segregated low-risk reserve, a permanent right of redemption and no interest. EBA supervises the significant ones directly.
E-money tokens (EMTs)
Currency-pegged stablecoins (one official currency). Issuable only by authorised credit institutions or e-money institutions. Redeemable at par at any time. No interest permitted. Supervised under the e-money framework, with EBA for significant EMTs.
CASP authorisation and 10 services
Crypto-asset service providers must be authorised, hold capital, safeguard client assets and treat clients fairly. One licence is valid Union-wide. Ten services defined, from custody and trading platforms to advice and portfolio management.
Market abuse and supervision
Insider dealing, unlawful disclosure and market manipulation are prohibited. National authorities supervise most issuers and CASPs; EBA oversees significant tokens; ESMA maintains the public register and issues technical standards.

White-paper exemption thresholds

  • Offers to fewer than 150 persons per Member State
  • Offers to qualified investors only
  • Total consideration not exceeding EUR 1 000 000 over 12 months
  • Also exempt: free crypto-assets, mining rewards, limited-network utility tokens
  • ART authorisation not required below EUR 5 000 000 or for qualified-investor-only offers

Significant-token and qualifying-holding thresholds

  • Retail right of withdrawal: 14 days (Title II)
  • Qualifying holding triggering notification: 10% of capital or voting rights
  • ART "used widely as means of exchange": above 1 million transactions AND EUR 200 000 000 per day in a single currency area, triggers duty to reduce activity
  • Significance thresholds for EBA direct supervision: large customer base, high market capitalisation or large transaction volumes

Scope and exclusions

Articles 1 to 2: what falls inside and outside MiCA

What MiCA covers
  • Persons engaged in the issuance, offer to the public or admission to trading of crypto-assets in the Union
  • Persons providing crypto-asset services (CASPs) in the Union
  • All three categories: e-money tokens, asset-referenced tokens and other crypto-assets including utility tokens
  • Algorithmic stablecoins are treated as ARTs or EMTs if they aim to stabilise value
What MiCA excludes (Article 2)
  • Crypto-assets that qualify as financial instruments under MiFID II (regulated separately)
  • Crypto-assets that qualify as deposits, funds, securitisation positions, insurance or pension products
  • Crypto-assets that are unique and non-fungible (NFTs such as digital art and collectibles)
  • Central bank digital money and crypto-assets issued by public authorities acting as monetary authorities
  • Fully decentralised services provided without any intermediary
  • Non-transferable digital assets such as closed-loop loyalty points

ESMA was mandated to issue guidelines on the crypto-asset / financial-instrument boundary by 30 December 2024 (Article 2(5)).

The three types of crypto-asset

Article 3 and recital 18: MiCA's core classification by stabilisation method

The classification logic

MiCA classifies crypto-assets by whether and how they seek to stabilise their value. The classification determines which Title of MiCA applies and therefore the applicable issuer regime. A token must be assessed against its actual design and purpose, not its label.

E-money tokens (Title IV)

An EMT references the value of one official currency (for example a euro-pegged or dollar-pegged stablecoin). It functions like electronic money. Only authorised credit institutions or electronic money institutions may issue EMTs. Holders have a permanent right of redemption at par. No interest may be paid on EMT holdings.

Asset-referenced tokens (Title III)

An ART references another value, right or basket, including one or more currencies, commodities or crypto-assets. This is the catch-all stablecoin category. Issuers must have a registered office in the Union, obtain authorisation and white-paper approval, hold own funds, maintain a segregated low-risk reserve of assets in custody, offer a permanent right of redemption, and may not pay interest.

Other crypto-assets including utility tokens (Title II)

Everything that is not an EMT or ART, including so-called utility tokens that give access to a good or service supplied by the issuer, and tokens with no stabilisation mechanism (such as Bitcoin or Ether in their original form). These are regulated primarily through the white-paper disclosure regime, with lighter requirements than for stablecoins.

The crypto-asset white paper

Title II and recitals 24 to 39: the mandatory disclosure document

What the white paper must contain

Offerors and persons seeking admission to trading of Title II crypto-assets (and ART/EMT issuers as part of authorisation) must draw up, notify to their competent authority and publish a crypto-asset white paper. Mandatory content includes: the identity of the issuer, the project and offer, the rights and obligations attached to the crypto-asset, the underlying technology and its consensus mechanism, the principal risks, and the environmental impact of the consensus mechanism. All information must be fair, clear and not misleading.

Supervision of Title II white papers

Competent authorities do not pre-approve Title II white papers but may require amendments, suspend or prohibit an offer where the white paper is incomplete, misleading or where there is a risk to investor protection or market integrity. For ARTs, the white paper is approved as part of the authorisation process, giving the competent authority a stronger prior-review role.

Exemptions from the white-paper obligation
  • Offers to fewer than 150 persons per Member State
  • Offers to qualified investors only
  • Total consideration not exceeding EUR 1 000 000 over any 12-month period
  • Free crypto-asset distributions (airdrops) where no payment is required
  • Crypto-assets created as mining or validation rewards
  • Utility tokens for services that already exist at the time of issue, if exchangeable only within a limited network of service providers
Retail right of withdrawal and civil liability

Retail holders who purchase Title II crypto-assets directly from the offeror have a 14-day right of withdrawal without incurring any fee. Civil liability attaches to the white paper: where information is incomplete, unfair or misleading, any natural or legal person responsible for the white paper is liable to holders of the crypto-asset for losses suffered.

Asset-referenced and e-money tokens

Titles III and IV: the stablecoin regimes

ART issuer requirements (Title III)
  • Registered office in the Union
  • Authorisation from the competent authority, with consultation of EBA, ESMA and the ECB (or relevant national central bank for non-euro currencies); the central bank's opinion can be binding on financial-stability grounds
  • Approved crypto-asset white paper
  • Own funds proportionate to the value of assets referenced
  • A reserve of assets that is segregated, low-risk, held in custody by a credit institution or crypto-asset service provider, and matches outstanding liabilities
  • Permanent right of redemption at any time at market value (or net asset value) for holders
  • No interest may be paid on ART holdings
  • Recovery and redemption plans for orderly wind-down
  • Authorisation not required below EUR 5 000 000 or for qualified-investor-only offers
EMT issuer requirements (Title IV)
  • Issuable only by an authorised credit institution or electronic money institution
  • Holders have a claim against the issuer and a permanent right of redemption at par (face value of the referenced currency) at any time
  • No interest may be paid on EMT holdings
  • Supervised under the existing e-money framework, with additional MiCA obligations layered on top
  • If the EMT references a currency other than the euro, the relevant national central bank is consulted and may render a binding opinion on financial-stability grounds

Significant tokens

Recitals 59 to 62 and 102 to 107: EBA direct supervision and systemic safeguards

What makes a token significant

ARTs and EMTs are designated significant when they meet certain thresholds, including a large customer base, high market capitalisation or a large number or value of transactions. The European Banking Authority and ESMA may jointly propose a significance designation. Significant tokens trigger a stricter requirements layer: higher own funds, mandatory interoperability measures, enhanced liquidity management policies, and a stricter remuneration policy.

EBA direct supervision and college of supervisors

EBA directly supervises issuers of significant ARTs and significant EMTs (not the national competent authority), with powers including on-site inspections, supervisory measures and periodic penalty payments. For each significant ART issuer, EBA establishes a college of supervisors comprising the relevant competent authorities from Member States where the ART is offered, the competent authority of any trading platform on which it is admitted, and other relevant supervisors. The college facilitates information exchange and coordinated action.

The "used widely as means of exchange" cap

Where an ART is used widely as a means of exchange within a single currency area, reaching more than 1 million transactions and EUR 200 000 000 per day, the issuer is required to reduce activity (Article 23). This provision protects monetary sovereignty by preventing a private stablecoin from displacing the official currency in daily transactions at scale. The issuer must submit a plan for reducing activity; continued non-compliance may lead to authorisation withdrawal.

Crypto-asset service provider (CASP) licensing

Title V and Article 3(1)(16): the ten services, authorisation and passporting

The ten crypto-asset services

Article 3(1)(16) defines ten crypto-asset services for which authorisation is required:

  1. (a) Custody and administration of crypto-assets on behalf of clients
  2. (b) Operation of a trading platform for crypto-assets
  3. (c) Exchange of crypto-assets for funds (fiat)
  4. (d) Exchange of crypto-assets for other crypto-assets
  5. (e) Execution of orders for crypto-assets on behalf of clients
  6. (f) Placing of crypto-assets
  7. (g) Reception and transmission of orders for crypto-assets on behalf of clients
  8. (h) Providing advice on crypto-assets
  9. (i) Providing portfolio management on crypto-assets
  10. (j) Providing transfer services for crypto-assets on behalf of clients
Authorisation requirements

A CASP must have its registered office and place of effective management in the Union, with at least one director resident in the Union. It must meet prudential own funds requirements, safeguard client crypto-assets and funds (segregated from own assets), act honestly and professionally in clients' best interests, adopt conflicts-of-interest and complaints procedures, and meet ICT security and business-continuity requirements. Custodians face strict liability for loss of client crypto-assets held in custody.

Passporting and fast-track for regulated firms

A single CASP authorisation is valid Union-wide (passporting): a firm authorised in one Member State may provide services across all other Member States by notification. Certain already-authorised financial firms (credit institutions, investment firms, UCITS management companies, AIFMs, payment institutions, e-money institutions) may provide crypto-asset services by notification to their existing competent authority rather than a fresh authorisation, provided the crypto-asset service is comparable to a service they already hold a licence for.

Market abuse

Title VI: insider dealing, market manipulation and disclosure obligations

The three prohibited behaviours

Title VI of MiCA prohibits three types of market abuse for crypto-assets admitted to trading on a trading platform:

  • Insider dealing: acquiring or disposing of crypto-assets using inside information (price-sensitive non-public information)
  • Unlawful disclosure of inside information: sharing inside information with third parties other than in the normal exercise of a function
  • Market manipulation: transactions, orders or other behaviour that give false or misleading signals as to supply, demand or price, or that fix prices at an artificial level

The regime is designed as a bespoke, lighter version of the Market Abuse Regulation (596/2014), acknowledging the prevalence of SMEs and retail participants in crypto markets while ensuring consistent investor protection.

Market-soundings and disclosure

CASPs operating trading platforms must establish and maintain effective systems to detect and report suspicious transactions. Issuers of crypto-assets admitted to trading must disclose inside information to the public as soon as possible, and may delay disclosure only in limited circumstances (to protect their legitimate interests, provided the delay is not likely to mislead the public and the issuer can keep the information confidential).

Competent authorities, EBA and ESMA

Title VII: the three-tier supervisory architecture

National competent authorities

Each Member State designates one or more national competent authorities (NCAs) responsible for authorising and supervising: issuers of Title II crypto-assets; issuers of non-significant ARTs; and CASPs. NCAs have investigatory powers (information requests, on-site inspections), supervisory powers (withdrawal of authorisation, activity suspension, public warnings) and penalty powers.

EBA: direct supervision of significant tokens

EBA directly supervises issuers of significant ARTs and significant EMTs, with on-site inspection powers, supervisory measures and the ability to impose periodic penalty payments and fines. EBA sets supervisory fees for significant-token issuers. A college of supervisors is established for each significant ART issuer, drawing in NCAs from affected Member States.

ESMA: register and technical standards

ESMA maintains a public register of white papers, ART and EMT issuers, and authorised CASPs. ESMA issues binding technical standards (regulatory and implementing) on white-paper content and format, supervisory convergence, market-abuse reporting, and the crypto-asset / financial-instrument boundary guidelines. ESMA also produces an annual report on market developments.

Transitional provisions and amendments

Articles 143 to 149: grandfathering, amendments and the CASP window

CASP grandfathering

CASPs operating lawfully under national law before 30 December 2024 (the general application date) may continue to provide those services until 1 July 2026 or until they are authorised or refused under MiCA, whichever is earlier. Member States were permitted to shorten or disapply this transitional window. This gave existing national-regulated exchanges, custodians and other crypto-service firms an 18-month runway to obtain MiCA authorisation.

ART grandfathering

ART issuers operating under national law before 30 June 2024 (the stablecoin application date) may continue their activities if they submitted an application for MiCA authorisation before 30 July 2024. They may continue while the application is pending, until it is granted or refused. This allowed incumbents in markets where national stablecoin frameworks already existed to seek MiCA authorisation without an immediate shutdown.

Four instruments amended by MiCA (Articles 144 to 147)

MiCA amends four existing EU instruments to integrate crypto-assets into the broader regulatory framework:

  • Regulation (EU) 1093/2010 (EBA founding regulation): crypto-asset supervision tasks added to EBA's remit
  • Regulation (EU) 1095/2010 (ESMA founding regulation): crypto-asset supervisory convergence tasks added to ESMA's remit
  • Directive 2013/36/EU (Capital Requirements Directive, CRD): EMT and ART issuance and crypto-asset service provision added to Annex I (list of activities subject to mutual recognition), enabling credit institutions to passport crypto-asset activities across the EU
  • Directive (EU) 2019/1937 (Whistleblower Directive): MiCA added to the list of Union acts covered, so that whistleblowers reporting MiCA violations benefit from the Directive's protection against retaliation

Key definitions

Essential terms from Article 3 of Regulation (EU) 2023/1114

Crypto-asset
A digital representation of a value or of a right that is able to be transferred and stored electronically, using distributed ledger technology or similar technology (Article 3(1)(5)). This is the overarching category that MiCA sub-divides into EMTs, ARTs and others.
Asset-referenced token (ART)
A crypto-asset that is not an e-money token and that purports to maintain a stable value by referencing another value or right, or a combination thereof, including one or more official currencies (Article 3(1)(6)). The catch-all stablecoin category.
E-money token (EMT)
A type of crypto-asset that purports to maintain a stable value by referencing the value of one official currency (Article 3(1)(7)). Functional equivalent of electronic money in the crypto space. Redeemable at par; no interest.
Utility token
A type of crypto-asset that is only intended to provide access to a good or a service supplied by its issuer (Article 3(1)(9)). Regulated under Title II; lighter regime than ARTs or EMTs provided the service already exists or will exist.
Crypto-asset service provider (CASP)
A legal person or other undertaking whose occupation or business is the provision of one or more crypto-asset services to clients on a professional basis, and which is authorised under MiCA (Article 3(1)(15)). Must be Union-registered; single authorisation valid EU-wide.
Crypto-asset white paper
A document containing mandatory information about the issuer, the crypto-asset project, the offer or admission to trading, the rights and obligations attached, the underlying technology and risks, and the environmental impact of the consensus mechanism (Article 6). Civil liability attaches.
Significant token
An ART or EMT designated as significant by EBA (with ESMA input) because it meets thresholds related to customer base, market capitalisation or transaction volumes. Triggers EBA direct supervision, a college of supervisors and enhanced own-funds and liquidity requirements.

Legislative context

MiCA in the broader EU financial-regulation ecosystem

Relationship to existing financial-services law

MiCA occupies the regulatory space left by existing financial-services regulation. Crypto-assets that qualify as financial instruments under MiFID II are already regulated by that framework and are explicitly excluded from MiCA's scope. The same applies to assets regulated as deposits under the Banking Consolidation Directive or e-money under the E-Money Directive (except where re-characterised as EMTs). MiCA therefore acts as a residual catch-all for the crypto-asset space that existing sectoral law does not reach.

DLT Pilot Regime and DORA

MiCA sits alongside two other digital-finance instruments adopted as part of the EU Digital Finance Package. The DLT Pilot Regime (Regulation (EU) 2022/858) creates a temporary sandbox for market infrastructures (multilateral trading facilities and securities settlement systems) that use distributed ledger technology to trade and settle financial instruments, operating under relaxed MiFID II and CSDR requirements. DORA (Regulation (EU) 2022/2554, the Digital Operational Resilience Act) imposes ICT risk-management requirements on all financial entities including CASPs authorised under MiCA.

AML and transfer-of-funds regulation

MiCA does not itself impose anti-money-laundering requirements. Those obligations are applied through the Transfer of Funds Regulation (as amended by Regulation (EU) 2023/1113 to extend the travel rule to crypto-assets) and the AML/CFT directives. MiCA authorisation does not substitute for AML/CFT obligations; CASPs must comply with both regimes in parallel.

ESAs Review and the supervisory toolkit

MiCA extends the supervisory convergence toolkit that the ESAs Review (Regulation (EU) 2019/2175, already in the Brubru Canon) gave to EBA and ESMA. The ESAs Review enhanced those authorities' powers to foster convergence, override gold-plating and investigate potential breaches of Union law. MiCA builds on that architecture by granting EBA direct supervision powers over significant tokens and ESMA register-keeping and standard-setting powers across the crypto sector.

Glossary

Key abbreviations and instruments referenced in Regulation (EU) 2023/1114

MiCA
Markets in Crypto-Assets Regulation: the common short name for Regulation (EU) 2023/1114. The official title does not use the acronym; it has become standard market usage.
ART
Asset-referenced token: the catch-all stablecoin category under MiCA. References a basket of assets, currencies or crypto-assets rather than a single official currency.
EMT
E-money token: a crypto-asset referencing the value of one official currency. Issuable only by credit institutions or e-money institutions. The crypto equivalent of electronic money.
CASP
Crypto-asset service provider: a firm authorised under Title V of MiCA to provide one or more of the ten defined crypto-asset services. Single authorisation valid Union-wide.
EBA
European Banking Authority: directly supervises issuers of significant ARTs and significant EMTs; sets supervisory fees; chairs colleges of supervisors for significant tokens. Founded under Regulation (EU) 1093/2010 (amended by MiCA).
ESMA
European Securities and Markets Authority: maintains the public register of white papers, ART/EMT issuers and CASPs; issues technical standards; produces annual market reports; issues guidelines on the crypto/financial-instrument boundary. Founded under Regulation (EU) 1095/2010 (amended by MiCA).
DLT
Distributed ledger technology: the underlying technology referenced in MiCA's definition of crypto-asset. The regulation is technology-neutral and does not require a specific protocol or consensus mechanism.
NFT
Non-fungible token: a crypto-asset that is unique and non-fungible. Excluded from MiCA's scope under Article 2. ESMA was mandated to issue guidance on the boundary between genuinely non-fungible tokens and those that may in practice be fungible.
DORA
Digital Operational Resilience Act (Regulation (EU) 2022/2554): applies ICT risk-management requirements to financial entities including CASPs authorised under MiCA. A parallel obligation that does not substitute for MiCA compliance.

Legislative timeline

From the Digital Finance Package to full application

September 2020
The Commission publishes the Digital Finance Package, comprising four legislative proposals: MiCA, the DLT Pilot Regime, DORA and the Digital Finance Strategy. MiCA enters the ordinary legislative procedure.
20 April 2023
The European Parliament adopts its position on MiCA at first reading. EP position confirms the main architecture: three token types, white-paper regime, CASP licensing, EBA supervision of significant tokens.
16 May 2023
The Council of the EU adopts MiCA at first reading, accepting the Parliament's amendments. No second reading required.
31 May 2023
Regulation (EU) 2023/1114 adopted. Published in the Official Journal of the European Union, OJ L 150, 9 June 2023, p. 40.
29 June 2023
MiCA enters into force (20th day after publication in the Official Journal). The Commission begins drafting delegated acts and ESMA/EBA begin work on technical standards.
30 June 2024
Titles III and IV of MiCA begin to apply: the stablecoin rules for asset-referenced tokens and e-money tokens are now in force. Existing ART issuers that submitted authorisation applications by 30 July 2024 may continue while applications are processed.
30 December 2024
MiCA applies in full: the Title II white-paper regime, Title V CASP authorisation and operating conditions, Title VI market-abuse rules, and Title VII supervisory powers all become applicable. National grandfathering windows for CASPs begin running.
1 July 2026
The CASP grandfathering window closes. Any CASP not yet authorised or refused under MiCA must cease providing services. Member States may have set an earlier deadline. All remaining national-law-only CASPs must exit or obtain MiCA authorisation.
