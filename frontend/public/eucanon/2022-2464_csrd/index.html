Making 50,000 companies report sustainability like they report finance: the CSRD

Directive (EU) 2022/2464, the Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive, amends four separate instruments to require large undertakings, listed SMEs and large non-EU groups with EU operations to disclose standardised, double-materiality sustainability information, prepared under mandatory European Sustainability Reporting Standards, checked by an independent assurance provider and digitally tagged for machine readability.

Overview

What the Directive does, why it exists, and how it is structured

One Directive, four instruments amended

Directive (EU) 2022/2464 of 14 December 2022, the CSRD, amends four separate instruments in a single act: Directive 2013/34/EU (the Accounting Directive), Directive 2004/109/EC (the Transparency Directive), Directive 2006/43/EC (the Statutory Audit Directive), and Regulation (EU) No 537/2014 (Audit of public-interest entities). It has 8 articles and runs to 66 pages of the Official Journal (OJ L 322, 16.12.2022, p. 15-80).

The bulk of the substance sits in Article 1, which inserts new Articles 19a, 29a to 29d and 40a to 40d into the Accounting Directive, and amends its Article 34 on assurance and Article 49 on delegated-act powers.

Why the CSRD replaces the NFRD

The Non-Financial Reporting Directive (NFRD, Directive 2014/95/EU) had covered only around 11,700 large public-interest entities with more than 500 employees, left the reporting framework voluntary, and imposed no assurance requirement. The result was inconsistent, incomparable, and often unverifiable sustainability disclosures across the Union.

CSRD expands the reporting population roughly fivefold, mandates a single Union-wide standard, requires independent assurance, and requires the information to be digitally tagged so it is machine-readable at scale.

A Directive, not a Regulation

The CSRD does not apply directly. Each Member State had to transpose it into national law by 6 July 2024, 18 months after entry into force. The substantive reporting obligations then apply undertaking by undertaking, wave by wave, according to financial year, not according to the transposition date itself.

Procedure and signature

The Directive was negotiated under the ordinary legislative procedure, file 2021/0104(COD). The European Parliament adopted its position on 10 November 2022 and the Council took its decision on 28 November 2022. It was signed at Strasbourg on 14 December 2022 by Roberta Metsola for the European Parliament and Mikuláš Bek for the Council, published in OJ L 322 on 16 December 2022, and entered into force on 5 January 2023, the twentieth day after publication.

Part of a wider sustainable-finance framework

CSRD is the disclosure engine of a broader EU sustainable-finance and corporate-responsibility architecture. SFDR (Regulation (EU) 2019/2088) governs how financial-market participants disclose their own sustainability approach, the Taxonomy Regulation (Regulation (EU) 2020/852) classifies which economic activities count as environmentally sustainable, and CSDDD (Directive (EU) 2024/1760) imposes due diligence obligations across own operations, subsidiaries and value-chain business partners. CSRD is currently the only one of these four instruments to have its own Brubru canon deep-dive; the others will appear here as Brubru's analysis extends.

Core requirements, topic by topic

Double materiality, ESRS, digital tagging, assurance, third-country reach, and sanctions

Double materiality (new Article 19a)

Sustainability information must sit in a dedicated, identifiable section of the management report, and must cover both directions of materiality at once: the undertaking's impacts on sustainability matters (impact materiality), and how sustainability matters affect the undertaking's own development, performance and position (financial materiality). Neither perspective can substitute for the other; both are legally required.

Mandatory content areas span business model and strategy, including a transition plan compatible with 1.5°C warming and climate neutrality by 2050, with disclosure of coal, oil and gas exposure; time-bound targets, including absolute greenhouse-gas reduction targets for 2030 and 2050; governance; sustainability policies; sustainability-linked incentive schemes; due diligence and principal adverse impacts across operations, products and the supply chain; principal risks and dependencies; and the indicators supporting all of the above. Information must be forward-looking and retrospective, qualitative and quantitative, and cover short-, medium- and long-term horizons.

Consolidated reporting (new Article 29a)

Parent undertakings of large groups must prepare consolidated sustainability reporting at group level. Subsidiaries included in a parent's consolidated report are exempt from reporting individually, subject to publicity conditions linking to the parent report. Large listed subsidiaries are not exempt, for investor-protection reasons.

European Sustainability Reporting Standards, ESRS (new Article 29b)

The Commission adopts ESRS by delegated act, based on technical advice from EFRAG, the European Financial Reporting Advisory Group. The first set of 12 general ESRS was adopted on 31 July 2023 by Commission Delegated Regulation (EU) 2023/2772: two cross-cutting standards (ESRS 1 general requirements, ESRS 2 general disclosures), five environmental standards (climate change, pollution, water and marine resources, biodiversity and ecosystems, resource use and circular economy), four social standards (own workforce, workers in the value chain, affected communities, consumers and end-users), and one governance standard (business conduct).

Sector-specific ESRS, originally due by 30 June 2024, were deferred to 30 June 2026 by Delegated Directive (EU) 2023/2775. A proportionate voluntary standard for non-listed SMEs (VSME) and a mandatory standard for listed SMEs (LSME) are mandated by Article 29c; EFRAG published its VSME draft in December 2024.

Digital tagging, ESEF (new Article 29d)

Sustainability information must be prepared in the single electronic reporting format under Commission Delegated Regulation (EU) 2019/815, and marked up (tagged) against a digital taxonomy the Commission adopts by delegated act. The tagging obligation applies from financial years starting on or after 1 January 2026, one year later than the wave 1 reporting obligation itself.

Assurance (amended Article 34)

Reporting undertakings must obtain an assurance opinion on their sustainability reporting. Limited assurance applies from wave 1. The Commission may adopt limited-assurance standards by 1 October 2026, and reasonable-assurance standards by 1 October 2028, both by delegated act. Assurance is performed by the statutory auditor by default, or by an independent assurance services provider accredited under Regulation (EC) No 765/2008, where a Member State chooses to open the market. Where the statutory auditor performs the assurance, the non-audit-services fee cap in Regulation (EU) No 537/2014 excludes sustainability-assurance work.

Third-country undertakings (new Articles 40a-40d)

A third-country undertaking with net turnover in the Union above €150 million in each of the last two consecutive financial years, and at least one EU subsidiary that is large or listed, or one EU branch with net turnover above €40 million, must publish an EU-territory-only sustainability report through that subsidiary or branch. Reporting standards for these undertakings were due by delegated act by 30 June 2024. This obligation applies from financial years starting on or after 1 January 2028, wave 4.

Sanctions

Amendments to Article 51 of the Accounting Directive require Member States to lay down effective, proportionate and dissuasive sanctions for infringements of the national provisions transposing the CSRD. The precise sanction regimes, and how strictly they are enforced, vary by Member State.

The value-chain grace period

Reporting must cover the undertaking's whole value chain, not only its own operations, but Article 19a recognises that undertakings cannot obtain reliable value-chain data on day one. A 3-year grace period allows an undertaking to explain why value-chain information was not available, rather than report it outright, while it builds the data-collection processes needed from its suppliers and business partners.

This grace period is frequently confused with the wave postponements introduced by the Stop-the-clock Directive. The two are unrelated: the value-chain grace period runs from an undertaking's own first reporting year, whichever wave it falls into, while the Stop-the-clock Directive shifts the reporting year itself for waves 2 and 3.

Application waves

Four waves, and two of them postponed two years by the Omnibus stop-the-clock

Wave Financial year starts First report due In-scope entities Status
Wave 1 On or after 1 January 2024 2025 Large public-interest entities (PIEs) with more than 500 employees, the group already reporting under the NFRD Unchanged
Wave 2 On or after 1 January 2027 2028 All other large undertakings meeting 2 of 3 thresholds: >€25M balance sheet, >€50M net turnover, >250 employees Postponed 2 years by Dir (EU) 2025/794 (originally FY2025 / report 2026)
Wave 3 On or after 1 January 2028 2029 Listed SMEs (except micro), small non-complex credit institutions, captive (re)insurance undertakings, with an optional Member State opt-out until FY2028 Postponed 2 years by Dir (EU) 2025/794 (originally FY2026 / report 2027)
Wave 4 On or after 1 January 2028 2029 Third-country undertakings with EU turnover above €150 million, reporting via an EU subsidiary or branch Unchanged

Do not conflate the two Omnibus tracks

The Stop-the-clock Directive (EU) 2025/794, adopted 14 April 2025 and published as OJ L, 2025/794, 16 April 2025, only postpones the wave 2 and wave 3 dates shown above. Wave 1, already reporting since financial year 2024, is untouched.

A separate, still-contested substantive Omnibus proposal (COM(2025) 81 and COM(2025) 82, tabled 26 February 2025) would raise the "large undertaking" employee threshold from 250 to 1,000, cutting in-scope firms by roughly 80% to around 7,000 undertakings, simplify the ESRS, make sector-specific ESRS voluntary, and cap the data that large undertakings can request from SME suppliers. These changes are not yet law and remain under co-legislator negotiation as of mid-2026.

CSRD vs NFRD

What changed when the Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive replaced the Non-Financial Reporting Directive

Feature CSRD, Directive (EU) 2022/2464 NFRD, Directive 2014/95/EU
Undertakings in scope Around 50,000, as originally designed: large undertakings, listed SMEs, and qualifying third-country undertakings Around 11,700, large public-interest entities only, with more than 500 employees
Reporting standard Mandatory ESRS, adopted by the Commission on EFRAG's technical advice No mandated framework; undertakings could choose their own, or none
Materiality Double materiality codified explicitly in the operative text (Article 19a) Materiality referenced but not defined as a dual, mandatory test
Assurance Mandatory, limited assurance from wave 1, with a path to reasonable assurance No assurance requirement
Digital format Mandatory digital tagging in ESEF from financial years starting 1 January 2026 No digital tagging requirement
Value chain Explicit value-chain coverage, with a 3-year grace period for unavailable information No explicit value-chain reporting requirement

Related EU acts referenced throughout this Directive

The CSRD does not stand alone. Beyond the four instruments it directly amends, the operative text and its delegated acts reach into a wider set of EU law:

  • Regulation (EU) 2021/1119, the European Climate Law, anchors the 2050 climate-neutrality benchmark against which every mandatory transition plan is measured
  • Directive (EU) 2024/1760, CSDDD, is the operational-conduct sibling: where CSRD requires disclosure of due diligence processes, CSDDD requires the due diligence itself
  • Commission Delegated Regulation (EU) 2019/815 supplies the ESEF format that CSRD's digital-tagging obligation now extends to sustainability information
  • Delegated Directive (EU) 2023/2775 raised the Accounting Directive's size thresholds by around 25% for inflation and deferred sector-specific ESRS to 2026
  • Directive 2004/109/EC and Directive 2006/43/EC, amended directly by CSRD Articles 2 and 3, extend reporting-format and audit obligations to listed issuers and statutory auditors respectively

How the Member States transposed it

A deadline that passed on 6 July 2024, and that several Member States missed

The CSRD is not directly applicable: each Member State had to write it into national law by 6 July 2024. That deadline has now passed. Some Member States, including Belgium, France, Germany, Italy, the Netherlands, Spain, Ireland, Denmark, Sweden, Finland, Poland and Czechia, have notified national transposing measures, with varying delays against the deadline. Others had still not notified a transposing measure well into 2025, and the Commission opened infringement proceedings against multiple Member States for late transposition during 2025 and into 2026. Glossary

Load-bearing terms as used in Directive (EU) 2022/2464

CSRD
Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive, Directive (EU) 2022/2464: the mandatory sustainability disclosure regime replacing the NFRD.
NFRD
Non-Financial Reporting Directive, Directive 2014/95/EU: the voluntary-framework predecessor covering around 11,700 large public-interest entities.
ESRS
European Sustainability Reporting Standards: the mandatory disclosure standards the Commission adopts by delegated act under new Article 29b.
EFRAG
European Financial Reporting Advisory Group: the technical body whose advice underpins every set of ESRS the Commission adopts.
Double materiality
The principle, codified in new Article 19a(1), that reporting must cover both impact materiality and financial materiality at once.
Impact materiality
The undertaking's actual and potential impacts on people and the environment, one half of the double-materiality test.
Financial materiality
How sustainability matters affect the undertaking's own development, performance and position, the other half of the double-materiality test.
PIE
Public-interest entity: listed companies, banks and insurers, the population that already reported under the NFRD and forms wave 1 under the CSRD.
Value chain
The undertaking's own operations, products, services, business relationships and supply chain, all within scope of the reporting obligation under new Article 19a.
ESEF
European Single Electronic Format: the digital reporting format under Commission Delegated Regulation (EU) 2019/815 that sustainability information must now also use.
XBRL
eXtensible Business Reporting Language: the machine-readable tagging technology underlying ESEF, used to mark up sustainability disclosures for digital consumption.
Limited assurance
The lighter-touch assurance opinion required from wave 1 under amended Article 34, pending the Commission's future limited-assurance standards.
Reasonable assurance
A higher-confidence assurance opinion the Commission may mandate by delegated act by 1 October 2028, once limited-assurance standards are established.
Wave 1
The first reporting cohort, financial years starting on or after 1 January 2024: large PIEs with more than 500 employees, already reporting since 2025.
Transition plan
The undertaking's disclosed plan for climate resilience compatible with 1.5°C warming and climate neutrality by 2050, mandatory content under new Article 19a(2)(a).
Omnibus
The Commission's 2025 sustainability-simplification package: a Stop-the-clock Directive already adopted, and a separate, still-contested proposal to narrow CSRD's scope and thresholds.

Sibling instruments in the EU sustainability reporting and due diligence family

CSDDD, the Corporate Sustainability Due Diligence Directive

Directive (EU) 2024/1760. The sibling operational mandate. Where CSRD forces reporting, CSDDD forces doing. Around 6,000 EU and 900 non-EU companies must identify, prevent, mitigate, remedy and publicly account for adverse human rights and environmental impacts across own operations, subsidiaries and business partners in the chain of activities, and adopt a climate transition plan compatible with 1.5 degrees Celsius. Article 22 CSDDD transition plans are DEEMED compliant when the company already reports one under CSRD Article 19a/29a/40a.
