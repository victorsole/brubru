DORA: the EU's first horizontal cybersecurity rulebook for finance

Regulation (EU) 2022/2554 consolidates ICT risk requirements scattered across CRD IV, MiFID II, Solvency II, PSD2, EMIR, CSDR and nine other acts into one directly applicable Single Rulebook. It covers approximately 22,000 EU financial entities and introduces the first Union-level oversight of critical cloud and software providers on whose resilience the financial system depends.

Overview

What DORA is, why it exists, and what it does not do

One Regulation in place of nine fragmented sectoral regimes

Before DORA, ICT risk requirements for financial entities were scattered across at least nine different EU legal acts: CRD IV, MiFID II, Solvency II, UCITS, AIFMD, PSD2, EMIR, CSDR, the CRA Regulation, and the Benchmarks Regulation. Each act addressed operational and ICT risk differently, creating regulatory fragmentation for cross-border groups and uneven protection across subsectors.

DORA replaces all of those provisions with a single, directly applicable Regulation. As a Regulation (not a Directive), it takes effect in every Member State without national transposition, creating a true Single Rulebook for digital operational resilience (recitals 9-14).

Lex specialis vs NIS 2 and companion DORA Directive

DORA is lex specialis with respect to NIS 2 (Directive (EU) 2022/2555): where DORA applies to financial entities, its more specific ICT risk and incident-reporting obligations prevail over NIS 2, while financial entities remain part of the NIS 2 ecosystem for cross-sector cooperation and learning (recital 16).

The companion Directive (EU) 2022/2556 amends eight financial-sector Directives (UCITS, Solvency II, AIFMD, CRD IV, BRRD, MiFID II, PSD2, IORP II) to align their operational-risk provisions with DORA and insert a cross-reference back to it. DORA itself amends five Regulations (CRA Reg 1060/2009, EMIR 648/2012, MiFIR 600/2014, CSDR 909/2014, BMR 2016/1011) by narrowing or replacing their ICT-related provisions.

Important: what DORA does not do

No capital requirements for ICT risk - that is a deliberate departure from the traditional "set a capital number" approach to operational risk (recital 12). DORA is a qualitative instrument: protect, detect, contain, recover, learn. Capital stays in CRR and Solvency II.

No data localisation - recital 82 is explicit: DORA does not require data storage or processing to be undertaken in the Union, even for critical ICT third-party service providers.

No new sectoral supervisors - DORA routes compliance through the existing CRD IV, MiFID II, Solvency II, PSD2 etc. authorities (Art 46).

The sixth building block: the Oversight Framework (Chapter V Section II)

DORA's most innovative provision is separate from the five pillars. Chapter V Section II (Arts 31-44) creates the first EU-level oversight of non-financial service providers: cloud, software, data-centre, and data-analytics companies whose availability the financial system depends on. The Lead Overseer (one of EBA, ESMA or EIOPA) can issue binding recommendations and impose daily penalty payments of up to 1% of worldwide turnover on designated critical ICT third-party service providers (CTPPs).

Pillar 1: ICT risk management (Arts 5-16)

Governance, identification, protection, detection, response, recovery, learning and communication

Governance (Art 5)

The management body bears ultimate responsibility for ICT risk. It must define, approve and oversee the ICT risk management framework, set the risk tolerance, approve the ICT business continuity policy plus response and recovery plans, allocate the ICT budget, approve the ICT third-party policy, and keep itself trained on ICT risk (Art 5(4)).

Financial entities other than microenterprises must establish a dedicated role to monitor ICT third-party arrangements, or designate a senior management member responsible for that exposure (Art 5(3)).

ICT risk management framework (Art 6)

Must be sound, comprehensive and well-documented. Reviewed at least yearly, plus after every major ICT-related incident or supervisory finding. Subject to internal audit (Art 6(6)). Must include a digital operational resilience strategy (Art 6(8)) covering risk tolerance, security objectives, ICT reference architecture, detection mechanisms, current resilience status, testing plan, and communication strategy.

Microenterprises and small or non-interconnected entities follow a simplified framework (Art 16) - Articles 5-15 do not apply; instead a leaner set of eight obligations.

Identification and protection (Arts 8-9)

Art 8 (identification): Map all ICT-supported business functions, classify information and ICT assets, identify all ICT third-party dependencies, run risk assessment on every major change, conduct yearly ICT risk assessments on legacy systems.

Art 9 (protection): Information security policy, network and infrastructure management, access control, strong authentication, cryptography, ICT change management, and patch management. Networks must be designed to be instantaneously severable or segmentable to prevent contagion.

Detection, response and recovery (Arts 10-11)

Art 10 (detection): Mechanisms to detect anomalous activities, multi-layer controls, alert thresholds, automatic escalation.

Art 11 (response and recovery): ICT business continuity policy, response and recovery plans, business impact analysis (BIA), redundant ICT capacities, testing at least yearly including cyber-attack scenarios and primary-to-redundant switchovers for non-microenterprises, crisis management function.

Backup, learning and communication (Arts 12-14)

Art 12 (backup): Backup systems segregated physically and logically from source ICT systems. CSDs must maintain at least one secondary processing site at a geographical distance from the primary, with full continuity capability (Art 12(5)).

Art 13 (learning): Post-incident reviews, lessons-learned feedback into the framework, ICT security awareness programmes and digital operational resilience training as compulsory staff modules (Art 13(6)) covering all employees and senior management.

Art 14 (communication): Crisis communication plans for responsible disclosure of major incidents or vulnerabilities. At least one person responsible for the public and media communication function.

Pillar 2: ICT-related incident reporting (Arts 17-23)

Three-stage reporting, six classification criteria, and the EU Hub feasibility study

Classification criteria (Art 18)

Six criteria determine whether an incident qualifies as a major ICT-related incident (requiring mandatory reporting) or a significant cyber threat:

  • Number or relevance of clients or financial counterparts affected; transactions affected; reputational impact
  • Duration and downtime of the service
  • Geographical spread, particularly if affecting more than two Member States
  • Data losses (affecting availability, authenticity, integrity or confidentiality)
  • Criticality of services affected, including for critical or important functions
  • Economic impact: direct and indirect, absolute and relative
Three-stage reporting to competent authorities (Art 19)

Mandatory reporting of major incidents to the competent authority (a single national authority is designated where the entity has multiple supervisors). Three stages:

  • Initial notification - as soon as the materiality thresholds are met
  • Intermediate report(s) - whenever status changes significantly
  • Final report - after root-cause analysis, with actual impact figures

Significant cyber threats may be notified voluntarily. Cross-border impacts flow through EBA, ESMA or EIOPA to relevant Member States. Significant credit institutions under the SSM report to the NCA, which immediately transmits to the ECB. Materiality thresholds, time limits and templates are set by RTS and ITS (Art 20), submitted to the Commission by 17 July 2024.

EU Hub feasibility study (Art 21) and payment incidents (Art 23)

Art 21: The ESAs were required to assess by 17 January 2025 whether a single EU Hub for major ICT-related incident reporting is feasible - to streamline reporting across the multiple competent authorities currently involved.

Art 23: Credit institutions, payment institutions, account information service providers and electronic money institutions now report operational and security payment-related incidents under DORA that were previously reported under PSD2 Article 96 - irrespective of whether they are ICT-related. DORA consolidates and replaces that PSD2 reporting obligation.

Pillar 3: digital operational resilience testing (Arts 24-27)

Yearly basic testing, TLPT every three years, and TIBER-EU alignment

Basic testing programme (Arts 24-25)

A comprehensive testing programme as an integral part of the ICT risk management framework. Risk-based and performed by independent parties (internal or external). Wide menu of tools: vulnerability assessments and scans, open-source analyses, network security assessments, gap analyses, physical security reviews, questionnaires, scanning software, source-code reviews where feasible, scenario-based tests, compatibility testing, performance testing, end-to-end testing, and penetration testing. At least yearly tests on all ICT systems and applications supporting critical or important functions.

CSDs and CCPs must perform vulnerability assessments before any deployment or redeployment of new or existing applications and infrastructure components.

TLPT - threat-led penetration testing (Art 26)

Threat-led penetration testing mimics the tactics, techniques and procedures of real-life threat actors, delivering a controlled, bespoke, intelligence-led (red team) test of the financial entity's critical live production systems.

  • Performed at least every 3 years (frequency may be adjusted by the competent authority)
  • Required only of financial entities identified by the competent authority based on: impact on the financial sector, possible financial-stability concerns including systemic character, ICT risk profile and ICT maturity
  • Must cover several or all critical or important functions
  • Pooled TLPT permitted (Art 26(4)) where ICT third-party participation could adversely impact other customers
  • Internal testers allowed with prior supervisory approval, no conflict of interest, periodic rotation (external testers required every three tests - Art 26(8))
  • Significant credit institutions under the SSM: external testers only
  • Attestation provided by the authority for mutual recognition across the EU; the financial entity retains full responsibility
  • Aligned with the TIBER-EU framework (ECB)
Tester requirements (Art 27)

TLPT testers must demonstrate: highest suitability and reputability, expertise in threat intelligence, penetration testing and red team testing, certification by a Member State accreditation body or adherence to formal codes of conduct, audited assurance on risk management, and professional indemnity insurance covering negligence in testing activities.

Pillar 4: ICT third-party risk management (Arts 28-30)

Register of information, concentration risk, contractual provisions, and exit strategies

Principles and mandatory artefacts (Art 28)

The financial entity at all times remains fully responsible for compliance with DORA - outsourcing does not transfer obligations. Mandatory artefacts include:

  • Strategy on ICT third-party risk, reviewed regularly by the management body
  • Register of information of all contractual arrangements on the use of ICT services, distinguishing those supporting critical or important functions from those that do not; reportable yearly to the competent authority (Art 28(3))
  • Pre-contracting due diligence: criticality assessment, supervisory conditions, ICT concentration risk analysis, due diligence on the prospective TPP, conflict-of-interest screening (Art 28(4))
  • Termination triggers (Art 28(7)): significant breach by the TPP, material changes affecting performance, evidenced ICT risk-management weaknesses, inability of the competent authority to effectively supervise the financial entity
  • Exit strategies for ICT services supporting critical or important functions (Art 28(8)) - comprehensive, documented, sufficiently tested
Concentration risk (Art 29)

Pre-contracting analysis: would the contract result in dependence on a TPP that is not easily substitutable, or in multiple critical-function contracts with the same TPP or closely connected TPPs? Subcontracting analysis is required especially where the subcontractor is in a third country - taking into account insolvency law of that country, data-protection enforcement, and the length and complexity of subcontracting chains.

Mandatory contractual provisions (Art 30)

Every contract for ICT services must include at a minimum: full description of functions and services; locations of data processing and storage; availability, authenticity, integrity and confidentiality provisions; data-access, recovery and return on insolvency or termination; service-level descriptions; TPP assistance during ICT incidents; full cooperation with competent and resolution authorities; termination rights with minimum notice; conditions for TPP participation in the financial entity's training programmes.

For ICT services supporting critical or important functions, additionally: precise quantitative and qualitative SLA performance targets; notice periods on developments materially impacting service delivery; business contingency plans; TLPT participation; unrestricted rights of access, inspection and audit by the financial entity, the competent authority and the Lead Overseer; adequate transition periods in exit strategies. Microenterprises may delegate audit rights to an independent third party appointed by the TPP (Art 30(3)).

The Oversight Framework: critical ICT third-party service providers (Arts 31-44)

The first EU-level oversight of non-financial service providers - cloud, software, data centres, and data analytics

The most innovative part of DORA

For the first time, the EU directly oversees, at Union level, the cloud, software, data-centre, and data-analytics providers on whose continued availability the financial system depends. These entities are not financial firms and were previously outside the perimeter of financial supervision. DORA changes that for designated critical ICT third-party service providers (CTPPs).

Designation as a CTPP (Art 31)

The three ESAs, through their Joint Committee, on recommendation from the Oversight Forum (Art 32), designate an ICT third-party service provider as critical based on four criteria:

  • Systemic impact on the stability, continuity or quality of financial services if the provider fails
  • Systemic character of the financial entities relying on it (G-SIIs, O-SIIs and their interdependencies)
  • Reliance of financial entities on its services for critical or important functions
  • Degree of substitutability - limited alternatives, market share, technical complexity, migration cost and risk

The list of CTPPs is published yearly. ICT TPPs not automatically designated may opt in by application (Art 31(11)). Third-country CTPPs must establish an EU subsidiary within 12 months of designation (Art 31(12)) and may continue providing services from outside the Union, but oversight presence in the EU is mandatory.

Carved out (Art 31(8)): financial entities providing ICT services to other financial entities, providers already under ESCB oversight (TARGET, T2S etc.), intra-group providers, and providers active only in one Member State serving entities active only in that Member State.

Structure: Joint Committee, Oversight Forum and JON (Arts 32, 34)
  • Joint Committee (ESAs) - cross-sector coordination, joint positions on CTPPs
  • Oversight Forum - sub-committee; prepares joint positions; includes Chairs of the three ESAs, one high-level NCA representative per Member State, and ECB, ESRB, ENISA and Commission as observers
  • Joint Oversight Network (JON, Art 34) - coordinates the three Lead Overseers for operational alignment
The Lead Overseer (Art 33)

For each CTPP, the Lead Overseer is the ESA (EBA, ESMA or EIOPA) responsible for the financial-entity sub-sector whose entities together hold the largest share of total assets among the financial entities using that CTPP. The Lead Overseer is the primary point of contact for the CTPP and conducts the assessment of governance, ICT risk management, business continuity, physical security, incident management, data portability, testing, audits and standards.

The Lead Overseer adopts a yearly individual oversight plan per CTPP (Art 33(4)); the CTPP may submit a reasoned statement within 15 calendar days before adoption.

Powers of the Lead Overseer (Art 35)
  • Information and documentation requests (Art 37): all relevant records, policies, agreements
  • General investigations (Art 38): examine records, take copies, summon representatives, interview persons, request data-traffic records
  • On-site and off-site inspections (Art 39): enter business premises, seal premises, books and records during inspection
  • Recommendations: on ICT security standards, service terms preventing single points of failure or systemic amplification, planned subcontracting including in third countries, and (most powerful) require the CTPP to refrain from a further subcontracting arrangement where it concerns a critical or important function and poses a clear and serious risk to Union financial stability
  • Periodic penalty payments: up to 1% of average daily worldwide turnover, daily, for a maximum of 6 months (Art 35(8))

The Lead Overseer can exercise these powers outside the Union (Art 36) if the CTPP consents and the relevant third-country authority does not object, subject to administrative cooperation arrangements.

Follow-up and last-resort suspension powers (Art 42)

Within 60 calendar days, the CTPP either notifies compliance with the recommendation or provides a reasoned explanation for not following it. If the explanation is insufficient, the Lead Overseer publicly discloses the non-compliance.

Competent authorities - as a measure of last resort, after notification and (voluntary) consultation with NIS 2 authorities - may take a decision (Art 42(6)) requiring financial entities to temporarily suspend, in part or completely, the use of a service provided by the CTPP, or to terminate the relevant contractual arrangements, until the identified risks are addressed.

Oversight fees (Art 43) and international cooperation (Art 44)

Art 43: Lead Overseer expenditure is fully fee-funded by CTPPs, proportionate to turnover. The amount and payment mechanism are set by delegated act (deadline: 17 July 2024).

Art 44: EBA, ESMA and EIOPA may conclude administrative arrangements with third-country regulatory and supervisory authorities to share best practices on ICT third-party risk review. A confidential report to the EP, Council and Commission is produced every five years.

Pillar 5: information-sharing arrangements (Art 45)

Voluntary cyber threat intelligence exchange in trusted communities

Trusted communities for intelligence sharing

Financial entities may voluntarily exchange cyber threat information and intelligence within trusted communities. Shareable intelligence includes: indicators of compromise, tactics, techniques and procedures, alerts, configuration tools, and information on cyber actors or campaigns.

Such arrangements must be governed by rules of conduct that respect business confidentiality, GDPR personal data protection rules, and EU competition law (Art 101 TFEU). Participation must be notified to the competent authority. The Commission and ENISA act as observers at these communities where appropriate.

Key definitions (Art 3)

Article 3 contains 65 definitions. The six load-bearing concepts that drive the rest of the Regulation:

Load-bearing definitions from Article 3

  • "critical or important function" (Art 3(22)) - a function whose disruption would materially impair financial performance, soundness or continuity, or compliance with authorisation conditions. This definition determines which ICT services attract the highest obligations under DORA.
  • "major ICT-related incident" (Art 3(10)) - an ICT-related incident with high adverse impact on systems supporting critical or important functions. The trigger for mandatory three-stage reporting under Art 19.
  • "threat-led penetration testing" or TLPT (Art 3(17)) - intelligence-led red team testing of live production systems, mimicking real-life threat actors. Required at least every 3 years for identified systemically important entities.
  • "ICT third-party service provider" (Art 3(19)) - an undertaking providing ICT services (including hardware-as-a-service; analogue telephony excluded). Distinct from the "ICT intra-group service provider" (Art 3(20)).
  • "critical ICT third-party service provider" or CTPP (Art 3(23)) - a TPP designated as critical under Article 31, subject to the full Oversight Framework and the Lead Overseer's powers.
  • "ICT concentration risk" (Art 3(29)) - exposure to one or related critical TPPs creating a dependency that could endanger critical or important functions or Union financial stability. The concept underpinning Art 29 analysis and the Oversight Framework rationale.

Legislative timeline and legal family

From the NIS 1 Directive to full DORA application and beyond

2016
NIS 1 Directive (EU) 2016/1148 - the first horizontal EU cybersecurity instrument, covering selected credit institutions, trading venues and CCPs identified at national level. Recognised the gap: fragmented financial-sector coverage.
8 March 2018
Commission FinTech Action Plan - identifies the need for a sector-specific ICT operational resilience instrument to address digital transformation risks in financial services.
April 2019
Joint ESA Technical Advice - EBA, EIOPA and ESMA call for a coherent EU approach to ICT risk in finance, flagging nine sectoral acts with fragmented requirements and proposing a single, cross-sectoral framework.
September 2020
DORA proposal COM/2020/595 - part of the Digital Finance Package, alongside MiCA. Introduces the five-pillar structure and the CTPP Oversight Framework for the first time.
14 December 2022
DORA adopted - Regulation (EU) 2022/2554 and Directive (EU) 2022/2556 published together in OJ L 333 of 27 December 2022. NIS 2 (Directive (EU) 2022/2555) adopted on the same day.
16 January 2023
Entry into force - day 20 after OJ publication. The two-year transition period begins. Financial entities start building frameworks, registers of information and testing programmes.
17 January 2024
First-batch RTS deadline - ESAs submit to the Commission the technical standards on ICT risk management policies, access management, detection mechanisms, business-continuity policy components, testing, response and recovery, and the register of information (Arts 15, 16, 18, 28, 41).
17 July 2024
Second-batch deadline - RTS, ITS and delegated acts on incident reporting templates and time limits, TLPT, subcontracting, CTPP designation criteria, and oversight fees (Arts 20, 26, 30, 31, 43).
17 January 2025
DORA applies in full (Art 64). Compliance enforced from this date. EU Hub feasibility report (Art 21) and Member State notification of implementing law (Art 53) also due by this date.
17 January 2026
Auditor review report (Art 58(3)) - the Commission reports to the EP and Council on whether statutory auditors and audit firms should be added to DORA's scope.
17 January 2028
Full review report (Art 58(1)) - covers CTPP designation criteria, voluntary cyber threat notification, the third-country CTPP regime, and JON effectiveness. May lead to legislative amendments.

Glossary

Load-bearing terms as used in Regulation (EU) 2022/2554 and its implementing measures

TLPT
Threat-Led Penetration Testing. An intelligence-led red team exercise on live production systems, mimicking real-life threat actors' tactics, techniques and procedures. Required every 3 years for systemically important financial entities under Art 26.
TIBER-EU
Threat Intelligence-Based Ethical Red Teaming, developed by the ECB. The TLPT methodology adopted by DORA as its reference framework. TIBER-EU tests are conducted on live production systems with intelligence-gathering, red team, and purple team phases.
CTPP
Critical ICT Third-Party Service Provider. An ICT service provider (cloud, software, data analytics, data centre) formally designated under Art 31 by the ESAs as critical to Union financial stability. Subject to the Lead Overseer's oversight powers and periodic penalty payments.
JON
Joint Oversight Network (Art 34). Coordinates the three Lead Overseers (one each from EBA, ESMA, EIOPA) to ensure consistent application of the Oversight Framework across the three ESAs. A permanent network without separate legal personality.
Lead Overseer
The ESA (EBA, ESMA or EIOPA) designated as primary point of contact and overseer for a specific CTPP. Determined by which ESA's supervised entities hold the largest share of total assets among all financial entities using that CTPP (Art 31(1)(b)).
ESAs
The three European Supervisory Authorities: EBA (banking), ESMA (securities and markets), and EIOPA (insurance and occupational pensions). They jointly govern the Oversight Framework through their Joint Committee and the Oversight Forum.
Joint Committee
The cross-sectoral coordination body of EBA, ESMA and EIOPA established under Art 54 of each ESA Regulation. Under DORA, it coordinates joint positions, exercises, and decisions on CTPP designation and the Oversight Forum's work (Art 32).
Oversight Forum
Sub-committee of the Joint Committee, chaired by the Lead Overseer, including Chairs of the three ESAs, one high-level NCA representative per Member State, and ECB, ESRB, ENISA and Commission as observers. Prepares CTPP designation recommendations (Art 32).
JET
Joint Examination Team. An ad hoc team composed of staff of the Lead Overseer and other ESAs, formed to conduct specific investigations or on-site inspections of a CTPP (Art 39(2)).
ICT concentration risk
Exposure to one or a group of related critical TPPs creating a dependency that could endanger critical or important functions or Union financial stability (Art 3(29)). The rationale for CTPP designation and Art 29 pre-contracting analysis.
Microenterprise
An entity with fewer than 10 persons employed AND annual turnover or balance sheet total not exceeding EUR 2 million (Art 3(60)). Microenterprises follow the simplified ICT risk management framework under Art 16 and have reduced obligations across the Regulation.
Critical or important function
A function whose disruption would materially impair the financial performance, soundness or continuity of the financial entity, or compliance with its authorisation conditions (Art 3(22)). The highest-obligation tier throughout DORA, triggering enhanced testing, contractual, exit-strategy and reporting requirements.
ICT third-party risk
The risk arising from the use of ICT services provided by third parties, including through subcontractors (Art 3(18)). Pillar 4 (Arts 28-30) and the Oversight Framework (Arts 31-44) address this risk. The financial entity remains fully responsible regardless of outsourcing.
Lex specialis
A rule of legal interpretation: the more specific law prevails over the more general one. DORA is lex specialis vs NIS 2 for financial entities (recital 16). Where DORA contains a specific provision, it applies; NIS 2 fills gaps for cross-sector cooperation.
Register of information
The full inventory of all contractual arrangements with ICT third-party service providers, distinguishing those supporting critical or important functions. Maintained continuously, reported yearly to the competent authority, and produced on request (Art 28(3)). The foundation for Art 29 concentration risk analysis.
Digital operational resilience
The financial entity's ability to build, assure and review its operational integrity and reliability, ensuring either directly or indirectly through use of ICT services the full range of ICT-related capabilities needed to address the security of network and information systems (Art 3(1)).
