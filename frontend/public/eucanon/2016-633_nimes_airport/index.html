The Nimes Airport State Aid Decision

Commission Decision (EU) 2016/633 is the EU’s landmark ruling on airport–airline commercial agreements. It established the incremental profitability test as the benchmark for assessing whether publicly funded airports give unlawful subsidies to low-cost carriers, and ordered France to recover up to EUR 7 million from Ryanair.

What happened

A regional airport, a dominant low-cost carrier and a decade of below-cost deals

The case in brief

Nimes-Garons airport, located 12 km south of the city of Nimes in the Gard department of southern France, operated as a joint civilian-military aerodrome. From 2003 onwards, after Air France and Air Littoral withdrew, Ryanair was effectively the sole scheduled carrier. The airport relied entirely on public support to keep operations viable.

Between 2000 and 2010, the successive airport operators signed a series of commercial agreements with Ryanair and its subsidiary Airport Marketing Services (AMS) that gave the airline discounted airport charges plus marketing payments. The Commission found these agreements cost the airport more than they earned. A market-rational private operator would never have signed them. The deficit was covered by public funds, so Ryanair was receiving an indirect subsidy.

On 23 July 2014 the Commission ordered France to recover up to EUR 7 million from Ryanair and AMS.

The airport

Nimes-Garons (IATA: FNI) sits 12 km from the city centre. Until 2 July 2011 it was primarily a military aerodrome with civilian aviation as a secondary use. Theoretical capacity was around 700,000 passengers per year; actual traffic peaked at 320,000 in 2001 and rarely exceeded 230,000 later.

The TGV high-speed rail link reached Nimes in 2001, connecting the city to Paris in roughly three hours. Montpellier airport is approximately 63 km away and handles around six times the traffic. Both factors were relevant to the Commission's rejection of France's argument that the airport provided a service of general economic interest.

The operators

CCI (2000–2006): The Nimes-Uzes-Le Vigan Chamber of Commerce and Industry held a 60-year public equipment concession from 1965. Its concession was terminated early in January 2006 and replaced by a public service delegation (CDSP) awarded to SMAN, a syndicate of local authorities grouping the Gard Departmental Council, Nimes Metropole and Ales Cevennes.

VTAN (2007–2012): Veolia Transport Aeroport de Nimes, a private subsidiary of the Veolia group, won the CDSP in a tender from 1 January 2007. Despite being privately owned, the Commission found VTAN's airline agreements were attributable to the state through the structure of its delegation contract.

Why public money was involved

Both operators received substantial public subsidies to cover operating losses. The CCI received exceptional grants from local authorities totalling roughly EUR 1.35 million in 2005 and EUR 200,000 in 2006, plus interest-free advances of approximately EUR 9 million from the CCI's general arm. VTAN received a flat-rate annual operating contribution of EUR 1.2 to 1.5 million from SMAN, raised through successive contract amendments.

The Commission found these subsidies were State aid (the operators could not have operated without them), but declared them compatible with the internal market under Article 107(3)(c) TFEU and the 2014 Aviation Guidelines. Small airports handling under 700,000 passengers per year that genuinely cannot cover operating costs may receive operating support, provided strict proportionality conditions are met.

What the Commission found incompatible was the slice of public money that flowed onward from the airport operators to Ryanair and AMS through commercial agreements that no market-rational operator would have signed.

The airport and the airline deals

Nine transactions over ten years — and the one that was not State aid

The 2000 agreement: a rare clean bill of health

Not every Ryanair agreement at Nimes was found to be State aid. The original agreement signed on 11 April 2000 between the CCI and Ryanair set discounted airport charges for a new route to London Stansted with an oral commitment to achieve 70% load factors. The Commission applied the incremental profitability method to this agreement and found positive net incremental flows over the agreement's term. A market-rational operator in the CCI's position at that time would have signed this deal. No State aid (recitals 453–457).

This outcome mattered: it demonstrated that the Commission was not applying a blanket presumption that all discounted airport agreements with Ryanair are unlawful. Each deal had to be assessed on its own economics.

The agreements that were found to constitute State aid began with the 2001/2002 correspondence, which amended the original 2000 deal by adding higher marketing payments that tipped the incremental balance negative. From that point on, every successive agreement deepened the problem.

Airport services agreements

Each agreement set the charges Ryanair paid for landing, passenger handling and ground services. The charge levels were negotiated bilaterally, without any transparent tender process open to other airlines. The agreed charges were typically below the airport's standard tariff, and in some periods the financial incentives (paid per passenger or per rotation) effectively brought the net charge to near zero.

Marketing services agreements (AMS)

In parallel with each airport services agreement, AMS — a Ryanair subsidiary registered in Ireland — signed a marketing services agreement under which it agreed to place advertising for the airport on Ryanair's website. In exchange, the airport operator paid AMS a lump sum or per-flight fee.

The Commission assessed whether these services had genuine market value. It found the payments to AMS for website advertising were not priced at arm's length and formed part of the overall financial package given to Ryanair for operating routes from Nimes.

The November 2010 amendment: coercion or commercial negotiation?

In late 2010 Ryanair threatened to reduce its Liverpool frequencies unless VTAN agreed to pay additional marketing fees of EUR 35,000 to 65,000. VTAN agreed and signed the November 2010 amendment. France argued this was a legitimate commercial renegotiation. The Commission found it was a payment to maintain an existing route, not to develop a new one. Under both the 2005 and 2014 Aviation Guidelines, aid to airlines is only justifiable for genuinely new routes. Payments to preserve existing traffic are operating aid to the airline and are incompatible under the Guidelines.

The Commission’s legal test

Four conditions, a market economy operator test and an incremental profitability model

The four-limb Article 107(1) TFEU test

For any measure to constitute State aid under EU law it must satisfy four cumulative conditions: (1) the measure is granted by the state or through state resources; (2) it confers a selective economic advantage on the beneficiary; (3) it distorts or threatens to distort competition; and (4) it is liable to affect trade between Member States. All four must be present. The Commission applied each limb to the Nimes agreements.

State resources and imputability

CCI period: The CCI is a public authority under French law — an intermediate state body with elected management, public service functions and state supervision. The Commission held that all CCI decisions are automatically imputable to the state. The standard Stardust Marine test (which asks whether a public undertaking's commercial decision would have been taken without state influence) does not apply to bodies that are themselves part of the public administration.

VTAN period: VTAN is privately owned, so state imputability was not automatic. The Commission found imputability through the structure of the CDSP: the SMAN set the traffic-development objective, funded the operating deficit through the flat-rate contribution, and framed the commercial environment in which VTAN dealt with Ryanair. VTAN's own tender documents acknowledged that the Ryanair relationship generated a net cost to the airport. A genuinely independent private operator would not have signed the same agreements.

Joint assessment of agreements

Each round of airport services agreements was signed simultaneously with an AMS marketing services agreement on the same date, for the same period, with the same economic counterparty (Ryanair and AMS are the same corporate group). The marketing agreements stated explicitly that they were conditional on Ryanair maintaining its routes from Nimes.

The Commission therefore assessed each pair as a single economic transaction. Structuring an airport services agreement and a marketing services agreement as separate documents does not prevent the Commission from treating them as one integrated commercial package for State aid analysis.

The Market Economy Operator test: incremental profitability

The central question in every airport-airline State aid case is whether a hypothetical airport operator, motivated by profit and not by public policy goals, would have agreed to the same terms. This is the Market Economy Operator (MEO) test.

For airport-airline agreements the Commission applies the MEO test through an ex-ante incremental profitability analysis: it reconstructs what a profit-motivated operator would have calculated at the time of signing, using only the incremental revenues and costs generated by the specific airline relationship.

  • Incremental revenues: aeronautical charges (landing fees, passenger charges, ground handling) plus all non-aeronautical commercial income attributable to the airline's passengers (retail, parking, food and beverage, advertising). This is the single-till approach — commercial income is credited to the revenue side of the analysis, not excluded from it.
  • Incremental costs: variable operating costs directly linked to serving the flights, plus all marketing payments and financial incentives paid to the airline or AMS. Sunk fixed costs (infrastructure depreciation, runway maintenance) are excluded because they would be incurred regardless of whether Ryanair operated.
  • Benefits beyond the agreement term are excluded unless specifically quantified by the operator at the time of signing. General claims about brand value, tourism spending multipliers or network externalities do not count.

A load factor of 85% was applied for post-October 2005 agreements, reflecting the traffic-boosting effect of the marketing services. Lower actual load factors were used for earlier periods. On these figures, every agreement from 2001/2002 onwards produced a negative net present value. No profit-motivated operator would have signed them.

Distortion of competition and effect on trade

The EU air transport market has been fully open to competition since January 1993. Any financial advantage that reduces Ryanair's costs relative to competing airlines on any intra-EU route strengthens Ryanair's competitive position. The Commission found both conditions satisfied without needing detailed quantification: the market is open, the aid was real, and Ryanair competes on multiple routes against other carriers across the EU.

The Commission rejected France's argument that the small size of Nimes airport (well under 1 million passengers) meant the aid was too minor to affect trade. Even small amounts of recurring State aid give the beneficiary a structural advantage in a competitive market.

The decision and recovery

What was declared compatible, what was found incompatible and who must repay

C
Aid to airport operators — Compatible
All operating subsidies and advances granted to CCI-Airport and VTAN declared compatible with the internal market under Article 107(3)(c) TFEU and the 2014 Aviation Guidelines. Airports under 700,000 passengers per year may receive operating support where genuinely necessary and proportionate. No recovery ordered.
I
Aid to Ryanair and AMS — Incompatible
All airport services and marketing agreements from 2001/2002 onwards declared incompatible. The 2005 Guidelines and 2014 Aviation Guidelines do not permit operating aid to airlines for existing routes, and none of the start-up aid criteria (degressiveness, business plan, transparent tender, net traffic link) were satisfied.
S
Sovereign task financing — Not State aid
Payments for aircraft rescue and firefighting, security screening, border control, wildlife hazard prevention and environmental monitoring through the national airport tax system do not constitute State aid. The system is non-discriminatory, applies uniformly to all French civil airports and contains strict anti-overcompensation controls.
N
2000 Ryanair agreement — Not State aid
The original April 2000 airport services agreement produced positive incremental flows. A market-rational operator would have signed it. No State aid and no recovery for the 2000 deal.
Recovery: who pays and how much

France was ordered to recover the incompatible aid from Ryanair and AMS within four months of notification of the decision (23 July 2014). The recovery obligation under Article 14 of Regulation (EC) No 659/1999 is unconditional: no grounds of legitimate expectation, domestic law or insolvency procedure can override it.

  • Ryanair and AMS jointly and severally: all aid from the October 2005 agreements onwards. Indicative principal: EUR 5 million to 7 million.
  • Ryanair alone: aid from the 2001/2002 and 2004 amendments. Indicative principal: EUR 150,000 to 300,000.
  • Interest: compound interest from the date the aid was placed at the beneficiary's disposal, calculated at the reference rate applicable on that date plus a margin, using the end of each annual calculation period as the reference date (Regulation (EC) No 794/2004).

The exact amounts were to be determined by France during recovery, cross-checked against the Commission's recovery table (Table 17 of the decision) and adjusted for actual versus ex-ante payments.

Why Ryanair alone was liable for the pre-2005 aid

The 2001/2002 correspondence and the 2004 correspondence were signed with Ryanair directly, before the October 2005 agreements that first involved AMS as a formal contracting party. Since AMS was not party to those earlier amendments, it could not be held jointly and severally liable for them. The Commission strictly limited liability to the relevant contracting party for each measure. This matters for any subsequent recovery calculation: the pre-2005 amount (EUR 150,000 to 300,000) is recoverable from Ryanair's balance sheet alone, whereas the post-2005 amounts can be pursued against either Ryanair or AMS.

Compatible operating aid to the airport operators: the five criteria

The Commission applied all five criteria from point 137 of the 2014 Aviation Guidelines to declare the airport operator subsidies compatible:

  • Common interest objective: local economic development, tourism (EUR 100 million annual tourist spending attributed to Nimes airport in 2006 data), 2,200 jobs linked to the airbase, and aviation industry presence.
  • Necessity: without public support the airport would have significantly reduced or ceased operations; small airports under 700,000 passengers typically cannot cover operating costs.
  • Proportionality: aid limited to the verified operating funding gap; French budgetary supervision mechanisms for CCIs prevent overcompensation.
  • Incentive effect: established by the counterfactual — the airport would have contracted without the subsidies.
  • Limited competition distortion: Nimes-Garons handled at most 320,000 passengers; the adjacent Montpellier airport (six times larger) was not materially disadvantaged; no adverse effect on trade contrary to common interest.

What it means for airports and airlines

Practical compliance principles drawn from the decision

Do the maths before signing

Any publicly owned or publicly funded airport must carry out a documented ex-ante incremental profitability analysis before signing any airport services or marketing agreement with an airline. The absence of any analysis is itself a strong indicator that the MEO test was not met.

Treat both agreements as one

If the airport concludes an airport services agreement and a marketing services agreement with the same airline on the same date, the Commission will assess them as a single transaction. The combined net incremental revenue must exceed the combined incremental costs including marketing payments.

New routes need degressiveness

Start-up aid for new routes is compatible only if it is degressive over a maximum of three years, linked to net traffic growth on the new route, supported by a business plan and awarded through a transparent public tender. Aid for existing routes does not qualify.

Maintenance payments are operating aid

If an airline conditions route continuity on additional payments from the airport, those payments are operating aid to the airline, not start-up aid. They are presumptively incompatible under the Aviation Guidelines and cannot be justified on traffic development grounds.

Public body = automatic imputability

A chamber of commerce, local authority body or public institution does not need to show specific government instruction for each commercial decision. All its acts are automatically imputable to the state for State aid purposes. The Stardust Marine test applies only to ordinary public undertakings.

Private operators can also be liable

A private company managing a public service delegation is not automatically shielded from State aid rules. If the conceding authority sets commercial objectives, funds operating losses and shapes the framework for airline relationships, those relationships are imputable to the state and must pass the MEO test.

What counts in the MEO analysis — and what does not

  • Counts: aeronautical charges, non-aeronautical commercial revenues (single-till), incremental variable operating costs, marketing payments, financial incentives per rotation or passenger.
  • Does not count: sunk fixed costs; tourism multiplier effects not quantified ex ante; brand value; route renewal probability; repeat visitor spending; SGEI compensation where the SGEI definition is not properly established.
  • Load factor: must reflect realistic, documented historical or contractual performance. A marketing services package specifically designed to boost passenger numbers may justify a higher assumed load factor from the date it takes effect.

Small airports can receive compatible operating support

The Nimes decision confirms that airports handling under 700,000 passengers per year — which cannot typically cover operating costs on commercial terms — may receive compatible operating support from public authorities. The aid must be: (1) necessary (the airport genuinely cannot cover costs); (2) proportionate (limited to the verified funding gap, no overcompensation); (3) linked to a genuine common interest objective such as connectivity or regional economic development; and (4) limited in competitive distortion (considering the catchment area and proximity of other airports). This derogation applies to support for the airport operator — it does not extend to the operator's below-cost agreements with its airline customers.

Frequently asked questions

Key questions on the Nimes decision and EU airport State aid rules

Why was the 2000 agreement not State aid but the 2001/2002 amendment was?

The Commission applied the same incremental profitability analysis to both. The 2000 agreement, which launched the London Stansted route, generated enough incremental revenue (aeronautical charges plus commercial income from Ryanair's passengers) to cover the incremental costs. A profit-motivated airport operator would have signed it. The 2001/2002 correspondence increased the marketing payments Ryanair received without adding an equivalent increase in revenue. That tipped the net position negative: the additional subsidy to Ryanair was no longer commercially rational.

Did Ryanair benefit from sovereign task financing?

No. The Commission drew a clear line between sovereign task financing (aircraft rescue and firefighting, security screening, border control, wildlife hazard prevention, environmental protection) and commercial financing. Sovereign task payments are not State aid because they cover non-economic public service functions that are not the airport's commercial activities. Ryanair benefited only from the commercial agreements, not from the sovereign task system.

Can a private company managing an airport under a concession escape State aid rules?

Not if the public authority that awarded the concession structures it in a way that shapes the private operator's commercial conduct. In the VTAN case, the SMAN set a traffic-development objective in the CDSP, funded VTAN's operating losses through the flat-rate contribution, and created the economic framework within which Ryanair agreements were negotiated. This was enough for imputability: VTAN's airline agreements were treated as acts of the state, even though VTAN itself was privately owned.

Does the decision mean all low-cost carrier incentive schemes are unlawful?

No. The decision confirms that airport-airline agreements can be entirely lawful if they pass the MEO test: the net present value of incremental revenues must exceed incremental costs over the agreement's term. The original 2000 agreement itself passed that test. The problem at Nimes was that, from 2001/2002 onwards, the marketing payments grew to a level where no rational private airport operator would have agreed to them. Airports that design incentive schemes on commercially rational terms, document the ex-ante profitability analysis, and do not subsidise existing routes are not at risk.

What happens if a Member State does not recover the aid?

If France did not recover the aid within four months, the Commission could refer the matter to the Court of Justice under Article 108(2) TFEU. Non-recovery is itself a separate infringement of EU law. The Court can impose periodic penalty payments. In practice, recovery proceedings are complex: the amounts must be calculated precisely, Ryanair and AMS can challenge the recovery before national courts, and the national courts must apply the decision while making findings of fact on the exact amounts. The Commission monitors compliance and may publish a recovery status update.

Is this decision final?

The decision was adopted by the Commission on 23 July 2014. It was subject to a two-month deadline for challenge before the General Court under Article 263 TFEU from the date of its notification to the parties or its publication in the Official Journal. The decision may have been subject to appeal before the EU courts; any such proceedings do not suspend the recovery obligation unless the court grants an interim suspension. Users should verify the current litigation status directly from the EUR-Lex case file at CELEX 32016D0633.

Key terms

MEO test
The Market Economy Operator test. It asks whether a hypothetical private operator, motivated purely by profit, would have agreed to the same commercial terms. If yes: no State aid. If no: an advantage has been conferred at public expense.
Incremental profitability
The specific methodology used to apply the MEO test to airport-airline agreements. It compares only the revenues and costs that vary with the airline relationship, excluding sunk fixed costs. Introduced in the Aviation Guidelines and confirmed in this decision.
AMS
Airport Marketing Services Limited, a Ryanair subsidiary that contracts with airports to place advertising on Ryanair’s website. In this decision, AMS and Ryanair were treated as one economic entity for State aid purposes.
Single-till
The approach of counting all airport revenues — both aeronautical and non-aeronautical commercial — as part of the same economic pot. In the incremental analysis, non-aeronautical revenue attributable to an airline’s passengers counts on the revenue side of the MEO test.
Imputability
The condition that a measure must be attributable to a public authority for it to constitute State aid. For public bodies like the CCI, imputability is automatic. For private operators under concessions, it depends on the degree of public authority control over the operator’s conduct.
Aviation Guidelines
Commission guidelines on State aid to airports and airlines. The 2005 Guidelines addressed airline start-up aid; the 2014 Guidelines replaced them and introduced the new framework for airport operating aid, including the compatibility criteria applied to CCI-Airport and VTAN in this decision.
