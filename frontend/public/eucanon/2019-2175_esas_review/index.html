The ESAs Review

Regulation (EU) 2019/2175 is the 2019 overhaul of the EU's three financial supervisory authorities. It made EBA the AML/CFT hub for the entire financial sector, gave ESMA direct powers over trading-data providers and critical benchmarks, and equipped all three authorities with sharper supervisory-convergence tools.

Overview

From the de Larosiere crisis response to the 2019 upgrade

The de Larosiere origins and the ESFS

After the 2008 financial crisis, the EU acted on the recommendations of the high-level group chaired by Jacques de Larosiere and built the European System of Financial Supervision (ESFS): a two-pillar structure combining micro-prudential supervision (coordinated by three new European Supervisory Authorities) with macro-prudential oversight (the European Systemic Risk Board, ESRB). The three ESAs became operational in January 2011.

The ESAs are: EBA (European Banking Authority, Regulation (EU) No 1093/2010), responsible for banking and payment services; EIOPA (European Insurance and Occupational Pensions Authority, Regulation (EU) No 1094/2010), responsible for insurance and pensions; and ESMA (European Securities and Markets Authority, Regulation (EU) No 1095/2010), responsible for securities markets. They issue binding technical standards, guidelines, opinions and Q&As, and conduct peer reviews of national supervisors.

Why the 2019 review was needed

The mid-term review of the Capital Markets Union Action Plan concluded that the supervisory system needed upgrading to address two structural forces: sustainable finance and technological innovation. At the same time, a string of major European bank money-laundering scandals between 2017 and 2019 exposed a critical gap: responsibility for AML/CFT supervision was fragmented across all three ESAs and dozens of national authorities, with no single EU body holding the leading role.

Regulation (EU) 2019/2175, adopted on 18 December 2019, is the omnibus amending act that resolves both issues. It is an amending regulation (it does not create a new regime; it rewires six existing ones) and is therefore binding and directly applicable in all Member States without any transposition requirement. Because it amends the founding regulations of the ESAs rather than establishing them afresh, it is often called the ESAs Review. It forms the centrepiece of the ESAs Review package, alongside Regulation (EU) 2019/2176 (amending the ESRB Regulation) and Directive (EU) 2019/2177.

Status: in force and applied

Entry into force: 30 December 2019 (third day after publication in OJ L 334, 27.12.2019). Articles 1, 2, 3 and 6 apply from 1 January 2020. Articles 4 and 5 apply from 1 January 2022. The regulation is currently in force with no sunset clause.

What it covers

Five reform themes across the three authorities

EBA as AML/CFT hub
EBA becomes the leading, coordinating and monitoring authority for anti-money-laundering and counter-terrorist-financing across the entire financial sector, with a central database and the power to push national regulators to investigate.
Supervisory-convergence toolkit
New or strengthened tools for all three ESAs: Union-wide supervisory priorities every three years, structured peer reviews, a public Q&A channel, no-action letters, whistleblower protection, fitness-and-propriety exchange, and coordination groups.
ESMA direct supervision
From 1 January 2022, ESMA directly authorises and supervises data reporting services providers and critical benchmark administrators, replacing national oversight with a single EU-level authority.
Sustainable finance + fintech
ESAs must identify ESG risks, reference the Paris Agreement in stress-test methodologies, and strengthen oversight of technological innovation and training of competent authorities.
Governance + accountability
Independence requirements and conflict-of-interest rules for Board of Supervisors members, a six-week record-of-proceedings obligation to the European Parliament, gender-balanced Management Boards.

The three authorities and the ESFS

EBA, EIOPA and ESMA inside the European System of Financial Supervision

EBA Banking

The European Banking Authority, established by Regulation (EU) No 1093/2010. EBA's remit covers credit institutions, payment institutions, electronic money institutions and investment firms. The ESAs Review added two new statutory objectives: supervisory convergence and AML/CFT prevention. The regulation also created a new Union supervisory handbook, a Union resolution handbook, a Committee on consumer protection and financial innovation, and a temporary product-intervention power. The AML changes (Articles 9a and 9b) took effect from 1 January 2020.

EBA relocated from London to Paris in March 2019 ahead of Brexit; its AML hub role is now operated from Paris.

EIOPA Insurance

The European Insurance and Occupational Pensions Authority, established by Regulation (EU) No 1094/2010. EIOPA's remit covers insurance undertakings, reinsurance undertakings, institutions for occupational retirement provision (IORPs) and insurance intermediaries. The ESAs Review applied the full convergence toolkit to EIOPA (Art 29a priorities, peer reviews, Q&A, no-action letters, whistleblower protection, fitness-and-propriety exchange). EIOPA participates without a vote in EBA's new permanent internal AML committee.

ESMA Securities

The European Securities and Markets Authority, established by Regulation (EU) No 1095/2010. ESMA's remit covers securities markets, market infrastructures, credit rating agencies, trade repositories and investment firms. The ESAs Review extended ESMA's direct supervision to data reporting services providers (from 1 January 2022 via MiFIR) and to critical benchmark administrators (from 1 January 2022 via the Benchmarks Regulation). ESMA also participates without a vote in EBA's AML committee.

The ESFS two-pillar architecture

The ESFS (European System of Financial Supervision) is a network-based system, not a single EU supervisor. It brings together the three ESAs (micro-prudential coordination) with the ESRB (European Systemic Risk Board, macro-prudential oversight) and the national competent authorities of all Member States. Day-to-day supervision of individual institutions remains with national authorities; the ESAs provide binding technical standards, guidelines, Q&As and peer reviews to ensure convergence across the single market. The ESAs Review strengthened this coordination layer without fundamentally altering the network structure.

EBA as the EU's AML/CFT hub

The headline reform of the regulation: Article 1, recitals 13 to 37

Article 9a: special AML/CFT tasks and central database

EBA takes a "leading, coordinating and monitoring role" for AML/CFT across the entire financial sector, including sectors that fall under EIOPA's or ESMA's remit. This was a direct response to the string of European bank money-laundering scandals of 2017 to 2019, which exposed the danger of fragmented responsibility.

Under Article 9a, EBA collects information from competent authorities on AML weaknesses (breaches, potential breaches, inappropriate or ineffective application), develops common AML guidance and technical standards, monitors market developments and assesses risks. It must establish and keep up to date a central AML database of institutions, weaknesses and enforcement actions; RTS defining "weaknesses" and database content were due by 31 December 2020. EBA may transmit evidence to national judicial authorities and to the European Public Prosecutor's Office.

A permanent internal AML committee (Article 9a(7) to (9)) is composed of high-level representatives of every Member State's AML competent authority, one vote per Member State, chaired by an elected voting member. EIOPA and ESMA participate without a vote; the Commission, ESRB and ECB Supervisory Board attend as observers. This committee replaced the former Joint Committee AML subcommittee.

Article 9b: request for investigation (10-working-day rule)

Where EBA has indications of a material breach of AML/CFT law, it may request a national competent authority to investigate the possible breach and to consider sanctions or an individual decision against the financial sector operator. The authority must respond within 10 working days. If the authority fails to respond or acts in a way that is manifestly incompatible with Union law, the breach-of-Union-law procedure under Article 17 is triggered. This gives EBA effective leverage over national supervisors without replacing them as day-to-day supervisors.

Article 81: the AMLA seed

Article 81(2b) directs the Commission, in its review of EBA's AML powers (due by 11 January 2022), to "thoroughly investigate the possibility of conferring specific tasks with regard to the prevention and countering of money laundering or of terrorist financing on an existing or new dedicated EU-wide agency." This is the explicit textual seed of the later EU Anti-Money Laundering Authority (AMLA), which is established by separate subsequent legislation and is not part of this regulation.

The concentration of the AML mandate in EBA and the provisional framing of the powers (recital 37) together set the institutional trajectory that eventually produced the dedicated AMLA.

New supervisory-convergence toolkit

Eight new or strengthened tools mirrored across all three ESAs

Art 29a: Union strategic supervisory priorities

At least every three years, by 31 March, each ESA identifies up to two Union-wide supervisory priorities that national competent authorities must reflect in their own work programmes. This creates a structured annual dialogue between each ESA and its national counterparts on what matters most for supervisory convergence across the EU in the coming period.

Art 30: structured peer reviews

Replaces the old peer review mechanism with structured ad hoc peer review committees: ESA staff plus members of national competent authorities, chaired by ESA staff. Reviews assess resources, independence, governance, convergence, best practices and enforcement. Reports are adopted by the Board of Supervisors; a follow-up report is published after two years; main findings are made public.

Art 16b: questions and answers (Q&A)

A web-based Q&A tool open to any natural or legal person, in any official EU language. Answers are non-binding; questions requiring interpretation of Union law are forwarded to the Commission. The Q&A channel provides operational guidance without requiring a formal guidelines process.

Art 9c: no-action letters

In exceptional circumstances (conflicting acts, missing delegated or implementing acts, or missing guidelines), the ESA notifies competent authorities and the Commission in writing and issues a public opinion, effectively signalling that enforcement action would be premature. This tool addresses the supervisory uncertainty that can arise when the legislative framework has gaps.

Art 17a: protection of reporting persons

Dedicated whistleblower channels within each ESA, with anonymity and confidentiality guarantees and protection from retaliation in line with the Whistleblower Directive (EU) 2019/1937. This brings the ESAs into the same protective framework as other Union institutions.

Art 31a: fitness and propriety exchange

The three ESAs jointly establish a system to exchange information on the fitness and propriety assessments of qualifying-holding holders, directors and key function holders. This facilitates cross-border supervisory intelligence on individuals in a way that was not systematically possible before.

Art 45b: coordination groups

A new tool: the Management Board may set up coordination groups on defined topics, with mandatory participation by all relevant competent authorities. This provides a flexible mechanism for the Management Board to convene cross-border supervisory dialogue without requiring a full Board of Supervisors process.

Art 33: equivalence monitoring

Each ESA monitors the third-country regulatory and supervisory regimes underpinning Commission equivalence decisions and submits a confidential annual report. ESAs may not conclude administrative arrangements with authorities of countries on the Commission's AML high-risk list.

ESMA gains direct supervisory powers (from 1 January 2022)

Articles 4 and 5: MiFIR and Benchmarks Regulation changes

Data Reporting Services Providers (via MiFIR, Article 4)

Authorisation and supervision of data reporting services providers (approved publication arrangements, consolidated tape providers, approved reporting mechanisms) are transferred from national competent authorities to ESMA from 1 January 2022, except for providers benefiting from a derogation. ESMA gains direct data-gathering powers from market participants and the power to conduct investigations and on-site inspections.

ESMA may impose fines on data reporting services providers up to a maximum of EUR 200,000. Periodic penalty payments may reach 3% of average daily turnover (2% of average daily income for natural persons) for up to six months. An independent investigation officer handles the procedural phase; the Court of Justice has unlimited jurisdiction to review fines.

ESMA charges fees to DRSPs to cover its supervisory costs (delegated act due 1 October 2021); RTS on management-body suitability were due 1 January 2021. Applications received before 1 October 2021 are handled by national authorities; files are transferred on 1 January 2022. If the market fails to produce a consolidated tape, ESMA may appoint one through a public-procurement fallback.

Critical benchmarks and third-country administrators (via Benchmarks Regulation, Article 5)

Certain critical benchmarks (such as EURIBOR) are supervised at Union level by ESMA, which becomes the competent authority for critical-benchmark administrators under Article 40(1) of the Benchmarks Regulation. ESMA also becomes the single authority for recognising third-country benchmark administrators, replacing the cumbersome "Member State of reference" determination.

ESMA may impose fines on benchmark administrators: up to EUR 1,000,000 or 10% of total annual turnover (whichever is higher) for legal persons; up to EUR 500,000 for natural persons (lower caps apply for specific categories of infringement). Periodic penalties reach 3% of average daily turnover. Multiple RTS were due by 1 October 2020.

ESMA must process third-country recognition applications within 90 working days. Third-country administrators must appoint a legal representative in the Union. ESMA may not recognise administrators from countries on the Commission's AML high-risk list unless cooperative arrangements are in place.

Sustainable finance and fintech mandate

ESG risks, Paris Agreement and technological innovation: recitals 7 to 10

ESG risks and the Paris Agreement

The ESAs are tasked to identify and report risks that environmental, social and governance (ESG) factors pose to financial stability. The Joint Committee monitoring system must assess material ESG risks "taking into account the Paris Agreement" (Art 29(1)(f)). Environmental risk is built into the Union-wide stress-testing methodologies (Arts 23, 32), so that scenarios reflecting climate-related physical and transition risks can be assessed alongside traditional macro-financial scenarios.

The ESAs are also required to provide guidance on embedding sustainability considerations in EU financial legislation, supporting the broader EU Sustainable Finance Action Plan.

Fintech and technological innovation

Each ESA's oversight role on technological innovation is strengthened. This includes monitoring of market developments in fintech, training of national competent authorities on digital and technological risks, and integration of technology-related risks into the supervisory convergence agenda. The expanded mandate reflects the period's concern that supervisory authorities needed to keep pace with the rapid digitalisation of financial services.

Governance and accountability changes

Board of Supervisors, Management Board and transparency obligations

Art 42: Board of Supervisors independence

Members of the Board of Supervisors must act independently and in the interests of the Union as a whole. They must declare conflicts of interest and abstain from voting where a conflict exists. This was a direct response to concerns that ESA decisions were too heavily influenced by the home-country positions of national supervisors.

Art 43a: transparency towards the EP

Within six weeks of each Board of Supervisors meeting, the ESA must provide the European Parliament with a comprehensive record of the proceedings. This strengthens parliamentary accountability over regulatory decisions taken at ESA level, which were previously not systematically communicated to the Parliament in a timely way.

Arts 45 and 45a: Management Board

The Management Board is composed of the Chairperson plus six members elected from the Board of Supervisors. Terms run for two-and-a-half years, renewable once. The composition must be gender-balanced and include at least two representatives from Member States that do not participate in the Banking Union or the equivalent institutional arrangements. Decision-making panels (Art 41) for breach-of-Union-law and binding-mediation cases are composed of the Chairperson plus six members, requiring four votes to decide.

Key terms defined

Central concepts used in the regulation

ESFS
European System of Financial Supervision: the network comprising the three ESAs (EBA, EIOPA, ESMA), the ESRB and the national competent authorities. Established after the de Larosiere recommendations in the wake of the 2008 financial crisis.
ESA
European Supervisory Authority: any of EBA, EIOPA or ESMA. Each is an EU agency with legal personality, financed jointly by the EU budget and contributions from national competent authorities.
AML/CFT
Anti-money-laundering and countering the financing of terrorism. Under the ESAs Review, EBA holds the leading, coordinating and monitoring role for the entire financial sector, even where individual operators fall under EIOPA's or ESMA's sectoral remit.
Data Reporting Services Provider (DRSP)
A provider of one or more data reporting services under MiFIR: an approved publication arrangement (APA), a consolidated tape provider (CTP), or an approved reporting mechanism (ARM). DRSPs were supervised at national level before 1 January 2022 and are supervised directly by ESMA from that date.
Critical benchmark
A benchmark designated as critical by the Commission under the Benchmarks Regulation (EU) 2016/1011 because its failure or cessation could significantly impact financial stability, market integrity or the real economy. EURIBOR is the leading example. Critical benchmark administrators are supervised directly by ESMA from 1 January 2022.
Financial sector operator
A term introduced by the ESAs Review (Art 4(1a) of the EBA Regulation) to describe any institution or entity subject to AML/CFT law whose supervision or oversight may involve EBA's leading role, even if the entity falls within EIOPA's or ESMA's primary sectoral remit.

Glossary

Abbreviations and technical terms used on this page

EBA
European Banking Authority, established by Regulation (EU) No 1093/2010. Responsible for banking, payments and AML/CFT convergence across the EU financial sector.
EIOPA
European Insurance and Occupational Pensions Authority, established by Regulation (EU) No 1094/2010. Responsible for insurance undertakings, reinsurance and institutions for occupational retirement provision.
ESMA
European Securities and Markets Authority, established by Regulation (EU) No 1095/2010. Responsible for securities markets, CCPs, trade repositories, CRAs and (from 2022) DRSPs and critical benchmark administrators.
ESRB
European Systemic Risk Board: the macro-prudential pillar of the ESFS, responsible for monitoring systemic risk in the EU financial system. Amended by the companion Regulation (EU) 2019/2176.
MiFIR
Markets in Financial Instruments Regulation, Regulation (EU) No 600/2014. Governs transparency and market structure for financial instruments. Article 4 of the ESAs Review amended MiFIR to transfer DRSP oversight to ESMA from 1 January 2022.
Benchmarks Regulation
Regulation (EU) 2016/1011 on indices used as benchmarks in financial instruments and contracts. Article 5 of the ESAs Review amended it to give ESMA direct supervisory jurisdiction over critical benchmarks and third-country administrator recognition.
TFEU
Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union. Article 114 (internal market approximation) is the legal basis for Regulation (EU) 2019/2175.
AMLA
Anti-Money Laundering Authority: a dedicated EU-level AML supervisory body established by separate subsequent legislation. The ESAs Review did NOT create AMLA; it planted the textual seed in Art 81(2b) that led to the later dedicated regulation.
DRSP
Data Reporting Services Provider: an approved publication arrangement, consolidated tape provider or approved reporting mechanism under MiFIR. Supervised directly by ESMA from 1 January 2022 under Article 4 of the ESAs Review.

Capital Requirements Regulation (CRR, Regulation (EU) No 575/2013)
The ESAs Review reshapes the supervisors; CRR is the substantive prudential rulebook that those supervisors apply. CRR sets the capital, liquidity and leverage requirements for credit institutions. EBA plays a central role in building CRR technical standards, issuing Q&As and conducting peer reviews of national supervisors' application of CRR. The two regulations are complementary: the ESAs Review defines the institutional architecture; CRR defines the rules within that architecture for the banking sector.
