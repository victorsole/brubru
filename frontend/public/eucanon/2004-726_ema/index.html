The EMA Regulation

Overview

The institutional spine of EU medicines regulation since 2005

From the EMEA to the EMA

Before Regulation (EC) No 726/2004 came into force, the European Agency for the Evaluation of Medicinal Products (EMEA) operated under Regulation (EEC) No 2309/93. The 2004 regulation repealed that earlier instrument (Art 88), renamed the Agency the European Medicines Agency (EMA) (recital 2), and fundamentally restructured the legal framework for the authorisation of medicines across the EU and the EEA.

The central innovation of 726/2004 is the centralised marketing authorisation procedure: a single application submitted to the EMA, a single scientific opinion from the Committee for Medicinal Products for Human Use (CHMP), and a single Commission decision granting a marketing authorisation that is valid in every Member State simultaneously (Art 13). Before the centralised procedure existed, a pharmaceutical company seeking EU-wide market access had to file separate national applications, country by country. The regulation ends that fragmentation for the products within its scope.

The regulation does not affect Member States' powers over pricing, reimbursement, or how medicines are dispensed within their healthcare systems (Art 1, second paragraph). Authorisation is a necessary precondition for market access, but national health technology assessment bodies retain control over whether and how a product is reimbursed.

Legal bases

The regulation rests on two TEC legal bases (now renumbered as TFEU articles following the Lisbon Treaty):

  • Article 95 TEC (now Art 114 TFEU): internal market approximation, which justified the creation of a single authorisation procedure to eliminate the distortions caused by divergent national authorisation decisions for innovative products.
  • Article 152(4)(b) TEC (now Art 168(4)(b) TFEU): public health measures on veterinary and phytosanitary matters, which anchors the quality, safety and efficacy assessment framework.

The co-decision procedure under Art 251 TEC applied, with EP President Pat Cox and Irish Council Presidency Minister Micheal Martin as signatories (31 March 2004, Strasbourg).

Entry into force and application

The regulation was published in OJ L 136 on 30 April 2004. It entered into force on that date. Titles I through V applied from 20 November 2005. The EMA as reconstituted under this regulation assumed its new name and governance structure on the same date.

As amended, the regulation has been modified several times: most significantly by the 2010 pharmacovigilance package (Reg (EU) 1235/2010 + Dir 2010/84/EU), which created the Pharmacovigilance Risk Assessment Committee (PRAC) and the EudraVigilance obligations; by Reg (EU) 2019/5 (the omnibus regulation); and by Reg (EU) 2022/123, which reinforced the EMA's mandate on medicine shortages and added the Medicines Shortages Steering Group (MSSG) and the Emergency Task Force (ETF).

Mandatory scope: the Annex

Products that may only be placed on the EU market via the centralised procedure (Art 3)

1
Biotechnology products
Products derived from recombinant DNA technology, controlled gene expression of biologically active proteins, or hybridoma and monoclonal antibody methods. The foundational biotech basket, covering insulin, erythropoietin, monoclonal antibodies, recombinant growth factors and equivalent biologics.
2
Veterinary performance enhancers
Veterinary medicinal products intended primarily for use as performance enhancers in order to promote growth or increase yields in treated animals. Mandatory centralised route to prevent fragmented national decisions on these sensitive products. (Largely superseded for veterinary medicines by Reg (EU) 2019/6.)
3
New human active substances: four disease areas
Human new active substances for AIDS, cancer, neurodegenerative disorders, and diabetes (applicable from the date of entry into force). From 20 May 2008, the scope extended to auto-immune diseases, other immune dysfunctions, and viral diseases.
4
Orphan medicinal products
All medicinal products that have been designated as orphan medicinal products pursuant to Regulation (EC) No 141/2000. The centralised route is the mandatory authorisation pathway for all orphan drugs, connecting directly to the COMP-based designation process.

Optional access (Art 3(2))

Beyond the mandatory Annex, applicants may use the centralised procedure on an optional basis for: (a) medicinal products containing a new active substance not yet authorised in the Community; or (b) products that represent a significant therapeutic, scientific or technical innovation, or for which granting a Community-level authorisation would be in the interest of patients or animal health at Community level.

Advanced Therapy Medicinal Products (ATMPs) were later added to the mandatory scope of the centralised procedure by Regulation (EC) No 1394/2007, which also created the Committee for Advanced Therapies (CAT) within the EMA.

Title II: Human medicines and the CHMP

Arts 5 to 29: the Committee for Medicinal Products for Human Use and the authorisation procedure

The CHMP (Art 5)

Title II establishes the Committee for Medicinal Products for Human Use (CHMP) as the EMA's principal scientific body for human medicines. The CHMP is responsible for preparing the Agency's opinions on all questions concerning the admissibility of applications, the granting, variation, suspension or withdrawal of marketing authorisations for human medicinal products. It may also give opinions on any question relating to the evaluation of human medicines, and carries out scientific advice work for applicants and the Commission.

Committee composition mirrors the Management Board structure: one member per Member State with one alternate, plus up to five co-opted experts for particular therapeutic areas, appointed on a three-year renewable basis (Art 61). Members must be free of any financial interest in the pharmaceutical industry (Art 63, declarations of interest).

The application (Art 6)

An application for a centralised marketing authorisation must be submitted to the EMA. The content mirrors the requirements of Directive 2001/83/EC and must include results of pharmaceutical, pre-clinical and clinical tests, a risk-management system description, and, where the product contains or consists of a genetically modified organism, an environmental risk assessment. The MA holder must be established in the Community (Art 2).

The CHMP assigns a rapporteur and co-rapporteur from among its members (Art 62). These two members lead the scientific assessment and draft the assessment report that the full Committee then deliberates.

The 210-day clock

Arts 6(3) to 10: from valid application to Commission decision

Assessment timeline (Art 6(3))

Once the CHMP declares an application valid, the clock starts. The Committee must reach its opinion within 210 days of receiving the valid application. The scientific analysis by the rapporteur and co-rapporteur must last at least 80 days. During that initial phase the clock may be "stopped" for questions addressed to the applicant; the clock restarts when the answer is received. These clock stops are a standard feature of EMA assessments and routinely extend the calendar time, even though the 210-day procedural deadline itself remains fixed.

Accelerated assessment: 150 days (Art 14(9))

For medicinal products of major public-health interest, particularly those offering therapeutic innovation, the CHMP may at the request of an applicant agree to an accelerated assessment procedure. Under the accelerated procedure, the CHMP must reach its opinion within 150 days from receipt of the valid application. The Committee evaluates whether the conditions for granting accelerated status are met before confirming the shorter timeline.

Re-examination (Art 9(2))

If the CHMP delivers a negative opinion, or conditions the opinion, the applicant may request a re-examination within 15 days of receiving the opinion. The grounds for re-examination must be submitted within 60 days. The CHMP must then re-examine its opinion within a further 60 days. The re-examination may be supported by an expert consultation. A final opinion confirming or revising the initial opinion is then issued.

Commission decision (Art 10)

Within 15 days of receiving the CHMP opinion, the Commission drafts its decision on the marketing authorisation. Member States have 22 days to comment. The Commission then issues its decision in the Standing Committee on Medicinal Products for Human Use (comitology). The Commission decision is directly applicable across all Member States and EEA states (Art 13). A summary of product characteristics (SmPC), product labelling and package leaflet, as adopted, are attached to the decision.

The Community Register and the EPAR (Art 13)

The Commission maintains the Community Register of Medicinal Products, which records every centrally authorised product. For each product, the EMA publishes a European Public Assessment Report (EPAR) containing the CHMP's scientific assessment and the summary of product characteristics. The EPAR is publicly available on the EMA website, making the scientific basis for every EU centralised authorisation decision transparent to healthcare professionals and patients.

Marketing authorisation types and data protection

Art 14: standard, conditional, exceptional circumstances, and the 8+2+1 formula

Standard marketing authorisation: 5 years then unlimited (Art 14(1)-(3))

A standard marketing authorisation is granted for five years. Before expiry the MA holder applies for renewal, at which point the CHMP re-evaluates the risk-benefit balance. If the renewal is granted, the authorisation becomes unlimited in duration (Art 14(2)-(3)), although it remains subject to annual re-examination if the Commission or the CHMP so requests on public-health grounds.

Sunset clause: 3-year rule (Art 14(4)-(5))

A marketing authorisation lapses automatically if the authorised product is not actually placed on the market within 3 years of the granting decision. Equally, if a product that was previously marketed is withdrawn from all EU markets and remains absent for 3 consecutive years, the authorisation lapses. The sunset clause prevents "evergreening" of unused authorisations and keeps the register current. Exemptions may be granted in exceptional circumstances, notably for public-health reasons.

Regulatory data protection: the 8+2+1 formula (Art 14(11))

Article 14(11) sets out the single most commercially significant incentive in EU medicines law. A reference medicinal product authorised via the centralised procedure benefits from:

  • 8 years of data exclusivity: generic or biosimilar applicants may not use the reference product's data to support their own applications during this period.
  • 2 additional years of market protection (totalling 10 years): even if a generic applicant has compiled its own dossier, the generic may not be placed on the market for 10 years from the initial authorisation of the reference product.
  • +1 further year of market protection if, within the first 8 years, the MA holder obtains an authorisation for a new therapeutic indication that brings significant clinical benefit compared with existing therapies.

The maximum combined protection period is therefore 11 years. This formula is frequently called the "8+2+1" rule. The 2023 pharma reform (COM(2023)193) proposed recalibrating this baseline, which became EFPIA's headline lobbying priority during the 2024-2025 legislative negotiations.

Conditional marketing authorisation (Art 14(7))

For products addressing unmet medical needs, including medicines for serious/life-threatening diseases, an MA may be granted on the basis of less complete data than normally required, subject to specific obligations (conditions) to complete clinical studies post-authorisation. Conditional MAs are valid for 1 year and renewable annually until the conditions are fulfilled, at which point a standard MA is issued.

Exceptional-circumstances authorisation (Art 14(8))

Where the applicant can demonstrate that comprehensive data cannot be provided on scientific grounds or because it is contrary to generally accepted principles of medical ethics, an authorisation may be granted subject to specific procedures involving enhanced monitoring and annual re-evaluation. Unlike conditional MAs, exceptional-circumstances authorisations do not anticipate that the specific obligations will eventually be resolved by completion of further studies.

Pharmacovigilance

Arts 23 to 29: post-authorisation safety monitoring for human medicines

Qualified person for pharmacovigilance (Art 23)

The MA holder must have a qualified person responsible for pharmacovigilance who is permanently and continuously available. This person must be resident and operating in the Community and is responsible for the establishment and maintenance of the pharmacovigilance system, including the pharmacovigilance system master file, the risk management system, and the submission of periodic safety update reports.

Adverse-reaction reporting: 15-day clock (Arts 24, 25)

The MA holder must report all suspected serious adverse reactions occurring within the Community and in third countries to the EMA and to the Member State on whose territory the reaction occurred within 15 days of receipt of the information (Art 24). Non-serious adverse reactions must be reported within 90 days. This 15-day clock applies to both fatal and life-threatening reactions and any other serious unexpected reactions.

Periodic Safety Update Reports (PSURs) must be submitted every 6 months for the first two years after initial authorisation, then annually for the following three years, and thereafter every three years (Art 24(3)). The CHMP evaluates PSURs; the PRAC (added by Reg 1235/2010) leads the safety assessment.

EudraVigilance and WHO cooperation (Arts 24, 27)

The EMA maintains the EudraVigilance database, the central EU repository for adverse reaction reports from MA holders, national competent authorities and healthcare professionals. Among the approximately 19 tasks listed for the EMA in Art 57, the establishment, maintenance and coordination of the adverse-reaction database is explicitly enumerated. Art 27 requires the EMA to cooperate with the World Health Organization on pharmacovigilance, providing the WHO with appropriate information on adverse reaction reports submitted to EudraVigilance.

Title III: Veterinary medicines and the CVMP

Arts 30 to 54: mirror architecture for animal medicines (as amended)

The CVMP and the veterinary centralised procedure

Title III of 726/2004 established a mirror architecture for veterinary medicines, centred on the Committee for Medicinal Products for Veterinary Use (CVMP). The procedure, timelines, MA types and pharmacovigilance obligations for veterinary products ran parallel to the human medicines framework of Title II, adapted for veterinary-specific requirements such as maximum residue limits for food-producing animals.

Carved out by Regulation (EU) 2019/6 (as amended)

The veterinary medicines framework in Title III of 726/2004 was largely superseded by Regulation (EU) 2019/6 on veterinary medicinal products, which became applicable on 28 January 2022. Reg 2019/6 repealed most of the veterinary provisions that had existed in 726/2004 and created a self-standing modern framework for veterinary medicines, including the centralised procedure for veterinary products, maximum residue limits, and antimicrobial resistance provisions. The CVMP remains within the EMA structure, but its operating rules now derive primarily from Reg 2019/6.

Title IV: The European Medicines Agency

Arts 55 to 80: the Agency's structure, tasks, governance and financing

Establishment and location (Art 55)

Article 55 formally establishes the European Medicines Agency as a Community body with legal personality. Its budget is drawn from the Community budget and from fees paid by undertakings seeking authorisation of medicinal products. The Agency's headquarters were originally in London. Following the United Kingdom's withdrawal from the European Union, the EMA relocated to Amsterdam, the Netherlands, where it has been based since 1 January 2019.

The EMA does not itself grant marketing authorisations. The Commission issues the decision on the basis of the CHMP opinion. The EMA's role is to coordinate the scientific assessment and to provide the scientific opinion on which the Commission decision rests.

Agency tasks: approximately 19 enumerated functions (Art 57)

Article 57 sets out the EMA's functions in detail. Among the approximately 19 tasks listed, the key ones are:

  • Coordinating the scientific evaluation of medicinal products for human and veterinary use within the centralised procedure
  • Providing scientific advice to companies researching new medicinal products
  • Publishing EPARs and maintaining the Community Register for each authorised product
  • Establishing and maintaining the EudraVigilance database for adverse reactions
  • Giving advice on questions relating to the use of antibiotics in food-producing animals and antimicrobial resistance (AMR)
  • Maintaining a public EU medicines database, including a section on paediatric medicines
  • Compiling information on suspected biological warfare agents, pathogens and vaccines, and advising on measures to counter biological threats
  • Giving scientific opinions for the WHO on the quality, safety and efficacy of medicinal products for use in countries outside the EU (Art 58)
Executive Director (Art 64)

The Executive Director is appointed by the Management Board on a Commission proposal, for a term of 5 years, renewable once. The Director is the legal representative of the Agency and is responsible for the day-to-day administration, the budget, staff management, and for ensuring that the committees function as required. The Director must make a statement to the European Parliament. The current Executive Director is Emer Cooke (Ireland).

Management Board (Art 65)

The Management Board is the Agency's governing body. Its composition (Art 65) includes: one representative per Member State, two representatives of the European Commission, two representatives of the European Parliament, two representatives of patients' organisations, one representative of doctors' organisations, and one representative of veterinarians' organisations. Members serve three-year renewable terms. The Management Board takes decisions by a two-thirds majority for significant matters. It adopts the annual budget, the work programme, and the rules of procedure.

Budget and fees (Arts 67, 70)

The EMA budget consists of a contribution from the EU general budget, fees paid by pharmaceutical companies for centralised procedure services, and other revenues. Pharmacovigilance, networks and surveillance activities are financed from the public contribution (Art 67). Fees are set by the Council (Art 70). SME reductions are available for smaller companies. Fee governance was updated most recently by Regulation (EU) 2024/568, which established a new EMA fee system. Public access to documents is governed by Regulation (EC) No 1049/2001 (Art 73).

The 7 scientific committees

Art 56: the institutional backbone of EMA's scientific work (as amended)

Original 4 committees (2004 text) and 3 added later

Regulation 726/2004 as adopted established four scientific committees within the EMA (Art 56): the CHMP, the CVMP, the COMP (Committee for Orphan Medicinal Products, cross-referenced from Reg 141/2000) and the HMPC (Committee on Herbal Medicinal Products). Three further committees were added by sibling regulations enacted after 2004. Together they cover the full range of medicinal products and patient populations reviewed by the EMA.

CHMP

Committee for Medicinal Products for Human Use. The EMA's principal committee for human medicines. Issues opinions on all centralised marketing authorisation applications, scientific advice, referrals, and compassionate use programmes. Composition: 1 member per Member State + up to 5 co-opted experts + alternates.

CVMP

Committee for Medicinal Products for Veterinary Use. Mirror of the CHMP for veterinary medicines. Issues opinions under the centralised procedure for veterinary products, maximum residue limits, and scientific advice on veterinary topics. Operating rules updated by Reg (EU) 2019/6.

COMP

Committee for Orphan Medicinal Products. Evaluates applications for orphan designation under Reg 141/2000. Designation by the Commission is a precondition for the orphan MA incentives (mandatory centralised procedure, 10-year market exclusivity, fee reductions). Cross-referenced from Reg 141/2000 into Art 56 of 726/2004.

HMPC

Committee on Herbal Medicinal Products. Established by Directive 2004/24/EC. Compiles European Union monographs for herbal substances and preparations, establishes the Community list of herbal substances for traditional herbal medicinal products, and advises on herbal pharmacovigilance.

PRAC

Pharmacovigilance Risk Assessment Committee. Added by Reg (EU) 1235/2010 (the 2010 pharmacovigilance package). Assesses and monitors safety issues for human medicines, leads PSUR evaluations, and recommends risk-minimisation measures including label changes, restricted indications and market withdrawals.

PDCO

Paediatric Committee. Added by Reg (EC) No 1901/2006 (the Paediatric Regulation). Reviews paediatric investigation plans (PIPs), evaluates results of PIP-compliant studies, and recommends whether paediatric reward extensions (PUMA + 6-month SPC extension) are warranted.

CAT

Committee for Advanced Therapies. Added by Reg (EC) No 1394/2007 (the ATMP Regulation). Evaluates the quality, safety and efficacy of advanced therapy medicinal products (gene therapies, somatic cell therapies, tissue-engineered products) and prepares draft opinions for the CHMP to adopt.

Title V: General and final provisions

Arts 81 to 90: reasoned decisions, compassionate use, penalties and the repeal

Reasoned decisions and one-per-applicant rule (Arts 81, 82)

All decisions, opinions and assessments must be accompanied by a reasoned scientific explanation (Art 81). This requirement underpins the transparency commitments of the centralised procedure: the grounds for granting, refusing or revoking an authorisation must be articulated in writing, are publicly available via the EPAR, and can be challenged before the EU Courts. Art 82 provides that only one authorisation may be granted to the same MA holder for the same product, preventing duplicate applications.

Compassionate use (Art 83)

Member States may make an unauthorised medicinal product available for compassionate use to patients with a chronically or seriously debilitating disease or a life-threatening condition where there is no satisfactory authorised treatment option, and where the product is either the subject of a centralised application or is undergoing clinical trials. The CHMP may adopt opinions on the conditions of use, distribution and the patients targeted. These CHMP opinions are not binding on Member States but provide a common framework.

Penalties and Commission financial penalties (Art 84)

The Commission, acting on a proposal from the EMA, may impose financial penalties on holders of marketing authorisations for human and veterinary medicinal products granted under the centralised procedure if those holders fail to comply with obligations imposed on them. The regime covers non-compliance with conditions attached to authorisations, obligations under risk-management systems, failure to submit required studies, and other regulatory obligations. The rules on the scale and enforcement of these penalties are set out in separate Commission regulations enacted under the comitology procedure (Art 87).

10-yearly Commission report and comitology (Arts 86, 87)

The Commission must submit a report to the European Parliament and the Council on the functioning of the centralised procedure every 10 years (Art 86). This report provides the evidence base for any legislative revision. The Commission is assisted by the Standing Committee on Medicinal Products for Human Use and the Standing Committee on Veterinary Medicinal Products in the exercise of its implementing powers (Art 87, comitology). Regulation (EEC) No 2309/93 was formally repealed by Art 88, with transitional provisions.

Key definitions

Title I (Arts 1-4): core concepts as used in the regulation

Definitions per Directives 2001/83/EC and 2001/82/EC

Article 1 states that the definitions in Directive 2001/83/EC (human medicinal products) and Directive 2001/82/EC (veterinary medicinal products) apply throughout the regulation. The regulation does not redefine "medicinal product" or "marketing authorisation" but incorporates those definitions by cross-reference, ensuring coherence with the broader EU pharmaceutical acquis. Key operative terms introduced or applied by 726/2004 include:

  • Centralised procedure: the single application-CHMP opinion-Commission decision pathway created by Title II of 726/2004, producing an authorisation valid across all Member States.
  • Marketing authorisation holder (MA holder): the legal entity established in the Community in whose name the Commission authorisation is granted. The holder bears post-authorisation obligations including pharmacovigilance, variations, and renewal (Art 2).
  • Reference medicinal product: for the purpose of Art 14(11), the innovator product whose data the generic or biosimilar applicant seeks to rely on. The 8+2+1 clock runs from the initial authorisation of this reference product.
  • Rapporteur / co-rapporteur: the CHMP members designated to lead the scientific assessment of a given application (Art 62). They draft the assessment reports on which the full committee's opinion is based.
  • Orphan designation: designation granted by the Commission on a COMP opinion under Reg 141/2000 as a precondition for orphan-specific incentives, including the mandatory centralised pathway under 726/2004.

Legislative timeline

Key milestones for Regulation 726/2004 and its family

1993
Regulation (EEC) No 2309/93 adopted, creating the EMEA (European Agency for the Evaluation of Medicinal Products) and the first centralised procedure. The institutional predecessor of 726/2004.
31 March 2004
Regulation (EC) No 726/2004 adopted in Strasbourg. Published in OJ L 136 on 30 April 2004. Renames the EMEA as the European Medicines Agency (EMA) and creates the new centralised procedure with the CHMP, CVMP, COMP and HMPC.
20 November 2005
Titles I through V of Regulation 726/2004 become fully applicable. EMA assumes its new name, governance and regulatory mandate under the regulation.
30 December 2006
Regulation (EC) No 1901/2006 (Paediatric Medicines Regulation) enters into force. Adds the PDCO to the EMA's committee structure (Art 56 of 726/2004 as amended), establishes paediatric investigation plans and the paediatric-use marketing authorisation (PUMA).
13 December 2007
Regulation (EC) No 1394/2007 (ATMP Regulation) adopted. Adds the CAT to the EMA and brings advanced therapy medicinal products into the mandatory scope of the centralised procedure.
2 July 2010
Regulation (EU) No 1235/2010 (pharmacovigilance package) adopted, alongside Directive 2010/84/EU. Adds the PRAC to the EMA, establishes the EudraVigilance obligations, and introduces the risk management system requirements.
25 October 2012
Regulation (EU) No 1027/2012 amends 726/2004 on pharmacovigilance provisions, aligning procedural rules between 726/2004 and the amendments to Directive 2001/83/EC.
1 November 2014
EMA announces its intention to relocate from London as UK Brexit negotiations begin. Decision on new host city follows later.
20 January 2019
EMA formally transfers its headquarters to Amsterdam, the Netherlands, in preparation for the UK's departure from the European Union (29 March 2019).
27 January 2019
Regulation (EU) 2019/5 (omnibus amending regulation) adopted, updating 726/2004 alongside amendments to several other pharmaceutical directives and regulations to improve the functioning of the internal market for medicines.
28 January 2022
Regulation (EU) 2019/6 on veterinary medicinal products becomes applicable, effectively replacing most of Title III of 726/2004 for veterinary medicines.
23 February 2022
Regulation (EU) 2022/123 adopted, reinforcing the EMA's role on medicine shortages and critical medical devices. Creates the Medicines Shortages Steering Group (MSSG) and the Emergency Task Force (ETF) within the EMA.
6 March 2024
Regulation (EU) 2024/568 adopted, establishing a new EMA fee system and replacing earlier fee governance provisions.
26 April 2023
Commission adopts COM(2023)193, the proposed new pharmaceutical regulation to replace 726/2004. Rapporteur: Tiemo Wolken (MEP). Among other things proposes recalibrating the 8+2+1 data-protection baseline.
11 December 2025
Provisional agreement reached between the European Parliament and Council on the proposed new pharmaceutical regulation (COM(2023)193), which once formally adopted and published will replace Regulation 726/2004.

Glossary

Key abbreviations and terms used throughout this page

EMA
European Medicines Agency. Decentralised EU agency based in Amsterdam since 2019. Coordinates scientific assessment of medicines for the EU market; the Commission issues the formal authorisation decision on EMA's CHMP opinion.
CHMP
Committee for Medicinal Products for Human Use. Principal EMA scientific body for human medicines. One member per Member State plus up to 5 co-opted experts. Issues opinions on centralised authorisation applications within the 210-day (or 150-day accelerated) clock.
Centralised procedure
Single application to EMA, single CHMP opinion, single Commission decision valid EEA-wide. Mandatory for biotech products, orphan drugs, ATMP and new active substances in key disease areas. Optional for other innovative products.
EPAR
European Public Assessment Report. Published by the EMA for every centrally authorised product, setting out the CHMP scientific assessment, the SmPC and the basis for the Commission decision. Publicly accessible on the EMA website.
8+2+1
Regulatory data-protection formula in Art 14(11): 8 years data exclusivity + 10 years market protection (2 additional years over data excl.) + up to 1 further year for a significant new indication. Maximum 11 years total. EFPIA's central commercial priority.
PRAC
Pharmacovigilance Risk Assessment Committee. Added by Reg 1235/2010. Leads safety assessments for human medicines, recommends risk-minimisation measures, and conducts periodic safety update report (PSUR) evaluations. Reports to the CHMP.
PDCO
Paediatric Committee. Added by Reg 1901/2006. Reviews paediatric investigation plans (PIPs) and recommends whether paediatric rewards (6-month SPC extension, PUMA) are warranted. Cross-linked into the EMA governance structure via Art 56 of 726/2004.
CAT
Committee for Advanced Therapies. Added by Reg 1394/2007. Evaluates gene therapies, somatic cell therapies and tissue-engineered products. Drafts opinions that the CHMP then formally adopts. ATMPs are mandatorily within the centralised procedure's scope.
EudraVigilance
The EU central database for adverse reaction reports, maintained by the EMA (Art 57 of 726/2004). MA holders, national competent authorities and healthcare professionals submit suspected adverse reactions. Accessible to regulators across the EEA and, at aggregate level, to the public.
PSUR
Periodic Safety Update Report. Submitted by MA holders under Art 24(3): every 6 months in years 1-2, annually for the next 3 years, then every 3 years. The PRAC leads the evaluation; the CHMP issues any resulting label or procedural changes.
Sunset clause
Art 14(4)-(5): a centralised MA lapses if the product is not placed on the market within 3 years of the authorisation, or if it is absent from all EU markets for 3 consecutive years. Prevents accumulation of unused authorisations in the Community Register.
Compassionate use
Art 83: pre-authorisation access for patients with a chronically or seriously debilitating or life-threatening condition when no satisfactory authorised alternative exists. The CHMP may issue non-binding opinions on conditions for compassionate use programmes across Member States.

Directive 2004/24/EC created the simplified traditional-use registration for herbal medicines with at least 30 years of documented use (15 within the EU) and the EMA herbal committee, the HMPC. It amends the Community Code.

Pharmacovigilance Directive (Dir 2010/84/EU)
Directive 2010/84/EU, with Regulation 1235/2010, delivered the 2010 pharmacovigilance overhaul: the PRAC, EudraVigilance single reporting, risk management plans, the black triangle and the urgent Union procedure. It rewrote Title IX of the Community Code.

Falsified Medicines Directive (Dir 2011/62/EU)
Directive 2011/62/EU closed the legal supply chain to fake medicines with the unique identifier and anti-tampering safety features verified through the EMVS (live since 2019), plus active-substance and broker controls. It amends the Community Code.

Pharmacovigilance amendments (Dir 2012/26/EU)
Directive 2012/26/EU, the post-Mediator follow-up, made the urgent Union safety procedure trigger automatically and forced disclosure of safety-driven withdrawals, including in third countries. It amends the Community Code.
eu_pharmaceutical family
Orphan Medicinal Products (Reg 141/2000)
Regulation (EC) No 141/2000 created the EU orphan designation system: the COMP, the 10-year market exclusivity, fee waivers and the protocol assistance framework. Orphan designation is the gateway into the mandatory centralised procedure under 726/2004. The two instruments operate in tandem for every rare-disease medicine authorised in the EU.
eu_pharmaceutical family
Clinical Trials Regulation (Reg 536/2014)
Regulation (EU) No 536/2014 modernised the EU framework for clinical trials on human medicines, replacing Directive 2001/20/EC. It created the Clinical Trials Information System (CTIS) and the single EU portal for trial authorisation. The clinical trial data generated under 536/2014 form the evidentiary basis for marketing authorisation applications submitted to the EMA under 726/2004's centralised procedure.
eu_pharmaceutical family
Advanced Therapy Medicinal Products (Reg 1394/2007)
Regulation (EC) No 1394/2007 created the EU framework for gene therapies, somatic cell therapies and tissue-engineered products, adding the CAT to the EMA's committee structure. ATMPs are mandatorily within the centralised procedure's scope, connecting directly into the CHMP opinion and Commission decision process established by 726/2004.
eu_pharmaceutical family
Paediatric Medicines (Reg 1901/2006)
Regulation (EC) No 1901/2006 created the paediatric investigation plan (PIP) framework and the PDCO within the EMA. Compliance with an agreed PIP is a condition for any new central marketing authorisation application, linking the paediatric framework directly to the 726/2004 centralised procedure. Successful PIP compliance rewards include a 6-month supplementary protection certificate extension and, for off-patent products, the paediatric-use marketing authorisation (PUMA).
