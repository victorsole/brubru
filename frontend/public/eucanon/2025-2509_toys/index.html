The Toy Safety Regulation: a digital passport for every toy sold in Europe

Regulation (EU) 2025/2509 is the EU's rulebook for any product designed or intended for use in play by children under 14 years of age. Adopted on 26 November 2025, it replaces the 2009 Toy Safety Directive, adds sweeping new prohibitions on hazardous chemicals including PFAS and named bisphenols, tightens fragrance allergen rules, and creates a dedicated digital product passport for toys under Chapter V.

Overview

What the Toy Safety Regulation is, why it exists, and how it is structured

A rulebook for anything designed for children's play

The Toy Safety Regulation is the EU's rulebook for any product designed or intended, whether or not exclusively, for use in play by children under 14 years of age. Adopted on 26 November 2025, it replaces Directive 2009/48/EC, the 2009 Toy Safety Directive that had itself modernised the EU's original 1988 toy safety rules. A Commission evaluation of the 2009 Directive found it relevant and generally effective but identified persistent shortcomings on harmful chemicals and continuing sales of non-compliant, unsafe toys on the EU market, recital 3.

Because toy safety rules need uniform application across the Union without divergent national implementation, the Commission chose a directly applicable Regulation over a directive this time, recital 5.

Chemicals Strategy meets child safety

The Regulation sits at the intersection of two EU policy priorities: consumer and child-safety protection, and the Chemicals Strategy for Sustainability, which in 2020 called for extending generic, preventive prohibitions on the most harmful chemicals to protect consumers and vulnerable groups, recital 4. It also plugs toys into the EU's wider digital product passport architecture, alongside construction products, batteries and the horizontal Ecodesign for Sustainable Products Regulation. Adaptive toys, modified for children with physical or cognitive limitations, are explicitly brought within scope for the first time, recital 2.

Complements, rather than duplicates, other EU consumer-safety law

Regulation (EU) 2023/988 on general product safety continues to apply to toys for online-sales due diligence, the Safety Gate Rapid Alert System, the Safety Business Gateway and the right to information and remedy, recital 6, while Regulation (EU) 2019/1020 continues to govern market surveillance and border controls on products entering the Union. Cybersecurity, AI and connected-toy privacy risks are deliberately left to the Cyber Resilience Act, the AI Act and the Radio Equipment Directive rather than being duplicated here, recital 14.

One of the 13 acts in the EU Digital Product Passport architecture

Chapter V (Articles 19 to 24) creates a dedicated digital product passport for toys, built to be interoperable with the ESPR's digital product passport system and registered in the same Commission-run digital registry, with automated verification at the EU's external borders via the interconnection between that registry and the European Union Customs Single Window Certificates Exchange System, EU CSW-CERTEX. This makes the Toy Safety Regulation one of the 13 acts in Brubru's EU Digital Product Passport legal architecture, alongside its sibling sectoral Regulations, the Construction Products Regulation and the Ecodesign for Sustainable Products Regulation.

Two chapters apply four years early

Chapter VII, notification of conformity assessment bodies, Articles 28 to 44, and Chapter IX, delegated and implementing powers, Articles 49 to 55, apply from 1 January 2026, four and a half years before the rest of the Regulation, so notified bodies, the toy digital product passport's technical rules, and the ECHA exemption process are operational well ahead of the 1 August 2030 general application date.

General provisions and scope (Chapter I, Arts 1-6)

What counts as a toy, what is excluded, and the essential safety requirements behind it all

Scope: what counts as a toy (Art 2)

Article 2 applies the Regulation to any product designed or intended, whether or not exclusively, for use in play by children under 14 years of age. A product need not be exclusively a toy: what matters is whether a parent or supervisor can reasonably assume, from its functions, dimensions and characteristics, that it is intended for play by children of the relevant age group. The Commission may adopt implementing acts clarifying borderline cases, generally with a minimum 18-month lead time before application.

What is excluded from scope (Annex I)

Excluded (Part I): playground equipment for public use, coin-operated or free automatic playing machines for public use, toy vehicles with combustion engines, and toy steam engines.

Not considered toys at all (Part II, a 21-item list): festivity decorations without play value, collectors' items labelled for over-14s, sports equipment such as skates and skateboards for children over 20 kg, bicycles with a saddle height above 435 mm, deep-water aquatic equipment, puzzles with more than 500 pieces, compressed-gas guns and pistols except water guns, fireworks, functional educational products above 24 volts sold for supervised teaching, general-purpose electronics such as PCs and games consoles unless specifically child-targeted, babies' soothers, and reading and educational books for over-36-months children without play value, among others.

Essential safety requirements (Art 5)

Article 5 sets a general safety requirement, toys must not present a risk to health or safety when used as intended or in a foreseeable way, taking account of children's typical behaviour, alongside the particular safety requirements of Annex II, covering physical and mechanical properties, flammability, chemical properties, electrical properties, hygiene and radioactivity.

Warnings and labelling (Art 6, Annex III)

Article 6 requires warnings specifying user limitations, minimum age, required abilities, maximum or minimum user weight, adult-supervision need, wherever necessary for safe use. Annex III sets category-specific mandatory warnings with prescribed wording, including "Not suitable for children under 36 months", "Only to be used under the direct supervision of an adult", and an allergy warning for fragrance-containing olfactory games. Warnings must be preceded by the word "Warning" or the prescribed yellow-triangle pictogram, and must be clearly visible before purchase, including in distance and online sales. Warnings cannot override otherwise-applicable safety requirements or disclaim a foreseeable use.

The new chemical rules (Annex II, Part III)

The Regulation's most significantly strengthened area, tied to the Chemicals Strategy for Sustainability

Generic hazard-class prohibitions

The presence in toys, toy components or micro-structurally distinct parts of substances classified as CMR category 1A, 1B or 2, carcinogenic, mutagenic or toxic for reproduction, endocrine disruptors category 1 or 2, specific target organ toxicants category 1, respiratory sensitisers category 1, or skin sensitisers category 1A, is prohibited outright. This is a generic, preventive approach replacing the substance-by-substance limit values of the 2009 Directive.

PFAS and bisphenols

The intentional use of per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances, PFAS, "forever chemicals", in toys, toy components or micro-structurally distinct parts is prohibited outright, a first for EU toy law. Ten named bisphenols not already caught by the CMR or endocrine-disruptor prohibition are separately banned, drawn from ECHA's wider assessment of 34 bisphenols of concern. Bisphenol A itself falls under the generic CMR prohibition and separately carries a migration limit of 0.005 mg/l.

Technically unavoidable traces from impurities or manufacturing, consistent with good manufacturing practice, remain permitted provided the toy stays safe overall, the ALARA, as low as reasonably achievable, principle. Narrow ECHA-assessed exemptions apply to nickel and cobalt in specified uses, with further exemptions requiring a formal ECHA opinion under Articles 51 to 52.

Allergenic fragrances

A list of named fragrance allergens, essential oils, aldehydes and related chemicals, is prohibited above 10 mg/kg unless technically unavoidable under good manufacturing practice. A further list of common fragrance allergens, limonene, linalool, citronellol, benzyl benzoate and others, must be named on the label, packaging, leaflet or digital product passport if present above the same threshold. Toys intended for children under 36 months, or meant to be placed in the mouth, may not contain the prohibited-list fragrances above 10 mg/kg at all. Olfactory board games and cosmetic kits get a narrow conditional carve-out, subject to clear labelling and the mandatory allergy warning.

Other new safety requirements

New maximum values for impulse and continuous noise from sound-emitting toys, referenced against the lower exposure action values set for workers in Directive 2003/10/EC. New specific requirements address the choking risk from toys combined with food, and the risk of intestinal perforation or blockage from strong magnets or expanding toy materials, recital 17. Batteries in toys are excluded from these chemical requirements because they are separately regulated under the Batteries Regulation (EU) 2023/1542, though toys containing batteries must be designed so the battery is difficult for a child to access.

Obligations of economic operators (Chapters II-III, Arts 7-14)

Differentiated obligations for every actor in the toy supply chain, including online marketplaces

Manufacturers (Art 7)

Manufacturers must ensure design and manufacture meet the essential safety requirements, draw up technical documentation and carry out the applicable conformity assessment procedure before placing a toy on the market, create the digital product passport, affix the data carrier and CE marking, and upload identifiers to the registry, maintain series-production conformity, and take corrective action where a toy presents a risk, notifying market surveillance authorities through the Safety Business Gateway. Technical documentation and the digital product passport must be kept for 10 years.

Authorised representatives, importers, distributors, fulfilment providers (Arts 8-11)

Authorised representatives act only within a written mandate, keeping technical documentation and the digital passport available for 10 years. Importers place on the market only compliant toys, verifying before doing so that conformity assessment, the digital product passport, the data carrier, registry upload and CE marking are in order. Distributors verify instructions, warnings, the data carrier and CE marking before making a toy available. Fulfilment service providers ensure warehousing, packaging and dispatch conditions do not jeopardise compliance, and refuse to support a suspected non-compliant toy.

Manufacturer-equivalent persons and identification (Arts 12-13)

Anyone placing a toy on the market under their own name or trademark, or carrying out a substantial modification, physical or digital, that creates a new hazard or increases an existing risk, assumes full manufacturer obligations. A consumer who substantially modifies their own toy is explicitly excluded from this. All economic operators must, on request, identify who supplied them and who they supplied, retaining that information for 10 years.

Online marketplaces (Chapter III, Art 14)

Offering or promoting non-compliant toys is treated as illegal content under the Digital Services Act, triggering its due-diligence obligations. Marketplaces must comply with Articles 30 to 32 of that Regulation and Article 22 of the General Product Safety Regulation, and must design their interface so economic operators can display the CE marking, any required warning, and the data carrier or weblink to the digital product passport before purchase. Any toy failing the particular safety requirements, or complying with them but still posing a risk, is deemed a dangerous product for General Product Safety Regulation purposes.

The toy digital product passport (Chapter V, Arts 19-24)

The Regulation's most structurally new element, entirely absent from the 2009 Directive

Creation and content (Art 19, Annex VI)

The manufacturer must create a digital product passport before placing a toy on the market, corresponding to a specific toy model, stating that compliance has been demonstrated, and remaining available for 10 years, including through insolvency, liquidation or cessation of the creating operator's activity. Mandatory content includes the toy's unique product identifier, manufacturer and operator details, references to all Union law the toy complies with, whether the passport substitutes for a declaration of conformity under other Regulations such as the AI Act or the Cyber Resilience Act, references to harmonised standards applied, the CE marking, and the list of labelled allergenic fragrances present.

Access, technical design and registry (Arts 20, 22-23)

The passport must be free to access for consumers, no registration or password required, fully interoperable with other Union-law digital product passports, built on open standards, and machine-readable, structured, searchable and transferable without vendor lock-in. Personal data cannot be stored without explicit consumer consent under the GDPR.

Before placing a toy on the market, the responsible economic operator must upload the unique product identifier and unique operator identifier to the digital registry established under the ESPR, which automatically issues a unique registration identifier. When a toy from a third country enters for release for free circulation, the importer must provide that identifier to customs authorities, who may release the toy only after verifying it against the registry, a control that runs automatically once the interconnection between the registry and EU CSW-CERTEX becomes operational.

SME assistance (Art 24)

By 1 August 2029 the Commission must provide guidelines to help micro, small and medium-sized enterprises set up and operate a digital product passport for toys, recital 60 noting that most toy manufacturers subject to the Regulation are SMEs.

Conformity assessment and CE marking (Chapter VI, Arts 25-27, plus Arts 15-18)

The safety assessment, two routes to conformity, and technical documentation requirements

Safety assessment and the two routes (Arts 25-26)

Article 25 requires manufacturers to carry out a safety assessment before placing a toy on the market, covering chemical, physical, mechanical, electrical, flammability, hygiene and radioactivity hazards, including combined-exposure effects and, for AI-enabled or connected toys, children's particular vulnerabilities under the AI Act, Cyber Resilience Act or Radio Equipment Directive.

Internal production control (Module A) is available where the manufacturer has applied harmonised standards or common specifications covering all relevant safety requirements. EU-type examination plus conformity to type (Modules B and C) is required where no harmonised standard exists, where one exists but was not applied, or where the manufacturer considers third-party verification necessary. A notified body examines the technical design and issues a certificate, reviewed at least every five years or whenever manufacturing changes.

CE marking (Arts 17-18)

All toys made available on the market must bear the CE marking, subject to the general principles of Regulation (EC) No 765/2008, affixed visibly, legibly and indelibly on the toy, an affixed label, or, where size or nature does not allow, the packaging or accompanying documentation, before the toy is placed on the market.

Technical documentation (Art 27, Annex V)

Technical documentation must include a detailed design and manufacture description, the list of components, materials, substances and mixtures used, the safety assessment, a description of the conformity assessment procedure followed, and test reports or the EU-type examination certificate as relevant. It must be drawn up in an official EU language, with a translation provided to a market surveillance authority within 30 days of a reasoned request, shorter if there is a serious and immediate risk.

Notification and market surveillance (Chapters VII-VIII, Arts 28-48)

Notified bodies, national enforcement, and the Union safeguard procedure

Notification of conformity assessment bodies (Chapter VII, Arts 28-44)

One of the two chapters that applies early, from 1 January 2026, Chapter VII requires Member States to designate a notifying authority responsible for assessing and notifying conformity assessment bodies and monitoring notified bodies. Conformity assessment bodies must be accredited, independent third parties free of conflicts of interest, with competent and impartial personnel whose remuneration does not depend on assessment outcomes. Notification follows a two-month objection window before a body can operate as a notified body. Notified bodies must review certificates whenever the manufacturing process changes, and in any case every five years.

Market surveillance and safeguards (Chapter VIII, Arts 45-48)

Where a market surveillance authority has sufficient reason to believe a toy presents a risk, it must evaluate the toy, require corrective action, and, if inadequate, take provisional measures, prohibition, restriction, withdrawal, recall, notifying the Commission and other Member States. A provisional national measure is deemed justified if no Member State or the Commission objects within three months. The Union safeguard procedure lets the Commission determine whether a contested national measure is justified. Notably, market surveillance authorities can act against a toy presenting a risk even where it complies with the particular safety requirements, reflecting experience under the 2009 Directive that compliant-on-paper toys have still posed real risks to children, recital 80.

Delegated powers and repeal (Chapters IX-XI, Arts 49-59)

The Commission's rule-making powers, confidentiality, penalties, and the staged repeal of the 2009 Directive

Delegated and implementing powers (Chapter IX, Arts 49-53)

The Commission is empowered, for five years from 1 January 2026, tacitly renewable, to adopt delegated acts on the digital product passport's technical requirements, amending the Annex VI passport content, amending the Annex II Appendix exemption list and limit values, and amending Annex III's warnings to reflect technical and scientific progress. The Committee on Toy Safety assists the Commission under Regulation (EU) No 182/2011, using the advisory procedure for routine matters and the examination procedure for common specifications and toy-classification decisions. ECHA must publish a substance-exemption request format and guidance before 2 February 2027, and deliver opinions within 12 months of a request, extendable by up to 6 months.

Confidentiality and penalties (Chapter X, Arts 54-55)

Chapter X sets confidentiality obligations for information exchanged under the Regulation and requires Member States to lay down effective, proportionate and dissuasive penalties for infringements, notified to the Commission by 1 August 2028.

The staged repeal of the 2009 Directive (Arts 56-57, 59)

Article 56 repeals Directive 2009/48/EC with effect from 1 August 2030, the same date most of the new Regulation starts applying; Annex VIII provides an article-by-article correlation table. Article 57 sets the bridge: Member States must not impede toys already placed on the market in conformity with the 2009 Directive before 1 August 2030 from continuing to be made available, and EC type-examination certificates issued under the old Directive remain valid until 1 February 2031 unless they expire sooner.

Six definitions worth memorising

Toy
Any product designed or intended, whether or not exclusively, for use in play by children under 14 years of age (Art 3(1) via Art 2(1)).
Essential safety requirements
The general safety requirement of Article 5(2) together with the particular safety requirements of Annex II (Art 3(14)).
Substance of concern
Substances classified as CMR category 1A, 1B or 2, endocrine disruptors category 1 or 2, specific target organ toxicants category 1, respiratory sensitisers category 1, or skin sensitisers category 1A (Annex II, Part III, point 4).
Digital product passport
A set of data specific to a toy, accessible via a data carrier, that demonstrates compliance with the Regulation and, potentially, other Union product law, and replaces the EU declaration of conformity (Art 3(17), Art 19).
Notified body
A conformity assessment body designated and notified by a Member State to carry out third-party EU-type examination tasks (Art 3(26), Chapter VII).
Economic operator
The manufacturer, authorised representative, importer, distributor and fulfilment service provider collectively (Art 3(8)).

2025 Regulation vs the 2009 Directive

Regulation (EU) 2025/2509 versus Directive 2009/48/EC, the Directive it repeals

Feature 2025 Regulation (Reg (EU) 2025/2509) 2009 Directive (Dir 2009/48/EC)
Legal instrument A Regulation, directly applicable and uniform across all Member States A Directive, transposed nationally, risk of divergent implementation
Chemical approach Generic, preventive bans on whole hazard classes, PFAS and named bisphenols Mainly substance-by-substance limit values
Digital product passport Chapter V, Arts 19-24, creates a dedicated toy digital product passport No equivalent existed, an EU declaration of conformity was used instead
Customs interconnection Automatic verification of a unique registration identifier via EU CSW-CERTEX before import release, Art 23 No equivalent control layer existed
Online marketplaces Chapter III, Art 14, toy-specific due-diligence obligations aligned with the Digital Services Act Neither the Digital Services Act nor the General Product Safety Regulation existed when adopted
Adaptive toys Expressly within scope, recital 2 Not expressly addressed
Noise limits New maximum impulse and continuous noise values, Annex II Part I point 10 Less developed noise provisions
Substance exemptions Codified ECHA request-and-opinion procedure, Arts 51-52 More ad hoc approach

Same protective goal, a much heavier rulebook

The 2025 Regulation keeps the underlying protective purpose of the 2009 Directive, keeping unsafe toys away from children, but adds the elements a decade of implementation experience and the Chemicals Strategy for Sustainability made unavoidable: generic chemical prohibitions, a digital product passport, customs interconnection, and toy-specific online marketplace duties.

Glossary

Load-bearing terms as used in Regulation (EU) 2025/2509

Toy
Any product designed or intended, whether or not exclusively, for use in play by children under 14 years of age (Art 3(1) via Art 2(1)).
Essential safety requirements
The general safety requirement of Article 5(2) together with the particular safety requirements of Annex II (Art 3(14)).
Substance of concern
Substances classified under Regulation (EC) No 1272/2008 as CMR category 1A/1B/2, endocrine disruptors category 1/2, specific target organ toxicants category 1, respiratory sensitisers category 1, or skin sensitisers category 1A (Annex II, Part III, point 4).
Endocrine disruptor
A substance classified as affecting the endocrine system under Regulation (EC) No 1272/2008; early exposure during critical developmental periods can cause adverse effects even at very low doses.
Allergenic fragrance
A named fragrance chemical listed in the Appendix to Annex II, either prohibited above 10 mg/kg or subject to mandatory labelling above 10 mg/kg.
Digital product passport
A set of data specific to a toy, accessible via a data carrier, that demonstrates compliance with the Regulation and replaces the EU declaration of conformity (Art 3(17), Art 19).
Notified body
A conformity assessment body designated and notified by a Member State to carry out third-party EU-type examination tasks under the Regulation (Art 3(26), Chapter VII).
Conformity assessment
The process demonstrating whether a toy's essential safety requirements have been fulfilled, via internal production control or EU-type examination plus conformity to type (Art 3(25), Art 26).
Economic operator
The manufacturer, authorised representative, importer, distributor and fulfilment service provider collectively (Art 3(8)).
EU CSW-CERTEX
The European Union Customs Single Window Certificates Exchange System, interconnected with the digital product passport registry for automated border verification (Art 3(23), Art 23(4)).
Unique registration identifier
The identifier automatically communicated by the digital product passport registry once a manufacturer uploads a toy's unique product and operator identifiers, used by customs to verify a passport exists (Art 22(2)).
Substantial modification
A physical or digital modification, other than one made by a consumer to their own toy, that creates a new hazard or increases an existing risk, triggering full manufacturer obligations for the person who made it (Art 12).
