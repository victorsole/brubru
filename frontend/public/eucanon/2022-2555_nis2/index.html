EU Canon / EU Cybersecurity Law

The NIS2 Directive

Directive (EU) 2022/2555 is the EU's horizontal cybersecurity law, replacing NIS1 (Directive 2016/1148) with effect from 18 October 2024. It covers 18 sectors, imposes board-level accountability, prescribes ten minimum security measures, requires incident reporting in 24 hours, 72 hours and one month, and sets maximum fines of EUR 10 million or 2% of global annual turnover for essential entities.

Overview

why NIS2 replaced NIS1 and what changed

From NIS1 to NIS2: the core shift

The first NIS Directive (2016/1148) was the EU's inaugural horizontal cybersecurity law. Its fundamental weakness was structural: it asked Member States to identify "operators of essential services" through a national procedure, with minimal constraints on how that identification should be done. The result was predictable fragmentation. Different Member States identified different entities, applied different security requirements, and imposed penalties ranging from trivial to substantial. The single market was operating with eighteen-plus different cybersecurity regimes.

NIS2 corrects this through three structural shifts. First, a size-cap rule replaces the discretionary identification exercise: any medium-sized or larger entity in a covered sector is automatically in scope. Second, the ten minimum measures in Article 21(2) set a harmonised floor rather than leaving security requirements to national discretion. Third, the supervisory and penalty framework is tightened, with maximum fines tied to global turnover on the same model as GDPR.

The directive rests on Article 114 TFEU (internal market approximation), was adopted on 14 December 2022, and published in OJ L 333 of 27 December 2022. It entered into force on 16 January 2023. Member States had until 17 October 2024 to transpose it. NIS1 (Directive 2016/1148) was repealed with effect from 18 October 2024 (Article 44).

Legal architecture and key instruments

NIS2 is a directive, not a regulation: it sets binding objectives that Member States must achieve through national transposition measures. This allows flexibility in how the rules are embedded in national legal systems, but the substantive obligations -- the ten minimum measures, the incident reporting timelines, the fine maxima -- are harmonised minima, meaning Member States can go further but not below the floor.

NIS2 is flanked by several related instruments that must be read alongside it:

  • DORA (Regulation 2022/2554): the sector-specific cybersecurity law for financial entities. Where DORA applies to an entity and its requirements are at least equivalent to NIS2, the relevant NIS2 provisions do not apply to that entity (Article 4 of NIS2). Competent authorities under both instruments must cooperate closely.
  • CER Directive (2022/2557): covers physical resilience of critical entities. Any entity identified as critical under CER is automatically an essential entity under NIS2 (Article 2(3)).
  • GDPR (Regulation 2016/679): where a NIS2 incident entails a personal data breach notifiable under GDPR Article 33, the two supervisory frameworks must coordinate. A GDPR fine for the same conduct blocks a separate NIS2 fine (Article 35).
  • ENISA Regulation (2019/881 -- the Cybersecurity Act): governs ENISA and creates the EU cybersecurity certification framework that underpins Article 24 of NIS2.

Scope and sectors

who is covered: the size-cap rule and the 18-sector framework

The size-cap rule (Article 2(1))

NIS2 applies to public or private entities in Annex I or II sectors that qualify as medium-sized enterprises or exceed those ceilings. Under Commission Recommendation 2003/361/EC, a medium-sized enterprise has 50 or more employees and annual turnover or balance sheet total above EUR 10 million. Entities exceeding the medium-enterprise ceilings (250 or more employees, turnover above EUR 50 million or balance sheet above EUR 43 million) in Annex I are classified as essential entities.

Micro and small entities (below 50 employees, below EUR 10 million turnover) are generally outside scope, unless they fall into one of the special size-exempt categories below.

Entities in scope regardless of size (Article 2(2)-(4))

  • Providers of public electronic communications networks or publicly available electronic communications services
  • Trust service providers (qualified or non-qualified)
  • TLD name registries and DNS service providers
  • Entities providing domain name registration services
  • The sole provider in a Member State of a service essential for critical societal or economic activities
  • Entities whose disruption could cause significant systemic risk or cross-border impact, or a significant impact on public safety, security or health
  • Central government public administration entities (and, following a risk assessment, regional-level entities)
  • Entities identified as critical under the CER Directive (2022/2557)

Essential versus important entities

the two-tier classification and why it matters for supervision

Essential entities (Art 3(1))

Broadly, the larger and more systemically important entities. The category includes:

  • Annex I entities that exceed the medium-enterprise size ceilings (250+ employees or EUR 50m+ turnover)
  • Qualified trust service providers and TLD registries and DNS providers (regardless of size)
  • Providers of public electronic communications networks and publicly available electronic communications services that are at least medium-sized
  • Central government public administration entities
  • Entities identified as critical under the CER Directive (2022/2557)
  • Entities previously identified by Member States as operators of essential services under NIS1, where the Member State so provides

Supervision: ex ante and ex post (Article 32). Proactive monitoring, inspections, audits and scans without waiting for evidence of non-compliance.

Important entities (Art 3(2))

All other in-scope entities: medium-sized Annex I entities that do not qualify as essential, and Annex II entities (unless in a special size-exempt category). The classification covers a much wider population of businesses than NIS1 ever reached.

Important entities are subject to the same substantive obligations as essential entities (Article 21 risk-management measures, Article 23 incident reporting, registration) but face a lighter supervisory touch: competent authorities supervise them reactively (ex post), triggered by evidence, indication or information suggesting non-compliance.

Supervision: ex post only (Article 33). The authority must have a basis before acting; it cannot conduct random proactive audits as it may for essential entities.

Entity list: 17 April 2025 deadline (Art 3(3))

Member States were required to establish, publish and notify to the Commission the list of essential and important entities (and domain name registration service providers) by 17 April 2025. The list must be reviewed and updated at least every two years. Entities were required to submit registration information including name, address, contact details, sector, and IP ranges.

Management accountability

Article 20 -- board-level responsibility and the training duty

Why Article 20 matters

NIS1 contained no management-level accountability clause. Cybersecurity was treated as an IT function. NIS2 changes this fundamentally: the directive now places responsibility for cybersecurity squarely with the governing body, not the technology team.

Article 20(1) requires Member States to ensure that the management bodies of essential and important entities approve the cybersecurity risk-management measures taken under Article 21, oversee their implementation, and can be held liable for infringements of Article 21. This creates a direct line of accountability from the board or equivalent governing body to the cybersecurity posture of the organisation.

Article 20(2) adds a training obligation: members of management bodies must follow cybersecurity training to gain sufficient knowledge and skills to identify risks and assess cybersecurity risk-management practices and their impact on the services provided. Entities are encouraged (though not mandated at EU level) to offer similar training to their employees on a regular basis.

Manager personal liability (Art 32(5)-(6))

For essential entities, Article 32(5) allows competent authorities, as a last resort, to request the temporary prohibition on any natural person responsible for or acting as a legal representative of the entity from exercising managerial functions in that entity. This power is available only after other enforcement measures have been exhausted and the entity has failed to remedy the deficiency within a set deadline.

Article 32(6) separately requires Member States to ensure that natural persons acting as legal representatives, holding managerial responsibility over, or with authority to take decisions on behalf of an essential entity, can be held personally liable for breach of their duty to ensure compliance with the directive. The same applies to important entities by cross-reference in Article 33(5). This personal liability provision is one of the most significant governance shifts introduced by NIS2.

The ten minimum security measures

Article 21 -- the all-hazards approach and the mandatory minimum floor

The all-hazards approach

Article 21(1) requires entities to take appropriate and proportionate technical, operational and organisational measures based on an all-hazards approach. This means the risk-management framework must address all plausible threats -- not only IT attacks, but also physical threats to the physical environment of systems (flooding, fire, power loss), insider threats, and supply-chain compromise. Proportionality is assessed against the entity's exposure to risks, its size, the likelihood and severity of incidents, and their societal and economic impact.

Article 21(5) requires the Commission to adopt implementing acts specifying technical and methodological requirements for the measures in paragraph 2 for certain digital service providers by 17 October 2024. These implementing acts flesh out what the ten measures mean in practice for cloud providers, DNS services, managed service providers and others.

Incident reporting

Article 23 -- early warning, notification and final report

What is a significant incident? (Art 23(3))

An incident is significant if it has caused or is capable of causing: (a) severe operational disruption of services or financial loss for the entity; or (b) considerable material or non-material damage to other natural or legal persons. The mere capacity to cause such disruption is sufficient -- actual harm need not have occurred. Recital 101 clarifies that initial assessment should consider the affected systems' importance, the severity and technical characteristics of the threat, any underlying vulnerabilities, and the entity's experience with similar incidents.

Within 24 hours of becoming aware -- Early warning (Art 23(4)(a))
Submit an early warning to the CSIRT or, where applicable, the competent authority. The early warning must indicate: (i) whether the significant incident is suspected to involve unlawful or malicious acts; and (ii) whether it could have a cross-border impact. It need only contain the information necessary to make the authority aware and allow the entity to seek assistance. Trust service providers: must submit a full incident notification (not just an early warning) within 24 hours.
Within 72 hours of becoming aware -- Incident notification (Art 23(4)(b))
Update the early warning. The notification must include an initial assessment of the significant incident covering its severity and impact, and, where available, the indicators of compromise. This is the main reporting event for most purposes and the one that triggers the CSIRT or competent authority's obligation to provide initial feedback within 24 hours of receiving it.
Upon request -- Intermediate report (Art 23(4)(c))
The CSIRT or competent authority may at any time request an intermediate report covering relevant status updates. There is no specific deadline for this type of report; it is provided within a reasonable timeframe.
Within one month of the incident notification -- Final report (Art 23(4)(d))
The final report must cover: (i) a detailed description of the incident including its severity and impact; (ii) the type of threat or root cause that is likely to have triggered it; (iii) applied and ongoing mitigation measures; and (iv) the cross-border impact where applicable. If the incident is still ongoing at the one-month deadline, a progress report is submitted then and the final report follows within one month of the incident being fully resolved.

No increased liability for notifying (Art 23(1))

The mere act of notification does not subject the notifying entity to increased liability. Voluntary notifications under Article 30 (for non-significant incidents, near misses and cyber threats) similarly do not result in any additional obligations beyond those that would apply had the entity not notified. These provisions are designed to encourage prompt, uninhibited reporting without entities holding back for fear that disclosure will be used against them.

European Vulnerability Database (Art 12(2))

ENISA must develop and maintain a European vulnerability database, open to all stakeholders including entities outside the scope of NIS2. The database lists publicly known vulnerabilities in ICT products and services, including affected products, severity, available patches and guidance on risk mitigation where patches are unavailable. Each Member State must designate one CSIRT as a coordinator for coordinated vulnerability disclosure, acting as a trusted intermediary between researchers and vendors.

Supervision and enforcement

Articles 31-37 -- differentiated regime for essential and important entities

Essential entities -- ex ante and ex post (Art 32)

Competent authorities have a full range of proactive supervisory powers over essential entities, including:

  • On-site inspections and off-site supervision, including random checks conducted by trained professionals
  • Regular and targeted security audits by an independent body or the competent authority
  • Ad hoc audits triggered by a significant incident or evidence of infringement
  • Security scans based on objective, non-discriminatory risk criteria
  • Requests for documentation, data, and evidence of policy implementation

Enforcement powers include: warnings; binding instructions; orders to cease infringing conduct; designation of a monitoring officer; public disclosure of infringements; administrative fines; and, as a last resort, temporary suspension of certifications or a temporary prohibition on an individual manager exercising managerial functions.

Important entities -- ex post only (Art 33)

Supervision of important entities is reactive. The competent authority must have evidence, indication or information suggesting non-compliance before it may act. Once triggered, the available supervisory tools and enforcement powers are the same as for essential entities.

Important entities are not required to systematically document compliance with cybersecurity risk-management measures for submission to the competent authority in the absence of a supervisory action. They must, however, maintain documentation internally and be able to produce it upon request.

The Commission estimated that the distinction between ex ante and ex post supervision represents the key lever for proportionality: the cost of proactive compliance documentation and audit readiness is substantial, and reserving it for the highest-risk entities avoids imposing disproportionate burdens on medium-sized businesses in less critical sectors.

Administrative fines

Article 34 -- maximum fine thresholds and aggravating factors

Entity category Maximum fine Article Notes
Essential entities EUR 10,000,000 or 2% of total worldwide annual turnover (whichever is higher) Art 34(4) Turnover of the undertaking to which the entity belongs; applies to infringements of Arts 21 or 23.
Important entities EUR 7,000,000 or 1.4% of total worldwide annual turnover (whichever is higher) Art 34(5) Turnover of the undertaking to which the entity belongs; applies to infringements of Arts 21 or 23.
Periodic penalty payments National discretion Art 34(6) Member States may provide for periodic penalty payments to compel an entity to cease an infringement.
Public administration entities National discretion Art 34(7) Member States determine whether and to what extent administrative fines apply to public bodies.

Factors in assessing the fine (Art 32(7))

When imposing a fine, competent authorities must take due account of at minimum:

  • The seriousness of the infringement and the importance of the provisions breached (repeated violations, failure to notify or remedy incidents, failure to comply with binding instructions, obstruction of audits, and providing false information are all considered serious in any event)
  • The duration of the infringement
  • Any relevant previous infringements
  • Material or non-material damage caused, including financial or economic loss, effects on other services and the number of users affected
  • Any intent or negligence
  • Measures taken by the entity to prevent or mitigate damage
  • Adherence to approved codes of conduct or certification mechanisms
  • The level of cooperation with the competent authority

Where the same conduct constitutes both a NIS2 infringement and a personal data breach under GDPR, and a GDPR fine has been imposed, the competent authority may not impose a separate NIS2 fine for the same conduct (Article 35(2)).

EU-level cooperation bodies

the Cooperation Group, CSIRTs Network and EU-CyCLONe

Cooperation Group (Art 14)

The strategic cooperation body, composed of representatives of Member States, the Commission and ENISA. It provides guidance on transposition and implementation, exchanges best practices, and oversees the coordinated security risk assessments of critical supply chains under Article 22. It reports to the European Parliament and the Council. It meets at least annually with the Critical Entities Resilience Group under the CER Directive.

CSIRTs Network (Art 15)

The operational cooperation layer: a network of national Computer Security Incident Response Teams plus CERT-EU. It exchanges information on capabilities, incidents, vulnerabilities and near misses, can coordinate cross-border incident response, and supports Member States with cross-border incident assistance. The Commission participates as an observer; ENISA provides the secretariat.

EU-CyCLONe (Art 16)

The European Cyber Crisis Liaison Organisation Network manages large-scale cybersecurity incidents and crises at operational level. It is composed of national cyber crisis management authorities. When an incident could significantly affect the Union, the Commission joins as a full participant rather than observer. It reports every 18 months to the European Parliament and the Council.

ENISA's role

ENISA (the EU Agency for Cybersecurity) plays a central role across NIS2. It provides the secretariat for the CSIRTs Network and EU-CyCLONe, develops and maintains the European vulnerability database (Article 12), maintains the ENISA registry of certain digital service providers (Article 27), publishes a biennial report on the state of cybersecurity in the Union (Article 18), and provides guidelines and technical assistance to Member States on developing CSIRTs and national cybersecurity strategies. ENISA's role is substantially expanded by NIS2 compared with NIS1.

Glossary

key defined terms and concepts in the NIS2 framework

Essential entity
An in-scope entity classified under Art 3(1). Subject to ex ante and ex post supervision (Art 32). Generally, larger Annex I entities, qualified trust service providers, TLD/DNS providers, and central government public administration entities.
Important entity
All other in-scope entities under Art 3(2). Subject to ex post supervision only (Art 33), triggered by evidence of non-compliance. Covers medium-sized Annex I entities and most Annex II entities.
Significant incident
Under Art 23(3): an incident that has caused or is capable of causing severe operational disruption or financial loss, or has affected or could affect other persons by causing considerable material or non-material damage. Triggers the 24h/72h/1-month reporting cascade.
Incident
Under Art 6(6): an event compromising the availability, authenticity, integrity or confidentiality of stored, transmitted or processed data, or of the services offered by or accessible via network and information systems.
Near miss
Under Art 6(5): an event that could have compromised network and information systems or data but was successfully prevented from materialising or did not materialise. Voluntary notification under Art 30 is encouraged.
CSIRT
Computer Security Incident Response Team. Each Member State must designate or establish one or more CSIRTs under Art 10 to handle incident response, provide early warnings and coordinate vulnerability disclosure. National CSIRTs are linked through the CSIRTs Network.
Managed service provider (MSP)
Under Art 6(39): an entity providing services related to the installation, management, operation or maintenance of ICT products, networks, infrastructure or applications via assistance or active administration, either on customers' premises or remotely. MSPs fall under Annex I (ICT service management B2B).
Supply-chain security
One of the ten minimum measures under Art 21(2)(d). Requires entities to assess and manage security risks arising from their direct suppliers and service providers, including the quality of their cybersecurity practices and secure development procedures.
All-hazards approach
The risk-management principle in Art 21(1) requiring entities to address all plausible threats to their network and information systems: IT attacks, physical threats to the environment of systems, insider threats, supply-chain compromise, and natural events. No single threat category may be excluded.
EU-CyCLONe
European Cyber Crisis Liaison Organisation Network (Art 16). Supports coordinated management of large-scale cybersecurity incidents and crises at operational level. Distinct from the CSIRTs Network (operational cooperation) and the Cooperation Group (strategic cooperation).
Main establishment
For certain digital service providers under Art 26(2): the Member State where decisions related to cybersecurity risk-management measures are predominantly taken. Determines which Member State's competent authority has jurisdiction.
Vulnerability
Under Art 6(15): a weakness, susceptibility or flaw of ICT products or ICT services that can be exploited by a cyber threat. ENISA maintains the European vulnerability database listing publicly known vulnerabilities (Art 12).

Frequently asked questions

practical compliance questions

How do I know if my organisation is in scope?

Two questions determine scope. First: does your organisation provide services in one of the 18 sectors in Annexes I or II of NIS2? Second: does it have 50 or more employees, and annual turnover or balance sheet total above EUR 10 million? If both answers are yes, you are in scope. Note that even if you are below these thresholds, you may still be in scope if you fall into one of the special categories in Article 2(2)-(4) -- for example, if you are the sole provider of an essential service in a Member State, or if you are a trust service provider or DNS service provider.

Does NIS2 apply to organisations outside the EU?

Yes, if they provide services within the Union. Certain categories of entity -- DNS service providers, TLD name registries, cloud computing service providers, data centre service providers, CDN providers, managed service providers, managed security service providers, and providers of online marketplaces, search engines and social networks -- fall under the jurisdiction of the EU Member State where they have their main establishment in the Union. If such an entity is not established in the EU but offers services within it, it must designate a representative in the Union (Article 26(3)). Failure to designate a representative does not exempt it from the directive's obligations; any Member State where the entity provides services may take legal action for infringements.

Can the same incident trigger both NIS2 and GDPR reporting obligations?

Yes. A single cybersecurity incident may constitute both a NIS2 significant incident (triggering the 24h/72h/1-month reporting cascade under Article 23) and a personal data breach notifiable under GDPR Article 33 (72-hour deadline to the supervisory authority). Where this occurs, NIS2 Article 35(1) requires the competent authority and the data protection supervisory authority to cooperate. Importantly, Article 35(2) provides that if the data protection authority has already imposed a fine under GDPR Article 58(2)(i) for the same conduct, the NIS2 competent authority must not impose a separate NIS2 administrative fine for that same conduct. The other NIS2 enforcement measures (warnings, binding instructions, etc.) may still be applied.

What does "worldwide annual turnover" mean for fine calculations?

Article 34(4) and (5) refer to the total worldwide annual turnover in the preceding financial year of the "undertaking to which the essential entity belongs." The concept of undertaking here follows the competition law definition in Articles 101 and 102 TFEU, meaning it encompasses the entire group of companies under common control, not just the legal entity that committed the infringement. For a subsidiary of a large multinational group, this can result in a significantly higher fine ceiling than if only the subsidiary's own turnover were used. Recital 130 of NIS2 confirms this interpretation.

Our sector has its own cybersecurity rules (e.g. DORA for finance). Do we also comply with NIS2?

It depends on whether the sector-specific rules are equivalent in effect. Article 4(1) of NIS2 provides that where a sector-specific Union legal act requires entities to adopt cybersecurity risk-management measures or to notify significant incidents, and where those requirements are at least equivalent in effect to the NIS2 obligations, the relevant NIS2 provisions do not apply. DORA (Regulation 2022/2554) is the main example: financial entities subject to DORA are exempt from the NIS2 risk-management and incident-reporting provisions to the extent covered by DORA. However, the Commission issued guidelines in July 2023 to assist with this analysis, and not all financial entities are covered by DORA to the same degree. If in doubt, seek legal advice on whether your sector-specific obligations genuinely meet the NIS2 equivalence test.
