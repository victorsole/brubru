The unsold goods destruction ban: the ten exceptions that make it workable

From 19 July 2026, the ESPR prohibits destroying unsold consumer products currently apparel, clothing accessories and footwear, once they fall unsold. Commission Delegated Regulation (EU) 2026/296 does not create that ban. It supplies the exhaustive list of circumstances in which destruction is still permitted, safety risks, legal non-compliance, intellectual property, unrepairable damage or defects, and genuinely unwanted donations, together with the documentation a company must keep to rely on each one.

Overview

What the derogations Regulation is, why it exists, and how it is structured

Not the ban itself, the exceptions to it

Commission Delegated Regulation (EU) 2026/296 supplements Regulation (EU) 2024/1781, the ESPR, by setting out derogations from the prohibition of destruction of unsold consumer products. The ban is in the ESPR itself, Article 25(1) prohibits economic operators from destroying certain unsold consumer products from 19 July 2026, and today that means the products listed in ESPR Annex VII: apparel and clothing accessories, and footwear. This Regulation does not touch that ban. It supplies the exhaustive list of circumstances in which destruction is nonetheless permitted, plus the documentation needed to prove them.

A Commission delegated act, under Article 25(5) ESPR

This is a Commission Delegated Regulation, adopted by the Commission alone at Brussels on 9 February 2026 and signed by Commission President Ursula von der Leyen, published in OJ L, 2026/296 on 22 April 2026. Its legal basis is Article 25(5) of Regulation (EU) 2024/1781, the ESPR, which empowers the Commission to adopt delegated acts setting out derogations from the Article 25(1) destruction ban. It is binding in its entirety and directly applicable in all Member States, as befits a Regulation rather than a Directive.

Two dates that do different jobs

Do not conflate them. Entry into force: this Regulation entered into force on the twentieth day following its 22 April 2026 publication, that is 12 May 2026 (Article 6). Application: the Regulation applies from 19 July 2026 (Article 6), the same date the ESPR Article 25(1) prohibition itself starts. In practice, the derogations and the ban they qualify become operative on the same day, so a company facing the ban already has the full list of exceptions available from day one.

Part of the wider EU Digital Product Passport architecture

Brubru tracks 13 acts in the EU Digital Product Passport legal architecture, the ESPR itself, the DPP registry Implementing Regulation, product-specific laws such as the Batteries, Construction Products, Toy Safety and Detergents Regulations, the harmonised standards Decision, and this pair of unsold-goods acts, the destruction ban and its derogations here, and the disclosure format alongside it. Together they turn the ESPR's ambition of a genuinely circular economy into enforceable, checkable obligations.

The ban, and what this act does (Articles 1 to 6)

Six short articles, each doing one job, plus the ESPR ban they qualify

The ban lives in the ESPR, not here

Article 25(1) of Regulation (EU) 2024/1781 prohibits an economic operator from destroying certain unsold consumer products, from 19 July 2026. The products in scope are those listed in ESPR Annex VII, which today means apparel and clothing accessories, and footwear. "Destruction" is defined in Article 2(34) of the ESPR. Even where the ban applies, operators may still remanufacture, refurbish, donate, or prepare products for reuse, none of that counts as destruction.

Article 1: definitions

Defines two terms used throughout the Regulation. A "social economy entity" is a social economy entity as defined in Article 3(4i) of the Waste Framework Directive, Directive 2008/98/EC. Something is "cost-effective" when the cost of repairing or refurbishing a product does not outweigh the total cost of destroying that product together with the materials, manufacturing, packaging, transport, stocking and other administrative or logistical costs of replacing it.

Article 2: derogations from the prohibition of destruction

Sets out the exhaustive list of ten circumstances, points (a) to (j), under which an unsold consumer product listed in ESPR Annex VII may be destroyed, provided the documentation required under Article 3 can be presented. See the full list below.

Article 3: documentation for verification of compliance

Requires economic operators to keep, for five years after destruction, the specific type of evidence that corresponds to whichever derogation ground they relied on, and to produce it to competent authorities in electronic form within 30 days of a request.

Article 4: statement to waste treatment operators

Economic operators aware that a derogation applies to a product must provide a statement on the applicable derogation to the waste treatment operator they deliver it to, so that operator can sort, reuse and recycle it more effectively.

Article 5: review

The Commission must review this Regulation, taking into account new products added to ESPR Annex VII and the appropriateness of the derogations, including whether new science or technology justify favouring high-quality recycling. It must present the results, with a draft revision proposal if appropriate, every time a new product is added to Annex VII and, in any case, no later than 12 May 2031.

Article 6: entry into force and application

The Regulation entered into force on the twentieth day following its publication in the Official Journal, 12 May 2026, and applies from 19 July 2026. It is binding in its entirety and directly applicable in all Member States.

The ten derogations (Article 2)

An exhaustive list, points (a) to (j). Destruction is otherwise banned for the ESPR Annex VII products from 19 July 2026

GroundWhat it covers
(a) Dangerous product The product is a dangerous product within the meaning of the General Product Safety Regulation, Regulation (EU) 2023/988.
(b) Other legal non-compliance The product is unfit for purpose because it is non-compliant with Union or national law for reasons other than point (a), and destruction is required by that law or is the appropriate and proportionate corrective action, for example a product made through forced labour.
(c) Intellectual property infringement found A final judicial decision, an alternative dispute resolution decision, a notification from a right holder or competent authority, or a substantiated internal investigation, finds the product infringes intellectual property rights.
(d) Expired licence or contractual IP restriction A valid, enforceable licence or similar contract restricted sale, distribution or transfer of the product after a specified date, that date has passed, and the operator can substantiate the infringement and show destruction is appropriate and proportionate.
(e) Unremovable branding or design The product cannot be prepared for reuse or remanufactured because it is technically unfeasible to remove or permanently hide labels, logos or recognisable design characteristics that are intellectual-property protected, or considered inappropriate.
(f) Damaged, deteriorated or contaminated The product is reasonably considered unacceptable for consumer use because of physical damage, deterioration or contamination, including hygiene issues, and repair or refurbishment is not technically feasible or cost-effective.
(g) Design or manufacturing defect The product is unfit for its intended purpose due to a design or manufacturing defect, and repair is not technically feasible.
(h) Donation offered and refused Only where none of (a) to (g) apply: the product was offered for donation, either directly to at least three suitable social economy entities in the Union or on an easily accessible page of the operator's website for at least eight weeks, and was not accepted.
(i) Donation received but unclaimed A social economy entity in the Union received the product as a donation, but no recipient could be found for it.
(j) Reused product unclaimed The product was made available on the market after being prepared for reuse by a waste treatment operator, but no recipient could be found for it.

Even permitted destruction follows the waste hierarchy

Where a derogation applies, destruction must still be carried out in accordance with the priority order of the waste hierarchy set out in Article 4 of the Waste Framework Directive, Directive 2008/98/EC, prioritising recycling over other recovery, including energy recovery, and disposal. Destruction is a last resort even inside the ten exceptions, not a default once an exception applies.

Proof you must keep (Articles 3 and 4)

Five years of retention, 30 days to produce it, and a different type of proof for each ground

Five-year retention, 30-day production (Article 3)

Economic operators must keep, for a period of five years after a product covered by a derogation has been destroyed, and put at the disposal of competent authorities in electronic form within 30 days of a request, the documentation proving the derogation applied, except where the authority already holds the information under another legal act. Documentation may be made collectively where multiple products share the same justifying circumstances.

A different proof for each ground
  • (a) Dangerous product: a safety-concern description with an assessment under Articles 6 to 8 of the General Product Safety Regulation, or a test report identifying non-compliant chemicals and the applicable law.
  • (b) Other non-compliance: a self-assessment statement naming the type of non-compliance and the applicable Union or national law.
  • (c) IP infringement found: the judicial decision, ADR decision or notification, or documentation of the internal investigation substantiating the infringement.
  • (d) Expired licence: the licence, contract or agreement specifying the restriction, plus a justification that destruction is appropriate and proportionate.
  • (e) Unremovable branding: an inspection report or supporting documentation, including visual evidence, technical analysis or expert opinion, showing the technical unfeasibility.
  • (f) and (g) Damage or defect: evidence of quality-assessment procedures prioritising restocking and repair, or an inspection record documenting the type and severity of the damage or defect and why correction is not feasible.
  • (h) Donation offered and refused: evidence of the donation offer.
  • (i) Donation unclaimed: a declaration attesting the product was received as a donation and that no recipient could be found.
  • (j) Reused product unclaimed: documentation from the waste treatment operator showing the product was received and that no recipient could be found.

Statement to the waste treatment operator (Article 4)

Where an operator is aware a derogation applies to a product it delivers to a waste treatment operator, it must provide that waste treatment operator with a statement on the applicable derogation, so sorting, reuse and recycling decisions downstream are made with the right information rather than guesswork.

Where it sits

One half of the ESPR's ban-and-disclose pair, inside the wider DPP and circular-economy architecture

The parent act: the ESPR, Articles 25 and 24

Article 25 of the ESPR, Regulation (EU) 2024/1781, is where the destruction ban itself lives, Article 25(1) sets the prohibition, and Article 25(5) empowers the Commission to adopt exactly this kind of delegated act setting out legitimate derogations. Article 24 of the ESPR is the separate disclosure obligation, requiring large enterprises, and from 2030 medium-sized ones, to publicly report how much they discard and why.

The sibling act: the disclosure format

A separate Commission Implementing Regulation, (EU) 2026/2, sets the common format large and, from 2030, medium-sized enterprises must use to disclose annually how many unsold consumer products they discard and why. It applies later, from 2 March 2027. Read together, the two acts form a single policy pair: this one bans destruction with ten documented exceptions, the other makes what actually gets discarded, and why, publicly visible and checkable.

Inside the wider Digital Product Passport hub

This derogations Regulation is one of 13 acts Brubru tracks in the EU Digital Product Passport legal architecture, alongside the ESPR itself, the DPP registry Implementing Regulation, the harmonised standards Decision, and sectoral laws such as the Batteries, Construction Products, Toy Safety and Detergents Regulations. It is the enforcement guardrail for one specific circular-economy failure mode, destroying unsold stock instead of reusing, remanufacturing, donating or recycling it, inside that broader architecture.

Glossary

Load-bearing terms as used in Commission Delegated Regulation (EU) 2026/296

Destruction
Defined in Article 2(34) of the ESPR. Remanufacturing, refurbishing, donating or preparing a product for reuse is not destruction, and remains available even where a derogation applies.
Derogation
One of the ten circumstances in Article 2, points (a) to (j), under which destroying an otherwise-protected unsold product is legally permitted, provided the matching documentation can be shown.
Social economy entity
As defined in Article 3(4i) of the Waste Framework Directive, Directive 2008/98/EC, an entity that by statute or habitual practice accepts donations of consumer products for use or reuse.
Cost-effective
Defined in Article 1(2) of this Regulation: repair or refurbishment is cost-effective when its cost does not outweigh the total cost of destroying and replacing the product.
Waste hierarchy
The priority order set out in Article 4 of Directive 2008/98/EC. Even where a derogation applies, destruction must follow it, prioritising recycling over other recovery and disposal.
ESPR Annex VII
The list of product categories the Article 25(1) destruction ban currently covers: apparel and clothing accessories, and footwear. This Regulation refers to it rather than repeating it.
