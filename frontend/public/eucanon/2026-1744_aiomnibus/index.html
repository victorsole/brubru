The Digital Omnibus on AI

Regulation (EU) 2026/1744 amends the AI Act, it does not replace it. It moves the high-risk deadlines to December 2027 and August 2028, adds two new prohibitions, gives the AI Office direct enforcement powers over certain AI systems, and extends simplification measures from SMEs to small mid-caps.

Overview

What it is, and what it is not

What it is

Regulation (EU) 2026/1744 of the European Parliament and of the Council of 8 July 2026 is formally titled "amending Regulations (EU) 2024/1689, (EU) 2018/1139 and (EU) 2023/1230 as regards the simplification of the implementation of harmonised rules on artificial intelligence (Digital Omnibus on AI)". It is binding law, published in the Official Journal on 24 July 2026 and in force since Monday 27 July 2026 (the third day after publication, Article 4). Its legal basis is Article 114 TFEU.

It is a simplification and timing package. The recitals explain why: harmonised standards were delayed, the AI Act's high-risk obligations would otherwise have applied from 2 August 2026 without the tools to comply, SMEs and small mid-caps carry a disproportionate cost, and supervision of AI built on general-purpose models needed a single enforcer. It also answers a public concern by adding two new prohibitions.

What it is not

It does not replace the AI Act. Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 stays the base text and its general date of application, 2 August 2026, is unchanged. The Omnibus works by amending it: 43 numbered changes in Article 1, plus targeted changes to the civil aviation Regulation (EU) 2018/1139 in Article 2 and the Machinery Regulation (EU) 2023/1230 in Article 3. To read the AI Act in its current form, read it together with this Regulation, or use the consolidated text. It is also separate from the data and cyber part of the Digital Omnibus package, which is a different proposal.

Structure: four articles
  • Article 1: amendments to the AI Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689), points (1) to (43).
  • Article 2: amendments to Regulation (EU) 2018/1139 (civil aviation safety).
  • Article 3: amendments to Regulation (EU) 2023/1230 (machinery).
  • Article 4: entry into force on the third day after publication.

The text has 47 recitals. It is an EU Regulation: it applies directly in every Member State and needs no national transposition.

Procedural history

The Commission proposed the text as COM(2025) 0836 on 19 November 2025, as part of the Digital Omnibus package. It is procedure 2025/0359(COD) (Omnibus VII), and OEIL shows it as completed. A joint IMCO and LIBE committee handled it, with rapporteurs Arba Kokalari (EPP, IMCO) and Michael McNamara (Renew, LIBE). Parliament adopted first-reading texts T10-0098/2026 on 26 March 2026 and T10-0198/2026 on 16 June 2026; the committee had approved the agreed text on 2 June 2026. The Council adopted the act on 29 June 2026 and it was signed on 8 July 2026. Opinions came from the ECB, the EESC (18 March 2026), the Committee of the Regions (7 May 2026) and, jointly, the EDPS and the EDPB (20 January 2026).

What it covers

Five areas of change to the AI Act, in one view

New application dates
Annex III high-risk from 2 December 2027, Annex I products from 2 August 2028, and a 2 December 2026 transition for marking synthetic content.
New prohibitions
Two additions to Article 5: non-consensual intimate imagery and child sexual abuse material, from 2 December 2026.
AI Office enforcement
New Articles 75a to 75d give the AI Office market-surveillance powers, binding commitments, fines and periodic penalty payments.
SME and small mid-cap relief
Simplified documentation, proportionate quality management and a lower-of fine cap, now extended to small mid-caps.
Sandboxes and testing
National sandboxes by 2 August 2027, a possible Union-level sandbox run by the AI Office, and wider real-world testing.

Grace period for systems already on the market (Article 111(2))

If at least one unit of a high-risk system of a given type and model was lawfully placed on the market before the relevant date, other units of that type and model may continue without new obligations while the design stays unchanged. The date of the first unit is decisive. A significant design change triggers full compliance. Providers and deployers of high-risk systems intended for use by public authorities have until 2 August 2030.

Two new prohibitions

Article 5(1)(ba) and (bb), with scope rules in Article 5(1a) and (1b), applicable from 2 December 2026

(ba) Non-consensual intimate imagery

AI systems that generate or manipulate realistic intimate images, video or audio of an identifiable person without that person's explicit consent are prohibited. Only realistic depictions of identifiable people are caught, not cartoonish or impossible ones. Manipulation that does not increase the exposure of the depicted intimate parts or alter the nature of the depicted activity is not caught.

(bb) Child sexual abuse material

AI systems that generate child sexual abuse material are prohibited, except where a "without right" defence applies under national law.

When a system is caught (Article 5(1a))

A system falls under either prohibition where that output is its intended purpose, or where it is a reasonably foreseeable and reproducible outcome and the system lacks adequate technical safeguards to prevent it. Deployers are caught only when they use a system for that purpose, and accidental generation is not covered.

Recital 44 gives examples of safeguards a provider may use: data cleaning, refusal training, safe prompt design, output controls, runtime guardrails, content classification and filtering, usage restrictions, abuse detection, and notice-and-action.

Enforcement by the AI Office (Articles 75 to 75d)

The biggest institutional change: one enforcer for AI built on general-purpose models

Who is supervised: exclusive competence (Article 75)

The AI Office becomes exclusively competent to supervise AI systems built on a general-purpose AI model where the system and the model come from the same provider, with exceptions where there is specific sectoral supervision. The recitals extend this to providers within the same undertaking, and to their deployers within that undertaking. It is also exclusively competent for AI systems that are, or are integrated into, a very large online platform or search engine under the Digital Services Act. Other deployers stay under national supervision, and so do AI systems used by Union institutions, which the European Data Protection Supervisor oversees.

For AI embedded in a very large platform or search engine, the first point of entry is the Digital Services Act's risk assessment, mitigation and audit duties (DSA Articles 34, 35 and 37). This is without prejudice to later AI Act enforcement, and the recitals expressly require ne bis in idem and proportionality where fines overlap.

Providers of high-risk systems under the AI Office report serious incidents to the AI Office, on the Article 73 timing (15 days, two days for a widespread infringement or critical-infrastructure disruption, and ten days for a death). A national authority can ask the AI Office to assess a case, and the AI Office must answer within four months.

Powers (Article 75a)
  • All market-surveillance powers of Regulation (EU) 2019/1020, Article 14(4) and Article 16(3), including prohibiting or restricting a system, ordering withdrawal or recall, and requiring access to an online interface to be restricted.
  • Investigations, starting on reasonable suspicion or after a complaint.
  • Information requests, by simple request or by decision.
  • Remote or on-site inspections, with national authorities required to assist.
  • Orders for access to AI systems and for data retention, and the right to appoint independent experts and auditors.
  • Recovery from the operator of the costs of its supervision and enforcement.
Commitments (Article 75b)

If an operator offers commitments during an investigation, the AI Office may make them legally binding by decision and close the case. It may reopen the proceedings if the facts change materially, if the operator breaks the commitments, or if the decision rested on incomplete or misleading information. It must reject commitments that cannot ensure effective compliance, in a reasoned decision.

Fines and penalty payments (Article 75c)
  • The AI Office adopts a non-compliance decision after sharing its preliminary findings with the operator.
  • Fines follow Article 99(3) to (7) of the AI Act, applied to the AI Office.
  • Periodic penalty payments compel operators to submit to investigations and inspections, answer information requests, apply corrective actions and comply with decisions. They may not exceed 5% of average daily income or worldwide annual turnover in the preceding financial year, per day.
  • The Court of Justice has unlimited jurisdiction: it may cancel, reduce or increase a fine or penalty.
  • A five-year limitation period applies to the AI Office's powers and to enforcing its decisions.
  • Funds collected go to the general budget of the Union.
Safeguards and cooperation (Articles 75(1d), 75d and 77)

Before a decision restricting, withdrawing or recalling a system on a national market, the AI Office must notify and consult the national authority. Article 75d adds safeguards for the operators concerned and provides for publication of decisions. Article 77 strengthens cooperation with the authorities that protect fundamental rights.

Does the Omnibus add an AI kill switch?

No. The Omnibus adds no duty to build in a shutdown capability and no emergency shutdown order. The terms "shut down" and "kill switch" do not appear in the text. The nearest tools are three, and only the first is new:

  • the AI Office's powers to restrict, withdraw or recall a system, available after an investigation under Articles 75a to 75c;
  • the Commission's existing request power over general-purpose models, Article 93(1)(c) of the AI Act;
  • the human-oversight "stop" button that high-risk systems must allow, Article 14(4)(e) of the AI Act.

Simplification for SMEs and small mid-caps

Relief that the AI Act gave to SMEs now reaches small mid-cap enterprises too

A new category: small mid-caps

The Omnibus adds definitions of SME and small mid-cap enterprise (SMC), the latter by reference to Recommendation (EU) 2025/1099. The simplifications for SMEs, including start-ups, are extended to SMCs.

Documentation and quality management
  • The Commission must create a simplified technical-documentation form for SMEs and SMCs, which notified bodies must accept (Article 11).
  • Quality-management duties are applied proportionately, with a simplified system for all SMEs without partner enterprises.
  • Two items are deleted from Annex VIII Section B, which lightens EU-database registration.
Fines: whichever is lower

Article 99(6a) applies the cap on fines for SMCs on the same "whichever is lower" basis as for SMEs. Article 99 also gains a new fine tier for the value-chain duties in Article 25(2) and (4), and Member States' penalty rules may include non-monetary measures.

Value-chain cooperation (Article 25)

The original provider of a system must cooperate with the new provider: documentation, known limitations and failure modes, and targeted technical access. The written-agreement duty in Article 25(4) does not apply to third parties offering tools, services, processes or components under a free and open-source licence, but this carve-out does not cover general-purpose AI models. SMEs and SMCs also get priority access to the sandboxes that the AI Office may establish.

AI literacy (Article 4)

Providers and deployers must take measures to support the AI literacy of their staff. The text expressly does not require them to guarantee any specific level of literacy. The Commission and Member States support them, and the AI Board adopts recommendations.

Bias detection (new Article 4a)

Providers of high-risk systems may exceptionally process special categories of personal data where strictly necessary to detect and correct bias, under six cumulative safeguards: no alternative data, technical limits on re-use, security controls, no transfer to others, deletion once the bias is corrected, and a record of why it was necessary. Other providers and deployers may do so under the same safeguards, without any obligation.

Standards, notified bodies and the Cyber Resilience Act link

Points 14 to 21 of Article 1: joining up conformity assessment and cybersecurity

One application for notified bodies

A body designated under both the AI Act and Annex I Section A sectoral law can make a single application and undergo a unified assessment. Designation uses the new Annex XIV, which sets codes by type of AI system. One of them is AIH 0401, "AI systems based on other emerging AI technologies, including Agentic AI".

Deadlines for notified bodies and guidance

Notified bodies already designated under Annex I Section A sectoral law must apply for designation under the AI Act by 28 January 2028 (Article 43(3)). The Commission's guidelines on combining AI Act and sectoral requirements are due by 1 August 2027 (Article 96(1)(g)).

Standards requests

The Commission must request European standards "without undue delay" to support compliance with the AI Act jointly with sectoral law. Delay in these standards is one of the reasons the recitals give for moving the high-risk dates.

The Cyber Resilience Act link (new Article 42(3))

A high-risk AI system that falls within the scope of the Cyber Resilience Act is deemed to comply with the cybersecurity requirements of Article 15 of the AI Act, where the essential requirement in Article 12(1) of the Cyber Resilience Act is met.

Codes of practice

The Commission assesses whether the codes of practice on the marking and labelling of AI-generated content, and on the obligations of general-purpose AI providers, are adequate.

Sandboxes and real-world testing

Points 22 to 26 and 33 of Article 1: more places to test, and a clearer timetable

National sandboxes by 2 August 2027

Each Member State must have its national AI regulatory sandbox operational by 2 August 2027. The European Data Protection Supervisor may run one for Union bodies.

A Union-level sandbox

The AI Office may run a Union-level sandbox for the AI systems under its own supervision. Its scope is defined to avoid overlap with national sandboxes, and SMEs and SMCs get priority access.

Real-world testing extended

Testing in real-world conditions is extended to systems under Annex I Section A. A new Article 60a lets Member States allow it for products under Annex I Section B.

Governance and monitoring

The AI Office must receive adequate resources (Article 64(3)). The Commission's guidance and template for the post-market monitoring plan is due by 2 September 2027.

Machinery and aviation amendments

Articles 2 and 3, and the change to Annex I: sector law takes the lead

Civil aviation: Regulation (EU) 2018/1139 (Article 2)

When the Commission adopts delegated or implementing acts on AI safety components under the aviation safety Regulation, it must take into account Chapter III, Section 2 of the AI Act, the requirements for high-risk systems.

Machinery: Regulation (EU) 2023/1230 (Article 3)

The Commission shall adopt delegated acts adding health and safety requirements for high-risk AI in machinery, to apply by 2 August 2028. Until machinery standards exist, high-risk AI that complies with AI Act standards is presumed compliant.

Machinery moves within Annex I

Point 1 of Annex I Section A is deleted and the Machinery Regulation is added to Section B. For Section B products, only Article 6(1), Article 60a and Articles 102 to 112 of the AI Act apply: AI in machinery is regulated through the sector's own law. The definition of "safety component" is also narrowed: AI used solely for non-safety user assistance, optimisation, efficiency, automation, convenience or quality control is not a safety component, and a product that needs third-party assessment only for non-safety risks, such as radio spectrum, does not trigger the high-risk route.

Key definitions

Terms the Omnibus adds or changes in Article 3 of the AI Act

Small mid-cap enterprise (SMC)

A new definition in the AI Act, set by reference to Recommendation (EU) 2025/1099. Being an SMC brings the proportionality measures that were previously reserved for SMEs, including simplified technical documentation, proportionate quality management and the "whichever is lower" cap on fines.

Safety component (narrowed). A component fulfils a safety function, or its failure endangers health and safety. Other uses, such as convenience or optimisation, are outside the definition.

Glossary

Terms used on this page

AI Office
The Commission body that supervises general-purpose AI models and, since the Omnibus, AI systems built on them by the same provider and certain AI systems on very large online platforms and search engines.
Digital Omnibus
A package of simplification proposals presented on 19 November 2025. The AI part is Regulation (EU) 2026/1744; the data and cyber part is a separate proposal.
SMC (small mid-cap)
An enterprise defined by reference to Recommendation (EU) 2025/1099. The AI Act's SME simplifications now extend to SMCs.
Annex III
The AI Act annex listing the use-case areas whose AI systems are high-risk. Its rules now apply from 2 December 2027.
Annex I
The AI Act annex listing Union product-safety legislation. AI embedded in these products becomes subject to the high-risk rules from 2 August 2028.
Periodic penalty payment
A daily payment, capped at 5% of average daily income or worldwide annual turnover, that the AI Office may impose to compel compliance (Article 75c).
Regulatory sandbox
A supervised environment in which providers can develop and test AI systems before placing them on the market.
Notified body
A conformity-assessment body designated by a Member State. The Omnibus lets bodies under both the AI Act and sectoral law apply once.
VLOP and VLOSE
Very large online platforms and very large online search engines, as designated under the Digital Services Act.
