The Cyber Solidarity Act

Regulation (EU) 2025/38 is a funding and solidarity instrument. It builds a voluntary network of Cyber Hubs to detect threats, a mechanism to prepare for and respond to serious incidents, an EU Cybersecurity Reserve of trusted private responders, and an ENISA review of major incidents. It is financed by the Digital Europe Programme and puts no new obligations on companies.

Overview

What it is, what it pays for, and what it leaves to other laws

What it is

Regulation (EU) 2025/38 of the European Parliament and of the Council of 19 December 2024 is formally titled "laying down measures to strengthen solidarity and capacities in the Union to detect, prepare for and respond to cyber threats and incidents and amending Regulation (EU) 2021/694 (Cyber Solidarity Act)". It was published in the Official Journal on 15 January 2025 and entered into force on 4 February 2025, the twentieth day after publication.

Its legal basis is Article 173(3) and Article 322(1), point (a), of the Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union, which cover industry and budget rules. It has 26 articles in five chapters, plus an Annex that rewrites the cybersecurity parts of Annexes I and II of the Digital Europe Programme Regulation (EU) 2021/694.

A funding and solidarity instrument

The Act does not regulate companies. It spends money from the Digital Europe Programme, mainly under Specific Objective 3 (Cybersecurity and Trust), to build shared detection capacity and to pool emergency help for Member States, EU institutions and, under conditions, associated third countries. The money flows mostly through the European Cybersecurity Industrial, Technology and Research Competence Centre (ECCC) in Bucharest. The Reserve is run by the Commission and ENISA.

No new obligations on companies

The Cyber Solidarity Act places no new obligations on companies. Joining the Alert System is voluntary for Member States, and entities take part in coordinated preparedness testing voluntarily. The criteria in Article 17 are procurement conditions for providers that choose to bid for the Reserve. The binding entity duties sit in NIS 2, Directive (EU) 2022/2555, and the binding product duties in the Cyber Resilience Act, Regulation (EU) 2024/2847.

Procedural history

The procedure is 2023/0109(COD). The Commission proposed the Act as COM(2023)0209 on 18 April 2023. Parliament adopted its first-reading position on 24 April 2024 and the Council adopted the act on 2 December 2024. It was signed on 19 December 2024. A consolidated text of 15 January 2025 is available on EUR-Lex under CELEX 02025R0038-20250115. A companion act adopted the same day, Regulation (EU) 2025/37, adds managed security services to EU cybersecurity certification.

What it covers

Five areas of the Regulation, in one view

European Cybersecurity Alert System
A voluntary pan-European network of National and Cross-Border Cyber Hubs that pool and analyse threat data and issue alerts (Articles 3 to 8).
Cyber Hubs and their funding
Up to 50% of acquisition costs for a National Cyber Hub and up to 75% for a Cross-Border Cyber Hub, through the ECCC (Article 9).
Cybersecurity Emergency Mechanism
Coordinated preparedness testing, other preparedness actions and grants for mutual assistance between Member States (Articles 10 to 13 and 18).
EU Cybersecurity Reserve
Incident response services from trusted managed security service providers, on request, with a 48-hour answer to requests (Articles 14 to 17 and 19).
European Cybersecurity Incident Review Mechanism
ENISA reviews significant and large-scale incidents on request and reports causes, vulnerabilities and lessons learned (Article 21).

Scope and limits

Articles 1 and 2: voluntary, complementary, and silent on national security

What it does
  • Sets up three pillars to detect, prepare for and respond to cyber threats and incidents (Article 1).
  • Lets Member States join the Alert System on a voluntary basis (Article 3) and take part in preparedness testing voluntarily (Article 12).
  • Complements the work of the CSIRTs network, EU-CyCLONe and the NIS Cooperation Group (Article 1(4)).
  • Amends the Digital Europe Programme Regulation so that the money can flow (Article 22).
What it does not do
  • It places no new obligations on companies.
  • National security stays the sole responsibility of each Member State (Article 1(5)).
  • Confidential information is shared only as far as it is relevant and proportionate, and nothing has to be supplied that would harm national security, public security or defence (Article 1(6)).
  • Support for Member States is given on request and complements their own efforts (Article 10(2)).

The incident tiers the Act uses

Tier What it means Where it is defined
Significant cybersecurity incident An incident fulfilling the criteria set out in NIS 2 Article 23(3). Article 2(10)
Large-scale cybersecurity incident A large-scale cybersecurity incident as defined in NIS 2 Article 6(7). Article 2(12)
Large-scale-equivalent cybersecurity incident For EU institutions, bodies, offices and agencies, a major incident. For DEP-associated third countries, an incident that disrupts more than the country can handle. Article 2(13)

The Alert System

Chapter II, Articles 3 to 9: National Cyber Hubs, Cross-Border Cyber Hubs and the money behind them

National Cyber Hubs (Article 4)

A Member State that joins designates or establishes one National Cyber Hub, a single entity under its authority. It may be a CSIRT, a national cyber crisis management authority, another competent authority or another entity. The hub may cooperate with private entities and may request telemetry, sensor and logging data where Union and national law allow.

Recital 14 adds that National Cyber Hubs are neither obliged nor empowered by the Regulation to enforce such requests.

Hosting Consortium and Cross-Border Cyber Hubs (Article 5)

At least three Member States may form a Hosting Consortium. They sign a written consortium agreement that establishes a Cross-Border Cyber Hub, a multi-country platform linking their National Cyber Hubs. Other Member States may join with the consent of the members. The ECCC keeps ownership of the tools jointly procured with the consortium.

Information sharing (Article 6)

The consortium agreement fixes what is shared and how, with a governance framework and targets for developing tools such as artificial intelligence and data analytics. Hubs conclude cooperation agreements with each other. ENISA must issue interoperability guidelines on formats and protocols without undue delay and in any event by 5 February 2026.

Early warnings and security (Articles 7 and 8)

Cross-Border Cyber Hubs cooperate closely with the CSIRTs network. When a hub learns of a potential or ongoing large-scale incident, it passes information and early warnings to Member State authorities and the Commission through EU-CyCLONe and the CSIRTs network, without undue delay. Participating Member States must keep the network secure and make sure sharing with non-public bodies does not harm Union or national security interests.

The Emergency Mechanism

Articles 10 to 13 and 18: preparing for serious incidents and helping each other

The Mechanism supports the Union's resilience and, in a spirit of solidarity, the preparation for and mitigation of the short-term impact of significant, large-scale and large-scale-equivalent incidents (Article 10). It is financed by the Digital Europe Programme and implemented mainly through the ECCC. The Reserve is the exception: it is implemented by the Commission and ENISA. Article 11 lists four types of action.

Article 12
Coordinated preparedness testing
Voluntary testing of entities in sectors of high criticality, for example penetration testing and threat assessment, supported mainly by grants.
Article 13
Other preparedness actions
Vulnerability monitoring, risk monitoring, exercises and training, for sectors of high criticality and other critical sectors, on request and mainly by grants.
Article 14
Response through the EU Cybersecurity Reserve
Response and initial recovery services from trusted managed security service providers, covered in the next section.
Article 18
Mutual assistance
Grants for technical assistance from one Member State to another affected by a significant or large-scale incident.
How testing is set up (Article 12)
  • The Commission, after consulting the NIS Cooperation Group, EU-CyCLONe and ENISA, picks the sectors or sub-sectors, from the sectors of high criticality in Annex I to NIS 2, for which a call for proposals may be issued. It takes coordinated risk assessments and resilience testing into account.
  • Member States take part in those calls voluntarily.
  • The NIS Cooperation Group, with the Commission, the High Representative and ENISA, develops common risk scenarios and methodologies.
  • Where testing leads to recommendations, the Member State authority responsible reviews the follow-up where appropriate.
How mutual assistance works (Article 18)
  • Support covers technical assistance between Member States, including the cases referred to in NIS 2 Article 11(3), point (f).
  • It is provided as grants, under the conditions of the Digital Europe work programmes.
  • In duly justified cases, costs may be eligible even if incurred before the grant application, under the amended Article 19 of the Digital Europe Programme Regulation.

The EU Cybersecurity Reserve

Articles 14 to 17: a pool of trusted private responders that public users can call on

What it is

The Reserve consists of response services from trusted managed security service providers selected under the criteria of Article 17(2). It helps users respond to, and begin recovery from, significant, large-scale and large-scale-equivalent incidents. It may include pre-committed services, which are convertible into preparedness services on incident prevention and response if they are not used for incident response during the period they are pre-committed. It is deployable, on request, in all Member States, in EU institutions, bodies, offices and agencies, and in associated third countries under Article 19.

Who can ask, and how

User (Article 14(3)) How the request is sent (Article 15(3)) Who receives the summary report (Article 16(9))
Member States' cyber crisis management authorities and CSIRTs Through the single point of contact designated under NIS 2 Article 8(3). The Commission, ENISA, the CSIRTs network and EU-CyCLONe.
CERT-EU By CERT-EU itself. The Commission, ENISA and the IICB.
Competent authorities of DEP-associated third countries Through the single point of contact referred to in Article 19(9). The Commission, which passes it to the Council and the High Representative.

Requests from third-country users are assessed by the Commission under Article 19(10). The 48-hour answer in Article 16(1) applies to Member State users and CERT-EU.

The 48-hour answer (Article 16)

The contracting authority assesses each request from a Member State user or CERT-EU and answers without delay and in any event within 48 hours, then informs the Council and the Commission of the result. A request must describe the affected entity and impact, the service wanted, and the mitigation already taken (Article 15(5)). ENISA prepares a request template, and the Commission may specify procedures by implementing act.

How requests are ranked (Article 16(3) and (4))
  • scale and severity of the incident
  • type of entity, with higher priority for essential entities under NIS 2
  • potential impact on Member States, EU bodies or third countries
  • cross-border nature and risk of spillover
  • measures the user has already taken

If two or more concurrent requests are assessed as equal, requests from Member State users go first.

Agreements and liability (Article 16(5) to (7))

Services are delivered under specific agreements between the provider and the user, which include liability conditions and follow templates prepared by ENISA. The Commission, ENISA and the users bear no contractual liability for damage caused to third parties by the services.

Reporting (Article 16(9) and (11))

Users send a summary report within 2 months of the end of support. For Member State and EU users, the contracting authority reports to the NIS Cooperation Group on use and results at least twice a year.

Who runs it (Article 14(4) to (7))

The Commission has overall responsibility and sets priorities with the NIS Cooperation Group, reviewed every 2 years. Under a contribution agreement, the Commission shall entrust the operation and administration of the Reserve, in full or in part, to ENISA (Article 14(5)). ENISA maps the services needed at least every 2 years.

Delegated acts (Article 14(7))

The Commission may adopt delegated acts to specify the types and the number of response services the Reserve requires, taking the ENISA mapping into account. The power is conferred for five years from 5 February 2025 (Article 23(2)).

The 11 criteria for trusted providers (Article 17(2))

  1. (a) People. Personnel with the highest degree of professional integrity, independence, responsibility and technical competence, with permanence and continuity of expertise.
  2. (b) Classified and confidential information. Compliance with the rules on classified information, and measures to protect confidential information about the service, including evidence, findings and reports.
  3. (c) Governance. A transparent governing structure, not likely to compromise impartiality or quality or to cause conflicts of interest.
  4. (d) Security clearance. Appropriate clearance, at least for personnel intended for deployment, where a Member State requires it.
  5. (e) IT security. The relevant level of security for its IT systems.
  6. (f) Hardware and software. Equipment that contains no known exploitable vulnerabilities, includes the latest security updates and complies with any applicable provision of the Cyber Resilience Act, Regulation (EU) 2024/2847.
  7. (g) Experience. Experience in delivering similar services to national authorities and to entities in sectors of high criticality or other critical sectors.
  8. (h) Speed. Ability to provide the service within a short timeframe in the Member States where it can deliver.
  9. (i) Languages. Ability to work in one or more official languages of the Union institutions or of a Member State, as required.
  10. (j) Certification. Once a European cybersecurity certification scheme for managed security services exists under Regulation (EU) 2019/881, certification under that scheme within 2 years of the date the scheme applies.
  11. (k) Conversion conditions. The tender states the conditions for converting unused incident response services into preparedness services such as exercises or training.

Article 17(1) adds three procurement principles: services deployable in all Member States, protection of the essential security interests of the Union and its Member States, and Union added value. The contracting authority may add criteria in close cooperation with Member States (Article 17(3)).

Third countries

Article 19: support for associated third countries only after a Council decision

  1. The association agreement must provide for it. A Digital Europe Programme associated third country may request Reserve support only where its association agreement provides for participation in the Reserve and requires it to meet the duties in Article 19(2) and (9).
  2. The country informs the Commission. Within 3 months of concluding the agreement, and in any case before receiving support, it provides information on its cyber resilience and risk management capabilities, then updates it at least once a year.
  3. The Commission assesses three criteria at least once a year. Whether the country complies with the agreement, whether it has taken adequate steps to prepare for incidents, and whether support is consistent with the Union's policy towards that country and with other security policies. It consults the High Representative on the third criterion.
  4. The Commission proposes, the Council decides. If all conditions are met, the Commission proposes an implementing act, and the Council may adopt it. The act applies for a maximum of one year and may be renewed. It may cap support for a single request at a limit of no less than 75 days.
  5. Speed. The Council acts expeditiously and, as a rule, within eight weeks of the Commission proposal.
  6. Support. The Commission assesses each request and the contracting authority may support a third country only while a Council implementing act for that country is in force (Article 19(10)).

Implementation note (source: ENISA)

ENISA reports that Moldova is so far the only eligible Digital Europe Programme associated third country, and that the Reserve was first deployed there in September 2025. This note comes from the ENISA FAQ on the Reserve, not from the text of the Regulation.

Incident review

Chapter IV, Article 21: ENISA looks back at a serious incident so others can learn

Who asks and who reviews

At the request of the Commission or EU-CyCLONe, ENISA reviews and assesses cyber threats, known exploitable vulnerabilities and mitigation actions for a specific significant or large-scale incident. It works with the support of the CSIRTs network and with the approval of the Member States concerned, and it gathers feedback from stakeholders including industry and, where appropriate, affected entities.

Who receives the report

ENISA delivers an incident review report to EU-CyCLONe, the CSIRTs network, the Member States concerned and the Commission. Where an incident affects an associated third country, ENISA also gives the report to the Council, and the Commission gives it to the High Representative.

What the report contains

The main causes, the known exploitable vulnerabilities and the lessons learned, with recommendations to improve the Union's cyber posture where appropriate. Data can be anonymised if the Member States or users affected ask.

What it must leave out

The report must not include details about actively exploited vulnerabilities that remain unpatched. ENISA may issue a public version, which contains only reliable public information, or other reliable information with the consent of those concerned.

Funding rules

Article 22: how the Act amends the Digital Europe Programme Regulation (EU) 2021/694

The budget lines as amended (Article 9(2) of the Programme Regulation)

Specific Objective Amount
Specific Objective 2, Artificial Intelligence EUR 1 760 806 000
Specific Objective 3, Cybersecurity and Trust EUR 1 372 020 000
Specific Objective 4, Advanced Digital Skills EUR 482 640 000
New operational objectives

Specific Objective 3 gains two operational objectives: to support the European Cybersecurity Alert System, and to establish and operate the Cybersecurity Emergency Mechanism including the Reserve. The Annex rewrites the actions and indicators for Specific Objective 3 accordingly.

Carry-over

Unused commitment and payment appropriations for the Reserve and for mutual assistance are automatically carried over and may be committed and paid up to 31 December of the following financial year.

Third-country-controlled entities

The usual restriction on entities established in the Union but controlled from third countries is lifted for Alert System and Reserve actions only if two conditions are met. There must be a real risk that the tools, infrastructure, services, technology, expertise or capacity will not be available from entities controlled by Member States or their nationals, and the security risk must be proportionate to the benefits and not undermine the essential security interests of the Union and its Member States.

Procurement and grants

For the Reserve, the Commission and ENISA may act as a central purchasing body, or as wholesaler, on behalf of associated third countries and of EU institutions, bodies, offices and agencies. A request from a single third country or a single EU body is enough to mandate them. The ECCC may award grants directly, without a call for proposals, to the selected Member States, to Hosting Consortia and, for the Mechanism, to Member States.

Conflict rule (new Article 16a)

For Alert System actions, Articles 4, 5 and 9 of the Cyber Solidarity Act prevail if they conflict with the Programme Regulation. For the Reserve, Article 19 of the Cyber Solidarity Act prevails on the participation of associated third countries.

Implementation

What the Commission and ENISA say has happened, each point attributed to its source

The Reserve (source: ENISA)

According to ENISA, the Commission entrusted ENISA with administering and operating the EU Cybersecurity Reserve under a contribution agreement signed in 2025, worth about EUR 36 million over three years. ENISA is the contracting authority.

The hubs (source: Commission policy page)

According to the Commission, in a first phase launched in November 2022 under the Digital Europe Programme, two consortia of cross-border Security Operations Centres, now called Cyber Hubs, were selected.

Key definition

The tier that lets EU institutions and third countries use the Reserve

Large-scale-equivalent cybersecurity incident (Article 2(13))

"'large-scale-equivalent cybersecurity incident' means, in the case of Union institutions, bodies, offices and agencies, a major incident and, in the case of DEP-associated third countries, an incident which causes a level of disruption that exceeds the capacity of the DEP-associated third country concerned to respond to it"

Member States use the significant and large-scale tiers from NIS 2. This third tier gives EU institutions and associated third countries an equivalent threshold.
