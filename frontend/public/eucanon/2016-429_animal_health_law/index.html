The Animal Health Law

Regulation (EU) 2016/429 replaced around forty scattered EU directives and regulations with a single principles-based framework for preventing and controlling transmissible animal diseases, applied from 21 April 2021.

Overview

One Health, consolidation and a five-year runway

From patchwork to single framework

Before the Animal Health Law (AHL) entered the statute book, EU animal-health legislation was described in recital 5 as "a series of linked and interrelated basic acts" with no overarching framework. Member States applied dozens of separate directives and regulations, each designed for a specific disease or species, resulting in fragmentation and overlap. The AHL swept approximately forty acts into a single regulation, replacing them with a coherent, principles-based architecture that runs from disease listing through operator responsibilities, surveillance and eradication programmes, traceability, intra-Union movements, third-country entry conditions, and emergency measures.

Because the AHL is a Regulation it is directly applicable in every Member State from its application date without national implementing legislation. There is no transposition requirement. This contrasts sharply with the Directives it replaced, several of which dated back to 1964 and had accumulated layers of national variation.

One Health at the centre

Recital 9 places the AHL explicitly within the One Health principle, recognising that human, animal and environmental health are interconnected. This is not merely rhetorical: the legal bases include Article 168(4)(b) TFEU (public health measures in the veterinary and phytosanitary fields) alongside Article 43(2) TFEU (agricultural policy) and Article 114 TFEU (internal market). The AHL treats antimicrobial resistance as if it were a transmissible disease (recital 32), and covers zoonoses without prejudice to the existing EU zoonoses-monitoring Directive (2003/99/EC) and salmonella Regulation (2160/2003).

The five-year gap: why 2016 to 2021?

The regulation was signed at Strasbourg on 9 March 2016 and entered into force on 20 April 2016, but the substantive requirements did not apply until 21 April 2021. Recital 172 explains the logic: the AHL is a framework act, and its operative rules live in a large body of subsequent Commission delegated and implementing acts (the "animal health package"). Member States and operators needed at least twenty-four months after those secondary acts to adapt systems, registers and procedures. The Commission used the five-year window to adopt approximately a dozen delegated and implementing regulations covering disease categorisation, terrestrial and aquatic animals, germinal products, entry into the Union, pet movements and more.

Two provisions applied from entry into force rather than from 2021: Article 270(1) (partial repeal of the sheep and goat identification Regulation) and Article 274 (references). Penalties notified by Member States by 22 April 2022. The Commission's evaluation report was due by 22 April 2026 (Article 282).

International alignment: SPS Agreement, OIE/WOAH, EFSA

Recitals 12 and 13 anchor the AHL in the WTO SPS Agreement and the standards of the World Organisation for Animal Health (OIE, now WOAH). The OIE compartmentalisation concept for disease-free recognition is generalised across species and diseases in Articles 36 to 42. Independent risk assessment informed by EFSA (recital 15) underpins the listing criteria and management measures.

What it covers

Five thematic pillars of the Animal Health Law

Listed diseases and A to E categorisation
Five diseases named in Article 5(1). All listed diseases sorted into five management categories (A to E) determining which control obligations apply. Emerging diseases caught even before formal listing (Article 6).
Responsibilities and biosecurity
Operators bear primary responsibility for kept animals, prudent use of veterinary medicines, and biosecurity measures. Veterinarians must prevent, detect and respond to disease. Competent authorities must have qualified staff, laboratories and resources.
Notification, surveillance and disease-free status
Immediate notification within Member States and Union-level reporting via ADIS. Compulsory (Category B) and optional (Category C) eradication programmes. Member States, zones and compartments may be recognised as disease-free.
Registration, traceability and TRACES
Operators register establishments and receive a unique registration number. Bovine, ovine, equine and porcine animals individually identified. Cross-border movements pre-notified via TRACES, the EU computerised veterinary system.
Entry into the Union and pet movements
Animals enter only from listed third countries, approved establishments, with an official health certificate. Non-commercial movement of up to five pet dogs, cats or ferrets requires a microchip, rabies vaccination and an EU pet passport.

The five named diseases (Article 5(1))

  • Foot-and-mouth disease
  • Classical swine fever
  • African swine fever
  • Highly pathogenic avian influenza
  • African horse sickness

All other listed diseases appear in Annex II and may be amended by delegated act against the criteria in Article 5(3) and the assessment parameters in Article 7.

Pet movements

  • Maximum five pet animals per non-commercial movement
  • Species covered: dogs, cats and ferrets (Annex I Part A)
  • Mandatory: microchip identification, rabies vaccination and an EU pet passport
  • Exception for competitions, exhibitions and sporting events (animals over six months old, registered)
  • Bilateral derogations for Italy/San Marino/Vatican, France/Monaco/Andorra, Spain/Andorra, Sweden/Norway, Denmark/Faroe Islands/Greenland

Why it exists

From the 2007 Animal Health Strategy to the 2016 consolidation

Fragmentation and the call for a single rulebook

EU animal-health law had accumulated over decades: different directives for cattle, swine, poultry, aquatic animals, sheep, goats and more, each shaped by the disease or species it addressed. Recital 5 of the AHL acknowledges this directly, describing the pre-existing framework as a series of linked but uncoordinated acts. The result was complexity for operators, variation across Member States, and gaps at the interfaces between species-specific rules.

The Commission's 2007 to 2013 Animal Health Strategy, subtitled "Prevention is better than cure", called for simplification and consolidation. A legislative proposal eventually followed, leading to the AHL adopted in 2016. The strategy's title captures the regulation's emphasis: investment in biosecurity, surveillance and early detection costs far less than emergency response once a disease spreads.

One Health: the conceptual anchor

Recital 9 places the AHL within the One Health framework, which recognises that human health, animal health and the health of ecosystems are interconnected. This goes beyond a rhetorical commitment: Article 168(4)(b) TFEU appears in the legal basis, bringing public health considerations into what would otherwise be a purely agricultural regulation. Antimicrobial resistance is treated as if it were a transmissible disease (recital 32), reflecting the cross-cutting nature of that threat. Zoonoses are covered without displacing the existing EU frameworks for zoonoses monitoring and salmonella control.

Alignment with international standards

The EU is a member of the WTO and participates in the SPS Agreement framework. The AHL aligns its listed-disease criteria and disease-free recognition rules with WOAH (formerly OIE) standards (recitals 12, 13), enabling the Union to defend its import conditions before the WTO Dispute Settlement Body. Independent risk assessment by EFSA (the European Food Safety Authority) underpins all listing and delisting decisions, keeping the scientific basis transparent and challengeable (recital 15).

Scope

Articles 2 to 4: what falls inside and outside the Animal Health Law

What the AHL covers
  • Kept and wild animals including birds, terrestrial mammals, bees and bumble bees (terrestrial); fish, molluscs and crustaceans (aquatic)
  • Germinal products: semen, oocytes, embryos and hatching eggs
  • Products of animal origin
  • Animal by-products and derived products (without prejudice to Regulation (EC) 1069/2009)
  • Facilities, means of transport, equipment and all other paths of infection
  • Transmissible diseases including zoonoses, without prejudice to the TSE Regulation (999/2001), the zoonoses-monitoring Directive (2003/99/EC), the salmonella Regulation (2160/2003) and the cross-border-health-threats Decision (1082/2013/EU)
  • Antimicrobial resistance, treated as if it were a transmissible disease (recital 32)
Article 4: 56 definitions

Article 4 sets out fifty-six definitions that apply throughout the AHL and its delegated acts. Key terms include:

  • Terrestrial animals: birds, terrestrial mammals, bees and bumble bees
  • Aquatic animals: fish, molluscs and crustaceans
  • Kept animals: animals held, fattened, bred, used or kept by humans
  • Wild animals: animals not kept by humans
  • Biosecurity: the set of management and physical measures designed to reduce the risk of introduction, development and spread of disease
  • Operator: any natural or legal person responsible for animals or products
  • Zone and compartment: geographic/epidemiological units used for disease-free recognition (generalising the OIE compartmentalisation concept)
  • Outbreak: official confirmation of one or more cases in an establishment or location
  • TRACES: the EU integrated computerised veterinary system for notification, certification and traceability

Disease categories A to E

Article 9: the five management classes that determine which control obligations apply

How categorisation works

Article 9 categorises listed diseases into five management classes. The detailed assignment of diseases to categories is set by Commission implementing rules. Critically, a single disease can fall into several categories simultaneously; the categories determine which provisions of the AHL apply, not a single definitive status.

Category Description Example from the AHL Key obligation
A Diseases not normally present in the Union requiring immediate eradication when detected Foot-and-mouth disease; classical swine fever Emergency slaughter, movement restrictions, restricted zones
B Diseases to be controlled in all Member States with the goal of Union-wide eradication Brucellosis Compulsory eradication programmes, approved by the Commission
C Diseases relevant to some Member States, where measures prevent spread to disease-free areas Infectious bovine rhinotracheitis Optional eradication programmes; movement restrictions to protect free areas
D Diseases for which movement restrictions and entry controls are needed to prevent spread Various diseases subject to movement-linked restrictions Movement restrictions, animal health certification requirements
E Diseases requiring ongoing surveillance within the Union Diseases kept under watch for early warning Surveillance programmes; data collection and reporting via ADIS

Emerging diseases: Article 6

Article 6 ensures that the AHL's framework applies even to diseases that have not yet been formally listed. Where a new disease could pose a significant risk, the competent authority may apply control measures before listing is complete. This prevents the regulation's applicability from being hostage to the formal listing process during an emerging outbreak.

Responsibilities and biosecurity

Articles 10 to 17: operators, veterinarians, competent authorities and laboratories

Operators

Operators bear primary responsibility for the health of kept animals. This includes prudent use of veterinary medicines, implementation of biosecurity measures (both physical barriers and management practices), and maintaining adequate animal-health knowledge. Article 10 sets the general obligation; Article 11 specifies biosecurity requirements.

Veterinarians

Veterinary professionals and aquatic animal health professionals must prevent, detect and respond to disease, and cooperate with competent authorities (Article 12). Official veterinarians sign animal health certificates for cross-border movements and play a central role in outbreak investigation and control.

Member State authorities and laboratories

Competent authorities must have qualified staff, laboratories and resources (Article 13). Certain activities may be delegated to non-official veterinarians (Article 14). Laboratories handling disease agents must apply biosecurity, biosafety and bio-containment rules (Article 16). A network of Union and national reference laboratories underpins diagnosis (Article 17).

Notification, surveillance and disease-free status

Part II, Articles 18 to 42: the detection-to-eradication chain

Notification and ADIS

Suspected and confirmed disease cases must be notified immediately within the Member State and, where required, to the Union level. Notification is supported by the Animal Disease Information System (ADIS), the EU computerised platform for disease reporting and epidemiological analysis (Articles 18 to 23).

Surveillance programmes

Operators conduct basic observation; veterinarians carry out animal health visits. Competent authorities run systematic surveillance programmes, including Union surveillance programmes approved by the Commission (Articles 24 to 30). Surveillance data feeds ADIS and informs risk assessment.

Eradication programmes

Compulsory eradication programmes apply to Category B diseases in all Member States, with Commission approval (Articles 31 to 35). Optional eradication programmes apply to Category C diseases in affected Member States. Both types run for defined periods and must demonstrate measurable progress.

Disease-free status: zones and compartments

Member States, zones (geographic units) and compartments (epidemiological units defined by biosecurity rather than geography) may be officially recognised as disease-free. The AHL generalises the OIE compartmentalisation concept across all listed species and diseases (Articles 36 to 42). Free status carries movement advantages and is linked to the disease-categorisation system.

Awareness, preparedness and disease control

Part III, Articles 43 to 83: contingency plans, vaccine banks and the graduated response

Contingency plans and simulation exercises

Member States must maintain contingency plans for Category A diseases, approved by the Commission, and conduct regular simulation exercises (Articles 43 to 45). Plans must cover command structures, resource availability, laboratory capacity and public communication. Simulation exercises test the plans and feed lessons back into preparedness.

Veterinary medicinal products and Union vaccine banks

Articles 46 and 47 address veterinary medicinal products including vaccines, hyper-immune sera and antimicrobials, with the possibility of restrictions where their use would interfere with eradication objectives. Articles 48 to 52 establish Union antigen, vaccine and diagnostic reagent banks for Category A diseases. Bank stock information is classified for bio-terrorism security reasons.

Graduated disease control: from suspicion to restricted zones

Articles 53 to 83 set out a graduated response machinery for Category A disease outbreaks:

  • Suspicion triggers an investigation; official confirmation triggers immediate control
  • Control measures: killing or depopulation, disposal of carcasses, cleaning and disinfection
  • Restricted zones: a protection zone (close to the outbreak) and a surveillance zone (wider perimeter) with movement restrictions and enhanced monitoring
  • Emergency vaccination: used where the epidemiological situation warrants it, either suppressive (vaccinate-to-die) or protective (vaccinate-to-live)
  • Special rules for wild animals as reservoirs of infection

Registration, traceability and movements

Part IV, Articles 84 to 228: the identification and movement control architecture

Registration and approval

Operators must register establishments, transporters of ungulates, and assembly operators before starting activity. The competent authority assigns a unique registration number (Articles 84 to 93). Higher-risk establishments, including assembly operations, germinal product establishments, hatcheries and certain poultry establishments moving animals between Member States, require prior approval after an on-site visit (Articles 94 to 100).

Record-keeping: at least three years

Operators, germinal-product establishments, transporters and assembly operators must keep records for at least three years (Articles 102 to 107). Records cover animal inventories, movements in and out, treatments, deaths, and biosecurity measures. They must be available for competent authority inspection.

Species-specific identification systems (Articles 108 to 123)

Member States run identification-and-registration systems with computer databases. The requirements differ by species:

  • Bovine animals: individual physical identification (two ear tags) plus an identification document (the bovine passport)
  • Ovine and caprine animals: a physical means of identification plus a movement document
  • Equine animals: a unique code plus a single lifetime identification document (the equine passport)
  • Porcine animals: establishment-level identification, not individual tagging
Movements within the Union and TRACES

Intra-Union movements of animals must meet general health requirements, transport biosecurity rules and, for higher-risk species, an animal health certificate verified and signed by an official veterinarian. Lower-risk movements may use a self-declaration document. Cross-border movements must be pre-notified in advance through TRACES, the EU integrated computerised veterinary system (Articles 124 to 154). Aquatic animals follow the same principles under Title II (Articles 172 to 226).

Entry into the Union and export

Part V, Articles 229 to 243: conditions for third-country imports and EU exports

Third-country entry conditions

Animals, germinal products and products of animal origin may enter the Union only from listed third countries and territories. Article 230 sets out the listing criteria including the country's disease status, its OIE reporting obligations, compliance history, its veterinary authority's guarantees and the equivalence of its animal-health legislation with EU standards. Imports must originate from approved establishments, meet requirements that are at least as stringent as those applying within the Union, and be accompanied by an animal health certificate issued by the third-country official veterinarian. Electronic certificates are issued via TRACES. Disease agents, plant material and means of transport are also covered.

Export conditions

Article 243 governs exports from the Union. Consignments must comply with intra-Union animal-health rules plus the requirements of the destination country. Where a destination country requires additional certifications, competent authorities issue certificates covering those requirements, provided they do not conflict with EU rules.

Non-commercial pet movements

Part VI, Articles 244 to 256: the EU pet passport, the five-pet rule and bilateral derogations

The EU pet passport and the five-pet rule

A maximum of five pet animals of the Annex I Part A species (dogs, cats and ferrets) may travel in a single non-commercial movement (Article 246). An exception applies for competitions, exhibitions or sporting events provided the animals are over six months old and registered for the event. Pets must be individually identified by microchip, meet preventive and risk-mitigation measures, notably rabies vaccination where relevant, and carry an EU pet passport (the identification document for pets) (Articles 247 to 250).

Bilateral derogations for neighbouring territories

Article 251 preserves special arrangements for movements between:

  • Italy and San Marino or the Vatican City
  • France and Monaco or Andorra
  • Spain and Andorra
  • Sweden and Norway
  • Denmark and the Faroe Islands or Greenland

These bilateral arrangements reflect practical realities of cross-border communities and were maintained from the previous pet-movement Regulation (EU) 576/2013 which the AHL replaced.

Legal bases and application timeline

The treaty foundations and the key procedural dates of Regulation (EU) 2016/429

Three TFEU legal bases

  • Article 43(2) TFEU (common agricultural policy): the core basis for animal-health legislation affecting agricultural production
  • Article 114 TFEU (internal market approximation): justifies harmonised rules for the intra-Union movement of animals and products
  • Article 168(4)(b) TFEU (public health measures in the veterinary and phytosanitary fields): anchors the One Health dimension, including zoonoses and antimicrobial resistance

Procedure and signatories

  • Ordinary legislative procedure (codecision)
  • European Economic and Social Committee opinion obtained
  • Committee of the Regions consulted
  • Signed by M. Schulz, President of the European Parliament
  • Signed by J.A. Hennis-Plasschaert, President of the Council
  • Delegated-act empowerments run from 20 April 2016 for five years, tacitly renewable; approximately 150 delegated-act empowerments in Article 264
  • Comitology: Commission assisted by SCoPAFF (Standing Committee on Plants, Animals, Food and Feed) under Regulation (EU) 182/2011

Key definitions

Essential terms from Article 4 of Regulation (EU) 2016/429

Biosecurity
The set of management and physical measures designed to reduce the risk of introduction, development and spread of diseases to, within and from a population of kept animals, as well as the risk arising from wild animals, other species or humans.
Zone
A clearly delineated part of the territory of one or more Member States, defined by precise geographic features, containing an animal sub-population with a distinct disease status for official disease-free recognition purposes.
Compartment
An animal sub-population defined not by geography alone but by common biosecurity management practices, under the direct control of the operator. Generalises the OIE concept; enables disease-free status even when surrounding territory is not free.
TRACES
Trade Control and Expert System: the EU integrated computerised veterinary system for the notification, certification and traceability of intra-Union movements and third-country imports of animals, germinal products and products of animal origin. The electronic successor to paper-based certificates.
Zoonosis
A disease or infection that is naturally transmissible between animals and humans (directly or via a vector). The AHL covers zoonoses without displacing the specific EU frameworks for zoonoses monitoring (Directive 2003/99/EC) and salmonella control (Regulation (EC) 2160/2003).
Outbreak
The official confirmation of the presence of a listed disease in one or more kept or wild animals in an establishment or other location. Triggers the graduated response machinery in Part III of the AHL.
Restricted zone
A zone established around an outbreak, comprising a protection zone (close to the affected establishment) and a surveillance zone (wider perimeter), within which movement restrictions and enhanced surveillance apply for a defined period.
ADIS
Animal Disease Information System: the EU computerised platform for disease notification and epidemiological reporting, supporting the immediate-notification obligations in Articles 18 to 23 and Union-level surveillance data aggregation.
Operator
Any natural or legal person who is responsible, even temporarily, for kept animals or germinal products, including owners, keepers, transporters, assembly operators and dealers. Primary responsibility for animal health rests with the operator under Articles 10 and 11.

Timeline

From the 2007 Animal Health Strategy to application in 2021 and beyond

2007 to 2013
Commission publishes the Animal Health Strategy ("Prevention is better than cure"), calling for consolidation of EU animal-health law and a shift to prevention-based frameworks.
9 March 2016
Regulation (EU) 2016/429 adopted at Strasbourg. Signed by the Presidents of the European Parliament (M. Schulz) and the Council (J.A. Hennis-Plasschaert). Published in OJ L 84 on 31 March 2016.
20 April 2016
Entry into force. Delegated-act empowerments begin to run. The Commission starts adopting the "animal health package" of delegated and implementing regulations needed before application.
2016 to 2021
Commission adopts approximately a dozen delegated and implementing regulations covering disease categorisation, terrestrial animals, aquatic animals, germinal products, entry into the Union, pet movements and more. Member States adapt national registers and systems.
21 April 2021
The Animal Health Law applies. Around thirty predecessor acts are repealed including Directive 64/432/EEC, Directive 2006/88/EC, Regulation (EC) 21/2004 and Regulation (EU) 576/2013. References to repealed acts are read via the Annex V correlation table.
22 April 2022
Deadline for Member States to notify the Commission of the penalty rules they have adopted in application of Article 268 (effective, proportionate and dissuasive penalties).
22 April 2026
The Commission was due to report to the European Parliament and the Council on the application, effectiveness and appropriateness of the AHL (Article 282 evaluation clause).

Glossary

Key abbreviations and instruments referenced in the Animal Health Law

AHL
The Animal Health Law: short name for Regulation (EU) 2016/429. The parenthetical title appears in the regulation's heading.
ADIS
Animal Disease Information System: the EU computerised platform for disease notification, supporting the immediate-notification obligations in Part II of the AHL.
EFSA
European Food Safety Authority. Provides independent risk assessments underpinning disease listing, categorisation and management measure decisions under the AHL (recital 15).
OIE / WOAH
World Organisation for Animal Health (formerly OIE). The AHL aligns its disease-free recognition and listing criteria with WOAH standards to ensure compatibility with WTO SPS Agreement obligations (recitals 12, 13).
SCoPAFF
Standing Committee on Plants, Animals, Food and Feed. Assists the Commission in adopting implementing acts under the AHL, pursuant to Regulation (EU) 182/2011 on comitology procedures.
SPS Agreement
WTO Agreement on Sanitary and Phytosanitary Measures. The AHL's import-condition provisions are designed to be SPS-compatible, ensuring the EU can defend its animal-health import restrictions before WTO dispute panels.
TRACES
Trade Control and Expert System: the EU's integrated computerised veterinary system for pre-notification, certification and traceability of animal movements within the Union and from third countries.
TFEU
Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union. The AHL rests on three TFEU legal bases: Article 43(2) (agriculture), Article 114 (internal market) and Article 168(4)(b) (public health in veterinary and phytosanitary fields).
One Health
The integrated approach recognising that human, animal and environmental health are interconnected. Anchored in recital 9 of the AHL and reflected in the inclusion of Article 168(4)(b) TFEU as a legal basis alongside the agricultural and internal market bases.
