The DPP Registry Implementing Regulation: the Commission's rulebook for one shared passport database

Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) 2026/1778 lays down how the EU's single digital product passport registry actually works: who can use it, how they prove who they are, how a passport gets registered, what happens to the data over time, and who is responsible for what. Adopted by the Commission alone on 16 July 2026 under its implementing powers, not through the ordinary legislative procedure, it is the operational rulebook for the registry the ESPR created in Article 13, and it already extends beyond ESPR products to batteries, construction products, toys, and detergents and end-user surfactants.

Overview

What the DPP Registry Implementing Regulation is, why it exists, and how it is structured

The rulebook for the shared registry behind every digital product passport

This Regulation is the Commission's rulebook for the single EU digital product passport registry, the central system that stores the identifiers behind every digital product passport issued across the EU's growing family of product-passport laws. Regulation (EU) 2024/1781, the Ecodesign for Sustainable Products Regulation, the ESPR, created the legal obligation for that registry in its Article 13(1). This Implementing Regulation, adopted a little over two years later, is where the Commission spells out how the registry actually works: who can use it, how they prove who they are, how a passport gets registered, what happens to the data over time, and who is responsible for what.

A Commission implementing act, adopted alone under Article 13(5) ESPR

Unlike the ordinary legislative procedure that produced the ESPR itself, this is a Commission Implementing Regulation: adopted by the Commission alone, signed only by the Commission President, following the examination procedure of the Committee established under Article 73 ESPR. Its legal basis is Article 13(5), second and third subparagraphs, of the ESPR. Adopted at Brussels on 16 July 2026 and published in OJ L, 2026/1778 the following day, it runs to 25 Articles, Articles 1 to 24 with an Article 6a inserted between Articles 6 and 7, with no chapters and no annexes.

Two dates, not one: registry go-live versus this Regulation's own entry into force

The registry itself became operational on 20 July 2026, meeting the ESPR's own Article 13(1) deadline of 19 July 2026 for the Commission to have the registry established. This Implementing Regulation's own rules, in contrast, formally entered into force on 6 August 2026, the twentieth day after its 17 July 2026 OJ publication (Article 24). In short, the registry launched under the ESPR's direct mandate roughly two weeks before this Regulation's detailed governance rules took legal effect. Always specify which date is meant when citing "entry into force" for this file.

One of the 13 acts in the EU Digital Product Passport architecture

The registry is not exclusive to ESPR products. Article 1(1) extends these implementation arrangements to any product whose enabling Union law requires a digital product passport registered in the Article 13 ESPR registry: products under ESPR delegated acts, batteries under Article 77 of the Batteries Regulation (EU) 2023/1542, construction products under Article 76 of the Construction Products Regulation (EU) 2024/3110, toys under Article 19 of the Toy Safety Regulation (EU) 2025/2509, and detergents and end-user surfactants under Article 21 of the Detergents Regulation (EU) 2026/405. That makes this Regulation one of the 13 acts in Brubru's EU Digital Product Passport legal architecture, the one that operationalises the central registry itself rather than a sectoral passport.

Scope, definitions and registry architecture (Arts 1-3)

Who this Regulation applies to, and the nine components the registry is built from

Scope beyond the ESPR (Art 1)

Article 1(1) extends these implementation arrangements to any product whose enabling Union law requires a digital product passport to be registered in the Article 13 ESPR registry: products covered by ESPR delegated acts, batteries under Article 77 of the Batteries Regulation, construction products under Article 76 of the Construction Products Regulation, toys under Article 19 of the Toy Safety Regulation, detergents and end-user surfactants under Article 21 of the Detergents Regulation, and any other product a future Union act brings within the same registry. This Regulation is the shared technical and procedural backbone underneath every sectoral digital product passport obligation, however different those sectoral rules are in substance.

The registry's nine components (Art 3)

Recital 1 explains the purpose plainly: the registry stores, securely, at least the unique identifiers behind each digital product passport. Article 3 sets out its structure as nine components:

  • a website providing a secure user interface for economic operators, value chain actors, competent national authorities and customs authorities;
  • an API for registering digital product passports and receiving information from the registry;
  • a verification platform confirming the existence and completeness of digital product passports;
  • a scheme for generating unique registration identifiers;
  • a storage component for unique identifiers and commodity codes of products intended for the customs procedure "release for free circulation";
  • a list of verified digital product passport service providers registered in the registry;
  • a semantic repository;
  • a log system;
  • identification and authorisation schemes for registry users.

A decentralised design: identifiers here, passport data elsewhere

Recital 3 notes the registry's design reflects the decentralised nature of the digital product passport system: the registry stores identifiers and metadata centrally, but the underlying passport data itself continues to live with digital product passport service providers, not inside the registry. The Commission is the registry's owner and manager (Article 21), and its data controller for personal data under Regulation (EU) 2018/1725 (recital 13).

Identity verification and user management (Arts 4-6a, 18)

No verification, no registration: every user proves who they are before touching a single passport

Economic operators and value chain actors (Arts 4-5)

Before an economic operator or verified value chain actor, such as a repairer, refurbisher, remanufacturer or recycler, can register anything, it must first pass identity verification. For natural persons acting as sole traders, and for legal persons, the routes differ depending on whether the person must be established in the Union, but all routes run through the eIDAS framework, Regulation (EU) No 910/2014: a qualified electronic signature backed by a qualified certificate, a qualified electronic seal issued by a qualified trust service provider for legal persons, an eIDAS "high" assurance-level electronic identification means, or an electronic attestation of attributes issued under Union law. "Verified" status lasts until the electronic identification means expires, and in any event no longer than three years from verification, whichever comes first. Where the registry is integrated with another EU system using an equivalent verification process, such as EPREL, a repeat verification is not required (recital 11).

Keeping profiles accurate, and transferring responsibility (Arts 6, 6a)

Verified economic operators and verified value chain actors must manage their own electronic verification process and keep registry profile data, including any change to legal representative, accurate, complete and up to date (Article 6(3)-(4)). Article 6a, inserted between Articles 6 and 7, requires a verified economic operator or value chain actor to formally transfer responsibility for its registered digital product passports to another verified actor on any change of ownership or organisational status, so a passport never sits orphaned in the registry after a corporate change.

What the registry stores about every user (Art 18)

Article 18 sets out the personal data the Commission stores on every registry user to verify identity: first and last name, or that of the legal representative; authentication credentials, including login credentials or authentication tokens; postal address of economic operators and value chain actors; email address; and any metadata embedded in uploaded documents that helps identify or verify a user. For natural persons specifically, the registry must also store a personal identifier such as a passport number, national identity card number, national eID number, civil registry number, tax identification number, or an equivalent third-country identifier (Article 18(2)).

National authorities (Art 7)

One official contact point per Member State, appointed by 18 February 2027

A single designated national administrator per country

Competent national authorities and customs authorities get access through a single designated national administrator per Member State, appointed by 18 February 2027 at the latest. This administrator is the sole official contact point for the Commission on that Member State's registry access rights, and may delegate access further within the Member State's own authorities (Article 7). Member States must also ensure an appropriate level of security for any national components used to access the registry, and inform the Commission without undue delay of changes affecting the registry's functioning (Article 22).

Registration and data lifecycle (Arts 8-11)

From registration levels and automatic checks to proof of registration and long-term retention

Registration levels: model, batch or item (Art 8(1)-(5))

Registration must happen at the granularity level, model, batch or item, set out in the applicable Union law for that product. Where a passport is created at item level, the corresponding batch and model identifiers must also be linked, provided a batch or model design exists for the product; products that are unique by nature, such as handmade goods, are exempt from this cascading requirement (recital 14). Where a passport is created at batch level, the model identifier must be linked in the same way. Where the same product is subject to different Union rules setting different granularity requirements, the most granular level applies.

Two registration pathways, one automatic verification (Art 8(6)-(10))

Article 8(6) is explicit: the relevant actor registers a digital product passport either through the secure user interface of the registry, or through the API. Both pathways lead to the same automatic verification and the same outcome. Upon submission, the Commission automatically checks the semantic conformity of the data, the coherence of mandatory data against the values provided, conformity with the required granularity level, the validity of the product's commodity code where relevant, and the link to the digital product passport service provider's back-up. The registry then communicates the outcome back "through the user interface or the API response, depending on the service used" (Article 8(10)).

The unique registration identifier is not proof of compliance

Following successful verification, the registry generates and stores a unique and persistent registration identifier (Article 8(8)-(9)). Recital 16 is deliberately narrow about what the automatic checks prove: they confirm data structure, granularity level and, where relevant, commodity code validity and the service-provider back-up link, "and accordingly, the automated verifications should not be deemed to constitute proof of compliance with the requirements of the Union rules applicable to the product, including with market surveillance rules." That reading is anchored directly in the parent ESPR: Article 13(5) states the unique registration identifier is not, of itself, proof of compliance with the ESPR or any other Union law. A registration identifier proves a passport exists and passed the registry's structural checks; it does not prove the underlying product is lawful.

Proof of registration and data retention (Arts 9-10)

An economic operator can request proof of registration for any digital product passport it is responsible for. That proof is a secure electronic document, sealed with a qualified electronic seal and an electronic time stamp, containing the unique product identifier, the commodity code where relevant, the responsible verified economic operator's identity, the date and time of the latest registration, and a hash of the digital product passport version concerned. It remains downloadable for 90 calendar days from generation, with the option to regenerate it if needed (Article 9). The Commission logs every change to registration data, supports versioning with a time-stamp for each update, and retains registration data for 10 years by default where Union law sets no other duration (Article 10(1)-(3)); a registry user's request to delete their account, once no longer responsible for registry activities, must also be processed (Article 10(4)).

The semantic repository (Art 12)

The one part of the registry that is free and open to everyone, not only verified users

Free access, a shared vocabulary, and a public search service

The semantic repository is a partial exception to the registry's role-gated access. It is the Commission-maintained collection of data models and semantic definitions that gives digital product passport data a common structure, versioning and cross-lingual interpretation across all users. Access to and use of the semantic repository and its APIs must be free of charge (Article 12(7)), and it includes a search service so any user, verified or not, can read, search and retrieve semantic definitions and data structures (Article 12(5)).

Support, logging, availability and security (Arts 13-17)

The graduated accountability regime that keeps the registry auditable and available

Helpdesk support (Art 13)

Support is provided through a Commission helpdesk operating year-round from 08:00 to 20:00 Brussels time, with a fully automated technical support tool due by February 2029 to give 24-hour, year-round coverage. Written exchanges with the helpdesk are stored for six months after a request is closed and made available to market surveillance authorities on request.

A categorised, graduated log system (Art 14)

The Commission runs a mandatory log system covering four categories of registry action: access and authentication entries; data modifications by all registry users; administrative actions, including account creation, changes and deletion, and configuration changes; and data exchange logs. Retention periods are graduated by risk: six months for access and authentication logs, five years for administrative-action and data-exchange logs, and for the full duration of the registration for logs of data modifications. Logs must be made available to competent national authorities and customs authorities for suspected incidents, audits and random security checks, and the Commission must guarantee at least the immutability and confidentiality of the logs.

Availability and maintenance (Art 15)

The registry as a whole must be accessible at all times outside necessary maintenance, with advance notice of planned maintenance published on the registry's public website. In exceptional circumstances, such as a malfunction, cyber-attack or urgent security need, the Commission may suspend access without prior notice, but must record the date and time of any such unavailability and keep that record available on request for at least five years.

Security and misuse, including "massive data download" (Arts 16-17)

Article 16 requires the Commission to prevent unauthorised access to and processing of registry data, detect unauthorised activity, prevent data breaches, and log security events to the Commission's own IT security standards. Article 17 gives the Commission power to act against inappropriate or fraudulent use of the registry, expressly including "massive data download", defined as retrieval of an exceptionally large or complex dataset, typically terabytes to petabytes, that exceeds normal tooling or interferes with monitoring safeguards. Every user who becomes aware of, or reasonably suspects, malicious behaviour must notify the Commission and, where relevant, the affected Member State immediately.

Responsibilities and governance (Arts 19-23)

Four actors, each carrying a distinct set of duties for the registry's data and its lifecycle

The verified economic operator's duty of accuracy (Art 19)

A verified economic operator must ensure the information it submits at registration is accurate and complete, keep it up to date, and act as controller of the data it submits (Article 19(1)-(2), (5)).

The Commission's role as registry owner and manager (Art 21)

The Commission owns and manages the registry's full lifecycle, development, availability, monitoring, updating, maintenance and hosting, and processes registry data securely and lawfully. The Commission also has access to registry data to obtain information necessary for measures under other EU legislative acts, including market surveillance, consumer protection and customs compliance (Article 21(3)).

Member State responsibilities (Art 22)

Each Member State must ensure an appropriate level of security for any national components used to access the registry, and must inform the Commission without undue delay of changes affecting the registry's functioning.

A recurring evaluation cycle (Art 23)

As part of the wider ESPR monitoring and evaluation exercise, the Commission must deliver its first evaluation of this Regulation by the end of 2032, and every six years thereafter (Article 23).

The practical effect

The registry began operating under the ESPR's direct mandate before the detailed governance rulebook in this Regulation had formally taken legal effect. Two further forward-looking dates sit downstream of go-live: Member States must appoint their designated national administrator by 18 February 2027 (Article 7(1)), and the Commission must deliver its first evaluation of this Regulation by the end of 2032, and every six years thereafter (Article 23).

Key definitions (Art 2)

The load-bearing concepts that drive the rest of the Regulation:

Six definitions worth memorising

Digital product passport registry
The information system established and maintained by the Commission under Article 13 of the ESPR, Regulation (EU) 2024/1781 (Art 2(1)).
Unique registration identifier
The identifier automatically generated and communicated once a digital product passport submission passes the registry's automatic verification checks; not proof of compliance with the underlying product rules (Art 8(8), (10); recital 16; ESPR Art 13(5)).
Verified economic operator
An economic operator that has successfully completed the identity-verification process set out in Article 4.
Verified value chain actor
A value chain actor, such as a repairer, refurbisher, remanufacturer or recycler, that has successfully completed the identity-verification process set out in Article 5.
Registration pathway
The two routes into the registry for registering a digital product passport: the secure user interface, a website, or the API (Art 3(a)-(b), Art 8(6)).
Designated national administrator
The single official contact point each Member State must appoint, by 18 February 2027, to manage and oversee that country's registry access rights (Art 7).

Before and after the registry

A fragmented starting point versus the single, shared registry Regulation (EU) 2026/1778 now governs

Feature Before this Regulation Under Regulation (EU) 2026/1778
Central registry No shared registry existed; each product law would need its own registration solution One EU-wide registry under Article 13 ESPR, with this Regulation as its operational rulebook (Art 1(1))
Product laws covered Not applicable, no shared coverage existed ESPR products, batteries, construction products, toys, detergents and end-user surfactants, and any future product law directed to the same registry (Art 1(1))
Identity verification No harmonised registry-level identity check eIDAS-grade electronic signature, seal or attestation of attributes required before any registration, valid up to 3 years (Arts 4-6a)
National access No single national contact point for registry access One designated national administrator per Member State, appointed by 18 February 2027 (Art 7)
Registration pathway Not applicable Two equivalent routes, a secure user interface or an API, with the same automatic verification either way (Art 8(6))
Proof of compliance Not applicable A unique registration identifier is issued, but it is explicitly not proof of compliance with the underlying product law (Art 8(8), recital 16)
Data retention Varied by product law, with no shared default 10 years by default where no other Union law sets a different duration (Art 10(3))
Logging and security No shared registry-level logging regime Categorised log system with graduated retention, from 6 months to the duration of registration (Art 14)

One shared backbone, not one more sectoral silo

The most important shift is structural, not substantive: instead of every product law inventing its own registration system, the ESPR's Article 13 registry, now operationalised by this Regulation, gives batteries, construction products, toys and detergents a single shared identity, registration and logging framework, while leaving each sectoral law free to define its own passport content and compliance rules.

The two routes into the registry for registering a digital product passport: the secure user interface, a website, or the API (Art 3(a)-(b), Art 8(6)).

The Commission-maintained collection of data models and semantic definitions that gives digital product passport data a common structure, versioning and cross-lingual interpretation across all users (Art 2(8), Art 12).

Economic operator
