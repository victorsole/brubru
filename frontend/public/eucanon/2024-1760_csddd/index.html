EU Canon / Sustainability Reporting & Due Diligence

Making large companies act, not just report: the CSDDD

Directive (EU) 2024/1760, the Corporate Sustainability Due Diligence Directive, requires large companies operating in the Union to identify, prevent, mitigate, remedy and publicly account for adverse human rights and environmental impacts across their own operations, their subsidiaries and their chain of activities, and to adopt a climate transition plan compatible with 1.5°C, backed by penalties of up to 5% of worldwide turnover and a harmonised civil-liability regime.

Overview

What the Directive does, why it exists, and how it is structured

The EU's mandatory horizontal due diligence law

Directive (EU) 2024/1760 of 13 June 2024, the CSDDD, requires large companies operating in the Union to identify, prevent, mitigate, remedy and publicly account for adverse human rights and environmental impacts arising from their own operations, the operations of their subsidiaries, and the operations of their business partners in the "chain of activities", upstream plus a limited set of downstream activities. It also obliges those companies to adopt and put into effect a climate transition plan compatible with the 1.5°C target of the Paris Agreement and the 2050 climate-neutrality objective of the European Climate Law.

The Directive has 39 Articles and one Annex, spread across 5 chapters, and runs to 54 pages of the Official Journal (OJ L, 2024/1760, 5.7.2024).

Do, not just report

Whereas CSRD, Directive (EU) 2022/2464, forces companies to report sustainability information, CSDDD forces them to do due diligence, act on findings, and compensate victims when they do not. Together with CSRD, the SFDR (Regulation (EU) 2019/2088) and the Taxonomy Regulation (Regulation (EU) 2020/852), CSDDD closes the EU's sustainable-finance and corporate-conduct framework.

A Directive, not a Regulation

The CSDDD does not apply directly. Each Member State must transpose it into national law by the postponed deadline of 26 July 2027. Substantive due diligence obligations then apply company by company, wave by wave, from 26 July 2028 at the earliest.

Procedure and signature

The Directive was negotiated under the ordinary legislative procedure, file 2022/0051(COD), on legal basis Article 50(1), Article 50(2)(g) and Article 114 TFEU. The Commission proposed it on 23 February 2022; after contested trilogues the European Parliament adopted its position on 24 April 2024 and the Council formally adopted the text on 24 May 2024. It was signed at Brussels on 13 June 2024 by Roberta Metsola for the European Parliament and Mathieu Michel for the Council, published in OJ L, 2024/1760 on 5 July 2024, and entered into force on 25 July 2024, the twentieth day after publication.

Contested from the start

CSDDD is heavily contested. The originally adopted 2024 text was already narrower than the 2022 Commission proposal, COM(2022) 71 final. Stop-the-clock, Directive (EU) 2025/794, postponed transposition and Wave 1 application by one year in April 2025, and a separate substantive Omnibus I proposal tabled on 26 February 2025 (COM(2025) 81 and COM(2025) 82) proposes to narrow due diligence to own operations and direct suppliers by default, soften Article 22 enforcement, and delete the Article 29 harmonised civil-liability regime. That substantive Omnibus is still under co-legislator negotiation in mid-2026, and must not be confused with the Stop-the-clock, which is already law.

Part of a wider sustainable-finance and due diligence framework

CSDDD is the operational-conduct sibling of CSRD, Directive (EU) 2022/2464, which is already a Brubru canon entry. Where CSRD requires disclosure of due diligence processes and a transition plan, CSDDD requires the due diligence and the transition plan themselves, backed by penalties and civil liability. A CSRD-compliant transition plan is deemed to satisfy CSDDD Article 22.

Core requirements, topic by topic

Scope, the 8-step due diligence process, the chain of activities, the transition plan, penalties, and civil liability

Scope (Article 2)

CSDDD applies to EU companies with more than 1,000 employees on average and net worldwide turnover above €450 million in the last financial year; to the ultimate parent of a group meeting that test on a consolidated basis; and to franchising or licensing arrangements with royalties above €22.5 million in the Union combined with turnover above €80 million.

Third-country companies fall in scope on an equivalent, extraterritorial basis: net turnover above €450 million in the Union in the financial year preceding the last, or the ultimate parent of a group meeting that test, or the same franchising threshold measured on Union turnover.

Thresholds must be met for two consecutive financial years to trigger scope, and scope drops after two consecutive years below threshold. Pure holding companies can be exempted where a designated Union subsidiary carries out Articles 6 to 16 and Article 22 on their behalf. Alternative investment funds and UCITS are excluded.

The 8-step due diligence process (Article 5)

1. Integrate
Build due diligence into company policies and risk-management systems (Article 7).
2. Identify and assess
Identify and assess actual and potential adverse human rights and environmental impacts (Article 8).
3. Prioritise
Where full simultaneous coverage is not feasible, prioritise actual and potential adverse impacts by severity and likelihood (Article 9).
4. Prevent, mitigate, and bring to an end
Prevent and mitigate potential adverse impacts (Article 10), and bring actual adverse impacts to an end or minimise their extent (Article 11).
5. Remediate
Provide remediation for actual adverse impacts the company caused or contributed to (Article 12).
6. Engage
Carry out meaningful engagement with affected stakeholders at relevant stages of the process (Article 13).
7. Notify and hear complaints
Operate a notification mechanism and complaints procedure open to affected persons (Article 14).
8. Monitor and communicate
Monitor the effectiveness of due diligence measures and publicly communicate on due diligence at least once a year (Articles 15 and 16).

Companies must retain documentation proving compliance for at least 5 years.

Chain of activities (Article 3(g))

CSDDD does not use "value chain" the way CSRD does. Instead it defines a narrower "chain of activities": upstream activities covering design, extraction, sourcing, manufacture, transport, storage and supply of raw materials, products and parts; and downstream activities covering distribution, transport and storage of the company's own product, but only where a business partner carries out those activities for the company or on its behalf.

Distribution, transport and storage of products subject to Regulation (EU) 2021/821 dual-use export controls, weapons, munitions or war materials are excluded once export has been authorised. Financial-services distribution and lending are also excluded from downstream due diligence. This downstream restriction is narrower than the 2022 Commission proposal and was one of the most contested points during trilogues.

Climate transition plan (Article 22)

Every in-scope company must adopt and put into effect a transition plan for climate change mitigation compatible with 1.5°C and 2050 climate neutrality. The plan must contain time-bound targets for 2030 and in 5-year steps to 2050, including absolute greenhouse-gas reduction for scope 1, scope 2 and, where relevant, scope 3 in each significant category; the decarbonisation levers and key actions; investment and funding quantification; and the role of the administrative, management and supervisory bodies. The plan is updated every 12 months.

CSRD deemer clause: a company that reports a transition plan under CSRD Article 19a, 29a or 40a of Directive 2013/34/EU is deemed to have complied with CSDDD Article 22(1).

Penalties (Article 27)

Member States must set effective, proportionate and dissuasive penalties. The pecuniary penalty cap must not be less than 5% of net worldwide turnover of the last financial year, consolidated turnover for group-parent cases. Where a pecuniary penalty is not paid on time, supervisory authorities must issue a public statement naming the non-compliant company. Penalty decisions are published, kept available for at least 5 years, and sent to the European Network of Supervisory Authorities established under Article 28.

Civil liability (Article 29)

Member States must ensure a company can be held liable for damage where it intentionally or negligently failed to comply with its Article 10 or Article 11 obligations aimed at protecting the person, and that failure caused damage to the person's legally protected interests.

  • A company cannot be held liable for damage caused only by a business partner, a concurrent-cause carve-out
  • Full compensation only, no punitive, multiple or over-compensation damages
  • Limitation period at least 5 years, not starting until the infringement ceases and the claimant reasonably knows of the harm and the infringer
  • Costs must not be prohibitively expensive, and injunctive relief and evidence-disclosure orders are available
  • Trade unions, NGOs and national human rights institutions may bring representative actions on the injured party's authorisation
  • Overriding mandatory application, the regime applies even where the law otherwise applicable is not that of a Member State, a Rome II carve-out

Article 29 civil liability is the single most politically contested provision in the Directive. The substantive Omnibus I proposal would replace it with a discretionary Member State option, rather than a harmonised Union regime.

Do not conflate the two Omnibus tracks

The Stop-the-clock Directive (EU) 2025/794, adopted 14 April 2025, only postpones the transposition deadline and the Wave 1 application date shown in this page. It is already law.

A separate, still-contested substantive Omnibus proposal, COM(2025) 81 and COM(2025) 82 of 26 February 2025, would narrow due diligence by default to own operations and direct, Tier 1, suppliers, delete the harmonised Article 29 civil-liability regime, soften Article 22 enforcement, and extend the transition-plan review period beyond 12 months. That proposal remains under co-legislator negotiation in mid-2026.

Roughly 6,000 companies in scope, before any Omnibus narrowing

The Commission's 2022 Impact Assessment estimated approximately 6,000 EU companies and around 900 non-EU companies in scope at full roll-out. That estimate may fall further if the substantive Omnibus I proposal narrows the due diligence obligation and, indirectly, the population effectively subject to enforcement.

Related EU acts referenced throughout this Directive

CSDDD does not stand alone. Beyond the instruments it directly amends, the operative text reaches into a wider set of EU law:

  • Directive (EU) 2022/2464, CSRD, is the sibling reporting mandate; a CSRD-compliant transition plan is deemed to satisfy CSDDD Article 22
  • Directive 2013/34/EU, the Accounting Directive, hosts the CSRD transition-plan reporting Articles 19a, 29a and 40a that the CSDDD deemer clause refers to
  • Regulation (EU) 2021/1119, the European Climate Law, anchors the 2050 climate-neutrality benchmark the Article 22 transition plan is measured against
  • Directive (EU) 2019/1937, the Whistleblower Protection Directive, is amended by CSDDD Article 32
  • Regulation (EU) 2023/2859, the European Single Access Point, is amended by CSDDD Article 33
  • Regulation (EU) 2021/821, dual-use export controls, is referenced in the Article 3(g) chain-of-activities carve-out

How the Member States are transposing it

A deadline postponed to 26 July 2027, and not yet reached by any Member State

CSDDD is not directly applicable: each Member State must write it into national law. The original deadline of 26 July 2026 was postponed by one year, to 26 July 2027, by Directive (EU) 2025/794, the Stop-the-clock, adopted 14 April 2025. As of mid-2026 no Member State has yet notified a national transposing measure, because the postponed deadline still lies more than a year ahead. Glossary

Load-bearing terms as used in Directive (EU) 2024/1760

CSDDD
Corporate Sustainability Due Diligence Directive, Directive (EU) 2024/1760: the EU's mandatory horizontal human rights and environmental due diligence law for large companies.
CS3D
The Directive's alternative shorthand, used interchangeably with CSDDD in EU institutional, press and NGO commentary.
Adverse impact
An actual or potential negative effect on the protected human rights or environmental interests listed in Annex I, arising from a company's own operations, its subsidiaries, or its chain of activities.
Chain of activities
The Article 3(g) concept covering upstream activities such as design, sourcing, manufacture and transport, plus limited downstream activities a business partner carries out for or on behalf of the company.
Business partner
An entity with which the company has a commercial relationship connected to its operations, products or services, whether direct or indirect.
Ultimate parent company
The company at the top of a group that controls, directly or indirectly, one or more subsidiaries, and whose Article 2 thresholds are assessed on a consolidated basis.
Stakeholder
Persons and groups whose rights or interests are or could be affected by a company's products, services and operations, including workers, trade unions, communities and their legitimate representatives, engaged under Article 13.
Remediation
Restoring an affected person, or the affected environment, to a situation equivalent to or as close as possible to the one they would be in had the adverse impact not occurred, under Article 12.
Meaningful engagement
The Article 13 requirement to consult affected stakeholders in good faith at relevant stages of due diligence, exchanging information and taking their views into account.
Notification mechanism
The Article 14 channel through which affected persons, trade unions and civil society organisations can raise concerns about actual or potential adverse impacts.
Appropriate measures
The proportionate, effective actions a company must take at each due diligence step, calibrated to the severity and likelihood of the impact and the company's own leverage and resources.
Severity of adverse impact
A factor used to prioritise which adverse impacts to address first under Article 9, assessed by scale, scope and irremediable character.
Transition plan
The Article 22 climate change mitigation plan every in-scope company must adopt and put into effect, with time-bound 2030 and 5-year-step targets to 2050, compatible with 1.5°C.
Civil liability
The Article 29 regime under which a company can be held liable for damage caused by its intentional or negligent failure to comply with Articles 10 or 11.
Overriding mandatory application
The Article 29(7) rule ensuring civil-liability claims apply even where the law otherwise applicable, under Rome II, is not that of a Member State.
Omnibus I
The Commission's February 2025 package proposing to narrow CSDDD's due diligence scope, delete the harmonised civil-liability regime and soften Article 22 enforcement, distinct from the already-adopted Stop-the-clock Directive.
