EU's anti-subsidy duties on stainless steel from India and Indonesia

Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) 2022/433 imposes definitive countervailing duties on stainless steel cold-rolled flat products from India and Indonesia, finding that Chromeni received a 7.56% subsidy margin and IRNC a 21.4% margin via Indonesia's nickel-ore export ban and Chinese state financing at the Morowali Industrial Park. It is the second EU case to apply the cross-border subsidy attribution doctrine established in the glass fibre fabrics case.

What this regulation does

Plain-English summary of the operative effects

Imposing definitive countervailing duties

Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) 2022/433, adopted on 15 March 2022 and published in OJ L 88 on the same date, imposes definitive countervailing duties on imports of stainless steel cold-rolled flat products (SSCR) originating in India and Indonesia. The investigation was initiated on 17 February 2021 following a complaint lodged on 4 January 2021 by EUROFER (European Steel Association) on behalf of the Union industry. The legal basis is Regulation (EU) 2016/1037, specifically Articles 15 and 24(1).

Countervailing duties (CVDs) offset subsidies granted by the government of an exporting country to its producers, restoring a level playing field for Union industry. The duties are expressed as ad valorem percentages of the net, free-at-Union-frontier price before duty, and apply in addition to any applicable customs tariff and, in this case, the parallel anti-dumping duties imposed by Implementing Regulation (EU) 2021/2012.

Applying the cross-border subsidy attribution doctrine to Indonesia

The most analytically significant element of this regulation is its treatment of Indonesian SSCR producers. The principal Indonesian exporter, the IRNC Group, operates at the Morowali Industrial Park (IMIP) in Sulawesi under a framework of Sino-Indonesian bilateral agreements dating back to 2005. Chinese state financing was channelled to IRNC Group via Chinese policy and state-owned banks and the China-ASEAN Investment Cooperation Fund.

Applying the doctrine first established in Regulation (EU) 2020/776 (the glass fibre fabrics case, recitals 16 and 941 of this regulation), the Commission found that this support was acknowledged and adopted by the Government of Indonesia (GOID) and therefore attributed it to the GOID as a countervailable subsidy. This is the second application of the cross-border attribution doctrine in EU trade defence practice.

Adjusting the parallel anti-dumping duties

Article 2 of this regulation amends Implementing Regulation (EU) 2021/2012 (the parallel definitive anti-dumping regulation on SSCR from India and Indonesia) to ensure no double-counting of export subsidies, as required under Article 24(1) of the Basic Anti-Subsidy Regulation. The Commission first imposed the full CVD, then set the remaining AD duty equal to the dumping margin minus the export-subsidy amount, capped at the injury margin. The two regulations operate as a coordinated pair.

Safeguard interaction and entry into force

Article 3 addresses the interaction with the EU's steel safeguard measures (Regulation (EU) 2019/159, prolonged to 30 June 2024). To prevent over-stacking, once the above-quota safeguard tariff applies to SSCR, only the higher of the safeguard duty or the combined AD plus CVD is collected; collection of the remainder is suspended for the safeguard period. Article 4 provides that the regulation enters into force on the day following its publication in the Official Journal.

Product scope

CN codes, product definition, and downstream use (recital 65, Article 1(1))

CN codes (19 codes across headings 7219 and 7220)

The product concerned is flat-rolled products of stainless steel, not further worked than cold-rolled (cold-reduced): SSCR. Classification codes given for information only in Article 1(1):

  • 7219 31 00, 7219 32 10, 7219 32 90, 7219 33 10, 7219 33 90, 7219 34 10, 7219 34 90, 7219 35 10, 7219 35 90, 7219 90 20, 7219 90 80
  • 7220 20 21, 7220 20 29, 7220 20 41, 7220 20 49, 7220 20 81, 7220 20 89, 7220 90 20, 7220 90 80
Mid-stream industrial input
SSCR is not a finished product. It feeds white goods (dishwashers, washing machines), automotive components, food-processing and chemical-industry equipment, kitchenware, and construction products.
Cold-rolled only
The definition excludes hot-rolled stainless steel (separately investigated) and further-processed products (e.g. cut-to-length sheets, precision strip). The "cold-rolled" qualifier is the key product-control boundary.
Like product
SSCR manufactured by the Union industry has the same basic physical and chemical characteristics as the imported product. The investigation used Product Control Numbers (PCNs) for like-for-like injury and undercutting comparisons.

Companies investigated

Indian and Indonesian exporter groups, Union producers, and the role of the Government of China

India: two groups (no sampling needed)

Two groups accounted for 100% of Indian SSCR exports to the EU, so formal sampling was abandoned (recitals 31-34):

  • Chromeni Steels Private Limited (Ahmedabad): not vertically integrated; starts from hot-rolled coils.
  • Jindal Group: vertically integrated from coke, gas, and ferrochromium through to SSCR; comprises Jindal Stainless Limited, Jindal Stainless Hisar Limited, and related traders.
Indonesia: sample of two groups (71% of exports)

A sample of two groups representing 71% of Indonesian SSCR exports to the EU was selected (recitals 35-37):

  • IRNC Group: PT. Indonesia Ruipu Nickel and Chrome Alloy and four related input suppliers (GCNS, ITSS, SMI, TSI), all located in the Morowali Industrial Park, part of the Tsingshan-linked complex; vertically integrated from nickel ore.
  • PT. Jindal Stainless Indonesia: not vertically integrated; starts from stainless steel coils.
Government of China: interested party

The Government of China (GOC) registered as an interested party (recitals 10-19) because part of the alleged Indonesian subsidies originated from Chinese state financing channelled through the Sino-Indonesian bilateral cooperation framework at Morowali. This is directly analogous to its role in the GFF case regarding the SETC-Zone.

Union industry: 13 known producers

The Union industry comprised 13 known producers with total production of approximately 3.1 million tonnes during the IP (recitals 945-947). Three were sampled (representing over 60% of production): Acciai Speciali Terni S.p.A. (Italy), Aperam Stainless Europe, and Outokumpu Stainless OY (recital 26).

Subsidies countervailed: India

Four export-linked schemes plus chromium ore at below-market prices (Section 3)

Advanced Authorisation Scheme (AAS)
Allows duty-free import of inputs (including raw materials, semi-finished goods, and consumables) that are physically incorporated in the exported product. Export-contingent: eligibility tied to export obligations.
Duty Drawback Scheme (DDS)
Refund of customs duties and central excise duties paid on inputs used in exported products. Export-contingent: duty credit certificates are generated at the time of export.
Export Promotion of Capital Goods Scheme (EPCGS)
Duty concession on imports of capital goods (plant, machinery, equipment) conditional on export obligations. Benefit flows over the life of the capital goods. Found countervailable for both Chromeni and the Jindal Group.
Merchandise Export from India Scheme (MEIS)
Transferable duty credit scrips (certificates) awarded as a percentage of FOB export value. Scrips can be used to pay customs duty or sold to third parties. Export-contingent; found countervailable for both sampled groups.
Chromium ore at LTAR (Jindal Group only)
India holds significant chromium ore reserves. The Commission found that the Jindal Group (vertically integrated from ferrochromium) benefited from chromium ore provided at less than adequate remuneration. Not found for Chromeni (not vertically integrated).

No lesser duty: full subsidy rate

Regulation (EU) 2018/825 removed the obligation to apply the lesser duty rule in anti-subsidy proceedings involving raw-material distortions. Accordingly, CVDs are set at the full level of countervailable subsidisation without reference to an injury margin cap (recitals 1057-1059). The result for India: Chromeni and all-other Indian producers face a 7.5% CVD, Jindal Group 4.3%.

Subsidies countervailed: Indonesia

Nickel-ore LTAR, the Morowali cross-border doctrine, and other schemes (Section 4)

Indonesia's nickel-ore strategy: the background

Indonesia held an estimated 21 million tonnes of nickel reserves, approximately 20% of the world's known nickel ore, as of 2020 (recital 307). Through the 2009 Mining Law (Law No. 4 of 2009), Article 103, the GOID imposed a domestic processing requirement: ore had to be processed or refined in Indonesia (recital 317). This was reinforced by an export ban imposed on 11 January 2014, relaxed partially in 2017 (10% export duty on ore below 1.7% nickel content), and then made complete again in 2020 (recitals 319-320).

The combined effect kept the domestic nickel-ore price significantly below international prices, giving Indonesian stainless-steel producers a captive cheap raw material. The Commission found that the lower domestic nickel-ore price was an intended objective, not an inadvertent by-product, of the GOID's interventions (recital 452). The subsidy amount for nickel ore LTAR, IRNC Group: 9.64% (recital 542). This is the single largest scheme in the case.

The Morowali cross-border doctrine

Production was concentrated in the Morowali Industrial Park (IMIP) in Sulawesi, jointly developed by Indonesia and China. The managing company, PT. Indonesia Morowali Industrial Park (IMIP), a Sino-Indonesian entity, began operations in October 2013 (recitals 319-324).

A chain of bilateral agreements framed the cooperation (recital 548):

  • Joint Declaration on Strategic Partnership, Jakarta, 25 April 2005
  • Plan of Action, 21 January 2010
  • Agreement on Indonesia-China Integrated Industrial Parks, 2 October 2013
  • Subsequent agreements continuing through 2018

Preferential financing reached the IRNC Group from Chinese policy and state-owned banks and from the China-ASEAN Investment Cooperation Fund (CAF) under China's "going out" and Belt and Road policies (Section 4.6). Because this support was provided within a framework the GOID solicited and adopted, the Commission attributed it to the GOID and countervailed it, citing GFF (Reg 2020/776) recitals 16 and 941 of this regulation.

Coal and land at LTAR
Provision of coal for less than adequate remuneration (Section 4.8) and provision of land within the Morowali Industrial Park for less than adequate remuneration, both found countervailable for the IRNC Group.
Tax holidays and exemptions
Income tax holiday facility and income tax allowance facility, found countervailable. Import duty exemptions for machinery (bonded zone). VAT exemption for machinery and PBB land-and-building tax allegation were NOT found countervailable (recitals 932-933).
Chinese state financing
Preferential financing from Chinese policy banks, state-owned commercial banks, and the China-ASEAN Investment Cooperation Fund (CAF), attributed to the GOID under the Morowali bilateral framework. Second application of the cross-border doctrine.

Combined CVD + anti-dumping duty

How the two instruments interact

This regulation operates alongside Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) 2021/2012, which imposed definitive anti-dumping duties on the same product from the same countries. Under Article 24(1) of the Basic Anti-Subsidy Regulation, no product may be subject to both anti-dumping and countervailing duties to compensate for the same situation of dumping or export subsidisation (recital 1063). The Commission first imposed the full CVD, then set the remaining AD duty equal to the dumping margin minus the export-subsidy amount, capped at the injury elimination margin.

Injury to the Union industry and causation

Key indicators from the period considered (2017 to IP: 1 July 2019 to 30 June 2020)

Market and production decline

Union consumption fell from 3,873,092 tonnes in 2017 to 3,206,766 tonnes in the IP, a decline of 17% (recital 950). Union production fell from 3,708,262 tonnes to 3,111,804 tonnes (down 16%), while capacity utilisation dropped from 84% to 68% (recital 976).

Union sales volume fell 15% (from 2,735,448 to 2,330,537 tonnes), yet the Union market share edged slightly upward from 70.6% to 72.7% (recital 983). This apparent paradox was because the Union industry cut prices to defend volume in a shrinking market.

Financial collapse

The price defence strategy destroyed margins. Profitability fell from 7.6% in 2017 to 0.4% in the IP (recital 1000). Cash flow fell by 52% over the period (recital 1002). Return on investments fell by 80%, and investments themselves fell by 13% (recital 1003). The Commission concluded material injury within the meaning of Article 8(5) of the Basic Regulation (recital 1009).

Price undercutting margins (recital 968)

  • India (Chromeni): 13.4% undercutting margin
  • India (Jindal Group): 5.8% undercutting margin
  • Indonesia (IRNC): 12.4% undercutting margin

Import prices from both countries were 5% to 19% below Union prices throughout the period considered (recital 1013). The joint import market share of India and Indonesia nearly doubled: from approximately 3.0-3.1% to approximately 5.5-5.8% of Union consumption, as subsidised import volume rose approximately 55% (recital 964).

Causal link

A strong causal link was found between the subsidised imports and material injury (recital 1015). The Commission examined COVID-19 and the general consumption decline as other potential causes but found they did not break the causal link: prices were consistently undercut before the pandemic (recitals 1016-1018). Jindal Indonesia's non-subsidised imports (de minimis margin, below 0.65% market share) did not attenuate the link (recitals 1019-1022).

Union interest

Under Section 7 of the Basic Regulation, the Commission assessed whether imposing measures was in the broader Union interest. No compelling reason against measures was found (recital 1055). Imports from both countries were assessed cumulatively under Article 8(3): India's IP market share was 3.4%, Indonesia's was 2.8% (recital 955), both above de minimis, both showing significant undercutting.

Safeguard interaction

Article 3: preventing over-stacking with the EU steel safeguard

The anti-stacking rule (Article 3)

SSCR is also covered by the EU's steel safeguard measures (Regulation (EU) 2019/159, prolonged to 30 June 2024). If the above-quota safeguard tariff activates for a particular SSCR category, it would in principle be stacked on top of both the CVD and the AD duty, producing a potentially excessive combined rate.

Article 3 prevents this by providing that, during any period in which the above-quota safeguard is in force, only the higher of (a) the safeguard duty or (b) the combined AD plus CVD is collected. The remainder is suspended for the duration of the safeguard period (recitals 1079-1080). This is a standard design feature when two trade-defence instruments overlap on the same product.

Key definitions and concepts

The legal and analytical building blocks of this case

Countervailing duty (CVD)

An import duty applied at the EU's external border to offset a specific subsidy granted by the government of the exporting country to its producers. The Basic Regulation (Regulation (EU) 2016/1037) defines a subsidy as a financial contribution by a government that confers a benefit on the recipient and is specific to certain enterprises or industries. CVDs are expressed as ad valorem percentages of the customs value.

Less than adequate remuneration (LTAR)

A subsidy form where a government provides goods or services (raw materials, energy, land) to producers at prices below what would prevail in a free market. The "adequate remuneration" benchmark is usually the price in an undistorted third-country market or an internationally recognised benchmark price. In this case: nickel-ore LTAR (Indonesia) and chromium-ore LTAR (Jindal Group, India).

De minimis threshold

Under Article 14(5) of the Basic Anti-Subsidy Regulation, a subsidy margin is considered de minimis if it is below 1% ad valorem. No CVD can be imposed where the subsidy margin is de minimis. PT. Jindal Stainless Indonesia's margin of 0.02% is the operative example here. The investigation is not terminated by a de minimis finding; the duty is simply set at zero for that specific company.

Cross-border subsidy attribution

The doctrine, first applied in the GFF case (Reg 2020/776) and applied again here, that financial support originating from one government (China) but channelled to producers in another country (Indonesia) via bilateral agreements and framework arrangements can be attributed to the host government and countervailed. The legal basis is the acknowledgement-and-adoption principle, analogous to Article 11 of the ILC Articles on State Responsibility (recitals 16, 941).

Related canon entries

Other EU Canon deep-dives in the same legal family: anti_subsidy_trade_remedies

Origin case -- anti_subsidy_trade_remedies
Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) 2020/776: Definitive Countervailing Duties on Glass Fibre Fabrics from China and Egypt

The GFF regulation is the case that established the cross-border subsidy attribution doctrine in EU trade defence practice. Chinese state financing was attributed to Egyptian producers in the China-Egypt Suez Economic and Trade Cooperation Zone (SETC-Zone) under the acknowledgement-and-adoption principle drawn from ILC Article 11. Regulation (EU) 2022/433 (this case) explicitly relies on Reg 2020/776 at recitals 16 and 941 when applying the same doctrine to Chinese financing at Indonesia's Morowali Industrial Park. Reading both cases together shows the doctrine's precise architecture and its extension from the SETC-Zone to the Morowali framework.

Successor case -- anti_subsidy_trade_remedies
Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) 2024/2754: Definitive Countervailing Duties on Chinese Battery Electric Vehicles

The BEV regulation is the 2024 flagship application of the same Basic Anti-Subsidy Regulation framework (Regulation (EU) 2016/1037) to a far higher-profile sector. Both the GFF case (2020), the SSCR case (2022), and the BEV case (2024) share the same legal architecture: complaint by Union industry, Commission investigation, subsidy findings, injury analysis, Union interest test, definitive duty table, and no lesser duty rule. The BEV case extended the no lesser duty principle and the cross-border doctrine to the electric-vehicle sector and involved rejected undertaking offers from major Chinese producers including BYD and SAIC.

Successor case -- anti_subsidy_trade_remedies
Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) 2025/500: Countervailing Duties on Aluminium Road Wheels from Morocco

Countervailing duties on Moroccan aluminium wheels; the cross-border subsidy doctrine applied to Chinese Belt-and-Road financing. The 2025 regulation extends the attribution framework used in the Morowali case (Reg 2022/433) to the automotive components sector, applying the same Basic Anti-Subsidy Regulation (Regulation (EU) 2016/1037) to Chinese state financing channelled to producers in Morocco.
