The La Rochelle Airport State Aid Decision

Commission Decision (EU) 2023/1683 examined a decade of public funding to a small French regional airport and its marketing deals with Ryanair, AMS and Jet2. The verdict: compatible aid to the airport, incompatible aid to the airlines, recovery ordered.

What happened

a decade of public funding at a small French Atlantic coast airport, examined under EU State aid rules

La Rochelle and its airport

La Rochelle is a coastal city in the Charente-Maritime department of western France, known primarily as a tourist destination and sailing hub. Its airport, Aéroport de La Rochelle–Île de Ré (IATA code LRH), sits a few kilometres east of the city centre and throughout the 2000s handled between 250,000 and 600,000 passengers a year, placing it firmly in the European Commission's category D: a small regional airport with under one million passengers per year.

The airport was operated and owned by the Chambre de Commerce et d'Industrie de La Rochelle (CCI), a public-law body established by statute under Article L 710-1 of the French Commercial Code. CCIs across France occupy an unusual dual role: they are public institutions created by the State, supervised by the French Ministry of the Economy, financed in part through a parafiscal apprenticeship levy, and yet also run commercial activities including airport operation, port management and trade centres.

That dual nature became central to the Commission's assessment: by being a public-law body, the CCI's decisions were imputable to the French State, and its financial resources counted as State resources for the purposes of Article 107(1) of the Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union (TFEU).

How the investigation started

From 2001 the CCI received investment grants and repayable advances from French central and local government to fund airport infrastructure works and to cover operating losses. From 2003 it also concluded a series of commercial agreements with low-cost airlines: principally Ryanair and Ryanair's wholly-owned marketing subsidiary Airport Marketing Services (AMS), as well as Jet2.com in 2008.

A competitor airline lodged a complaint with the Commission in 2012. Under the State aid procedural rules in force at the time, the Commission opened a formal investigation under Article 108(2) TFEU, notifying France and the beneficiaries and inviting comments. The case number assigned was SA.26494 (2012/C, ex 2012/NN). The "NN" designation means the measures had not been pre-notified; the Commission had learned of them through the complaint.

The investigation ran for nearly a decade. It involved extensive exchanges of information with France, the CCI, Ryanair, AMS and Jet2, economic expert reports, and the application of the Commission's evolving guidelines on airport and airline State aid. The final Decision was signed by Commissioner Margrethe Vestager on 26 July 2022 and published in the Official Journal on 7 September 2023.

Why this case matters

The La Rochelle case is one of a significant series of Commission decisions applying the 2014 Aviation Guidelines to low-cost carrier incentive programmes at European regional airports. Similar decisions were issued for airports including Nimes, Angouleme, Montpellier, Pau, Carcassonne, Limoges and Rennes in France, and Frankfurt-Hahn and Kassel-Calden in Germany, among others.

What makes La Rochelle particularly instructive is the depth and clarity of the Commission's incremental profitability analysis: the decision runs to over 500 recitals, contains detailed NPV tables tested against multiple scenarios, and sets out precisely why the single-till approach was preferred, how Ryanair and AMS were treated as one entity, and why none of the airline payments could be saved by compatibility arguments. It is a near-complete manual for how the Commission assesses airport-airline State aid.

The airport and airline deals

two distinct sets of measures: public funding to the airport and commercial agreements with airlines

Public funding to the airport (2001-2012)

Between 2001 and 2012 a series of French public authorities provided the CCI with financial support to cover both capital investment and operating losses at La Rochelle airport:

  • Investment grants (2001-2005): Approximately EUR 2.986 million in capital grants, co-financed by the French State and the local authorities (the département of Charente-Maritime, the Communauté d'Agglomération de La Rochelle, and the Région Poitou-Charentes), to fund physical infrastructure works including runway resurfacing, terminal works and safety equipment.
  • Repayable advances (2001-2012): Approximately EUR 7.06 million in repayable advances advanced to cover operating losses. These were structured as advances that would in principle be repaid if the airport returned to profitability, but given the airport's structural loss-making position this was largely theoretical.
  • Underinvoicing of shared services (2001-2005): The CCI's general administrative arm provided management, HR and accounting services to the airport branch but did not fully invoice their cost. For the period 2001-2005, the allocation key used understated the cost charged to the airport, conferring an indirect advantage.
  • Local authority promotional subsidies: The département of Charente-Maritime and other local bodies paid subsidies to the CCI to cover its participation in regional tourism promotion campaigns.
The Ryanair and AMS marketing agreements (2003-2011)

From 2003 the CCI concluded a series of commercial agreements with two distinct but closely linked Ryanair entities:

  • Airport services agreements (ASAs) with Ryanair: setting out the commercial terms on which Ryanair would operate routes from La Rochelle, including the charges payable (or waivers granted) for landing, handling and passenger processing.
  • Marketing services agreements (MSAs) with Airport Marketing Services (AMS): setting out payments from the CCI to AMS in exchange for promotional services delivered via the Ryanair.com website, including featured placement, banner advertising and visibility to Ryanair's customer base.

AMS is a wholly-owned subsidiary of Ryanair. Its only client for airport marketing services was airports seeking visibility on Ryanair.com. The Commission found that Ryanair and AMS always contracted with airports together or in immediate sequence, that AMS's marketing services were inseparable from Ryanair's route decisions, and that the two entities had to be treated as a single economic unit for State aid assessment purposes (recitals 432-438).

The practical consequence: the CCI could not argue that it was merely purchasing advertising services from AMS at arms' length while separately operating a routine airport services agreement with Ryanair. Both contracts were part of a single arrangement by which the CCI paid for Ryanair's presence at La Rochelle.

The Jet2 marketing agreement (2008)

On 4 July 2008 the CCI concluded a marketing services agreement with Jet2.com Ltd, the British leisure airline, under broadly similar terms to the Ryanair/AMS arrangements: a lump-sum payment from the CCI to Jet2 in exchange for marketing services and a commitment to operate routes to La Rochelle with specified minimum frequencies. The Commission assessed this agreement separately from the Ryanair/AMS package but applied the same incremental profitability methodology.

What was NOT State aid

Not every measure examined constituted State aid. Article 2 of the Decision lists the measures found to fall outside Article 107(1) TFEU:

  • Subsidies granted by French authorities to reimburse the CCI for carrying out FIATA-mandated safety and security tasks: these were cost-coverage reimbursements for a public-remit obligation, not selective advantages.
  • CCI underinvoicing of shared services for the period 2006-2012: from 2006 the CCI adopted a proper allocation key that correctly apportioned shared costs to the airport branch.
  • The Rochefort airport management contract: the CCI acquired this contract through a competitive tender process, meaning no selective advantage was granted.
  • The Ryanair lighting charge exemption: applied to all aircraft operating daytime flights, not selectively to Ryanair.
  • Charge discounts to Flybe and Aer Arann: applied under a general, non-discriminatory scheme available to all airlines of relevant weight categories, not selectively.
  • Alleged marketing payments to Buzz and easyJet: the Commission found insufficient evidence that these were made.

The Commission's legal test

Article 107(1) TFEU, the Market Economy Operator test, and the incremental profitability analysis

State resources and imputability (recitals 231-242)

The first two elements of Article 107(1) TFEU require that a measure involves State resources and is imputable to the State. The Commission resolved both questions swiftly for all CCI measures.

The CCI is a public-law institution established by statute, supervised by the French Ministry of the Economy, and financed in part through parafiscal levies collected by the State. Its key decisions are subject to administrative oversight. On that basis all CCI funding decisions were imputable to the French State. The financial resources it deployed, including the operating budgets that funded airline marketing payments, were resources of the State.

This finding foreclosed a common defence in airport State aid cases: the argument that a CCI, as a commercially active body, exercises independent commercial judgment not attributable to the State. The Commission rejected this: the CCI's public-law status, regardless of its commercial activities, brings all its decisions within the scope of Article 107(1).

SGEI and the Altmark rejection (recitals 253-256)

France argued that La Rochelle airport performed a service of general economic interest (SGEI) and that the public funding constituted Altmark-compliant compensation falling outside Article 107(1) altogether.

The Commission rejected this argument quickly. The four Altmark conditions must be met cumulatively. The core problem for La Rochelle was the first: the airport does not serve a genuinely isolated or remote area that lacks alternative transport modes. La Rochelle is connected to Paris and other major French cities by both the national railway network (TGV services) and the motorway network. Passengers travelling to La Rochelle have realistic alternatives to flying for many journeys.

The Commission also noted that even if the first Altmark condition could be satisfied, the fourth condition (that compensation must either be set by a competitive tender process or benchmarked against an efficient operator's costs) was clearly not met for the operating advances. No competitive process had been run and no efficiency benchmark analysis was in evidence.

The Market Economy Operator test: choosing the incremental approach (recitals 485-495)

For the airline marketing agreements the decisive question was whether, by making the payments, the CCI acted as a market economy operator (MEO): would a private airport operator in the same position, seeking to maximise profit, have concluded these agreements?

Two analytical methods were available to the Commission:

  • Comparative analysis: benchmark the CCI's terms against what other airports charge (or pay) in comparable agreements with comparable airlines. An agreement is market-conform if its terms match what the market offers.
  • Incremental profitability analysis: model whether, for this specific airport and this specific airline relationship, the incremental revenues the airport expected to earn (from the airline's passengers) exceed the incremental costs it accepted (including the marketing payments). An agreement is market-conform if it generates a positive Net Present Value (NPV).

The Commission preferred the incremental approach. A comparative analysis is of limited value for small category D airports because there is no reliable benchmark market: the terms that one small regional airport negotiates with Ryanair reflect that airport's specific competitive situation and the strength or weakness of its negotiating position, not a genuine market price. The incremental profitability test directly addresses the fundamental question: was this deal rational for this airport?

The incremental profitability calculation and NPV results

For each agreement (or package of simultaneous ASA + MSA agreements) the Commission modelled the incremental revenues the CCI could reasonably have expected to earn over the contract lifetime:

  • Additional aeronautical revenues: landing fees, passenger handling charges, aircraft parking fees.
  • Additional non-aeronautical revenues: retail concession income, food and beverage, car parking, advertising, and other commercial revenues generated by the additional passengers brought by the airline (the single-till approach).

Against those revenues the Commission set the incremental costs the CCI accepted:

  • Additional staffing, ground handling, security and operational costs for the additional flights.
  • The marketing payments themselves, including all amounts paid to AMS under the marketing services agreements.

The resulting cashflow for each contract year was discounted at 6% per annum to calculate the NPV. The Commission computed both a base scenario (realistic revenue assumptions) and an "imprudent" scenario (most favourable possible revenue assumptions for the CCI).

The result, set out in Tables 18 and 19 of the Decision, was unambiguous: every Ryanair/AMS agreement returned a negative NPV in both scenarios. The Jet2 agreement likewise returned a negative NPV. No combination of plausible assumptions could produce a positive result. A rational private airport operator would not have concluded these agreements on these terms.

The Commission emphasised a further factor: the CCI had not prepared any ex ante business plan or profitability projection at the time it signed the agreements (recital 533). A prudent private investor would have tested viability before committing. The absence of any contemporaneous documentation was itself indicative of non-market behaviour.

Why local authority subsidies did not rescue the airline deals

France and the CCI argued that local authority promotional subsidies received by the CCI from Charente-Maritime and other bodies should be taken into account in the NPV calculation: those subsidies reduced the net cost to the CCI of the airline agreements, potentially turning a negative result into a positive one.

The Commission rejected this (recitals 294-302). The local authority subsidies were paid to the CCI to cover its own promotional obligations as airport operator, not as part of the deal with the airlines. They formed part of the airport's general operating budget. Including them in the incremental analysis would have amounted to treating one layer of public support as an offset against another layer of public support, which is not how the MEO test works. The test asks whether the specific agreement with the airline was rational from the airport's perspective, treating the airline payment as a cost.

The decision and recovery

compatible aid to the airport, incompatible aid to the airlines, recovery with compound interest

Compatible aid to the airport (Article 1)

The Commission found that the investment grants and repayable advances to the CCI, the underinvoicing of shared services for 2001-2005, and the local authority promotional subsidies were State aid under Article 107(1) TFEU but were compatible with the internal market under Article 107(3)(c) TFEU, which permits aid to facilitate the development of certain economic activities where it does not adversely affect trading conditions to an extent contrary to the common interest.

Compatibility was assessed under the 2014 Aviation Guidelines for post-2014 measures and under the 2005 Community Guidelines for earlier measures. Investment grants were necessary and proportionate, at an aid intensity of 50.8%. Operating advances covered genuine structural losses at a category D airport with a regional connectivity role. Competitive distortion was limited.

Incompatible aid to the airlines (Articles 3 and 4)

The Ryanair/AMS agreements (Article 3) and the Jet2 agreement (Article 4) were declared incompatible State aid. No compatibility exception applied. The agreements were not start-up aid: the 2014 Aviation Guidelines allow time-limited, degressive start-up grants for genuinely new routes, but the Ryanair arrangements covered routes already in operation for years and were structured as ongoing incentive payments. No other compatibility ground was available.

Recovery order (Article 5)

France was ordered to recover the incompatible aid from the beneficiaries with compound interest under Commission Regulation (EC) No 794/2004. Interest runs from the date each payment was disbursed to the date of actual repayment.

  • Ryanair and AMS: joint and several liability. France may pursue either or both for the full amount of incompatible payments under the combined ASA/MSA packages.
  • Jet2: separate liability for the marketing payments under its 2008 agreement.
  • Compound interest over more than a decade substantially increases the total recovery amount relative to the original principal.
  • Four-month deadline from notification. Failure to comply exposes France to infringement proceedings.

What it means for airports and airlines

practical compliance principles drawn from the Commission's analysis

1
Prepare ex ante business plans for every airline deal
The absence of contemporaneous NPV analysis before signing was treated as evidence of non-market behaviour. A prudent private operator would document why the deal is expected to generate net positive incremental revenues before committing.
2
Use the incremental profitability test, not just benchmarks
Comparing your terms to another airport is necessary but not sufficient. The Commission asks whether this specific relationship generates a positive NPV for this airport. Model all incremental revenues (aeronautical and non-aeronautical) against all incremental costs including the marketing payments.
3
Treat simultaneous airline and marketing contracts as one deal
Where an airline and its marketing subsidiary contract with an airport together or in sequence, the Commission assesses both contracts as a single package. Structuring the arrangement across two legal entities does not disaggregate the State aid analysis.
4
SGEI/Altmark requires genuine isolation
An airport with rail or motorway connections to major cities cannot credibly claim SGEI status. All four Altmark conditions must be met cumulatively. The cost-benchmark condition (competitive tender or independent efficiency analysis) is typically the hardest and cannot be skipped.
5
Ongoing incentive payments are not start-up aid
The 2014 Aviation Guidelines allow start-up aid for genuinely new routes, time-limited and degressive. Multi-year, recurring marketing payments to airlines operating established routes do not qualify as start-up aid, regardless of how they are labelled.
6
Recovery is joint and several between economically linked entities
Ryanair and AMS were ordered to repay jointly and severally. Where an airline and its subsidiary constitute a single economic entity, the full recovery obligation attaches to both. Corporate restructuring after the aid is disbursed does not eliminate the exposure.
7
Account for compound interest over long investigations
Recovery interest is compounded annually from the date of disbursement. Over a decade-plus investigation the interest component can approach or exceed the principal. Airlines and airports in contested cases should provision for this exposure as soon as an investigation opens.
8
Document the regional connectivity rationale for airport support
Compatibility of operating and investment aid requires clear documentation of the airport's regional connectivity role, the absence of less distortive alternatives, and proportionality to the financing gap. Aid intensity for investment must remain within the Guidelines' ceiling.

Glossary

key terms in EU State aid law and aviation economics

State aid
Any advantage granted by a Member State or through State resources, on a selective basis, to certain undertakings, that distorts or threatens to distort competition and affects trade between Member States. All four conditions of Article 107(1) TFEU must be met.
Market Economy Operator (MEO) test
The test applied to determine whether a public body granting an advantage acted as a prudent private operator would have acted in the same circumstances. If yes, there is no advantage and thus no State aid. If no, the full amount of the payment constitutes an advantage.
Incremental profitability analysis
An analytical method for applying the MEO test to airport-airline agreements. It asks whether the incremental revenues an airport expects to earn from an airline's passengers (aeronautical and non-aeronautical) exceed the incremental costs of the relationship, including any marketing payments. A positive NPV indicates the deal is commercially rational.
Net Present Value (NPV)
The present value of a stream of future cashflows, discounted at a rate that reflects the time value of money and risk. In the La Rochelle analysis the Commission used a 6% discount rate. A negative NPV means that, at the time of signing, the agreement was expected to destroy value for the airport.
Single-till accounting
An airport accounting approach in which all revenues generated at an airport (aeronautical fees and non-aeronautical commercial revenues) are pooled before calculating profitability. Under a single till, non-aeronautical revenues attributable to an airline's passengers count toward that airline's contribution to the airport's bottom line.
Airport category D
Under the 2014 Aviation Guidelines, a small regional airport handling under one million passengers per year. Category D airports may receive operating aid under Article 107(3)(c) TFEU, subject to conditions. La Rochelle fell within this category throughout the investigation period.
Single economic entity
A group of legally distinct companies that function as one economic unit for competition law purposes. Where a parent and its wholly-owned subsidiary always act together, the Commission may treat them as one entity for State aid assessment and recovery purposes. Ryanair and AMS were so treated.
Altmark conditions
Four cumulative conditions set out by the Court of Justice in Altmark Trans (C-280/00): (1) the undertaking has a genuine SGEI obligation; (2) compensation parameters are established in advance; (3) compensation does not exceed the cost of discharging the obligation; (4) either the undertaking was selected by competitive tender or the compensation level was set by reference to an efficient operator. All four must be met for the funding to fall outside Article 107(1) TFEU.
Compound interest (recovery)
Under Commission Regulation (EC) No 794/2004, recovery interest on incompatible State aid is compounded annually. The interest base grows each year, meaning the total recovery amount increases faster than under simple interest, particularly over long periods.

Frequently asked questions

common questions on the La Rochelle decision and airport State aid

Did the Commission find that all public money given to La Rochelle airport was illegal?

No. The Commission distinguished two categories of measures. Public funding to the airport itself (investment grants, repayable operating advances, shared-service underinvoicing in 2001-2005, and local authority promotional subsidies) was found to be State aid but compatible with the internal market under Article 107(3)(c) TFEU. No recovery was ordered for these measures.

The incompatible aid, for which recovery was ordered, was the marketing payments made by the CCI to Ryanair, AMS and Jet2 under the airline agreements. These payments were found to confer an advantage that no private airport would have granted because the NPV of every agreement was negative.

Why were Ryanair and AMS treated as one entity when they signed separate contracts?

Airport Marketing Services is wholly owned by Ryanair. Its sole commercial activity is selling marketing services on the Ryanair.com platform to airports that want to promote their routes to Ryanair passengers. AMS has no independent commercial existence separate from the Ryanair group.

The Commission found that Ryanair and AMS always contracted with airports together or in immediate sequence, that the marketing services AMS provided were inseparable from Ryanair's route-operating decisions, and that the overall commercial arrangement was a package: the CCI paid for Ryanair's presence, partly through the ASA with Ryanair and partly through the MSA with AMS. Treating them as separate entities would have allowed artificial disaggregation of what was in economic substance a single transaction.

Could the CCI not argue that attracting Ryanair brought tourism revenue to the region?

The Commission's MEO test focuses on the rationality of the agreement from the airport's own financial perspective, not from the perspective of the wider regional economy. Tourism benefits accruing to hotels, restaurants and the local economy are externalities that a private airport investor would not credit to itself when deciding whether to sign an agreement.

The incremental profitability analysis therefore included only the revenues the CCI itself could capture: landing fees, passenger charges, car parking, retail and food-and-beverage concessions. Regional tourism multipliers were excluded. This reflects the fundamental logic of the MEO test: a private investor does not receive a share of the regional GDP uplift from incoming tourists; it receives the revenues that flow through the airport.

What is the difference between start-up aid and the marketing payments at La Rochelle?

The 2014 Aviation Guidelines allow start-up aid for genuinely new routes at regional airports. To qualify, the aid must be time-limited (maximum three years), degressive each year, and provided for a route that is demonstrably new, meaning the airport has not previously offered regular scheduled service on that specific origin-destination pair within a defined look-back period.

The Ryanair/AMS payments at La Rochelle did not fit this framework because the routes were not new: Ryanair had operated them for years before each successive agreement was signed. The agreements were also not structured as degressive launch-phase grants but as ongoing commercial incentive payments. Relabelling them as "marketing services" did not change their economic character.

How is the recovery amount calculated if the exact figures are not in the operative articles?

The operative articles of Commission recovery decisions typically order France (or the relevant Member State) to recover "the aid referred to in Article [X]" without specifying a euro figure. The recovery amount is determined by France and the beneficiary during the national recovery process, working from the financial data disclosed during the investigation and the interest calculation rules in Commission Regulation (EC) No 794/2004.

Interest is compounded annually from the date of each individual payment. For payments made in 2003 and recovered in, say, 2023 or 2024, twenty years of compounding at the applicable rate substantially increases the total. France is required to report the recovery steps and amounts to the Commission under Article 6 of the Decision.

Does this decision mean regional airports in the EU cannot have any commercial agreements with airlines?

No. Regional airports may conclude commercial agreements with airlines. The State aid rules require only that those agreements are commercially rational from the airport's perspective, in the same way a private investor would assess them. If the incremental NPV is positive (the airline's passengers generate more revenue for the airport than the cost of attracting and serving them), there is no State aid issue.

The 2014 Aviation Guidelines also explicitly permit limited public support for new route development, provided it takes the form of time-limited, degressive start-up aid that complies with the Guidelines' conditions. What is not permitted is structuring ongoing commercial subsidies to airlines as "marketing agreements" when the economic substance is a payment to the airline to keep operating routes that the airport's own NPV analysis shows are not commercially self-sustaining.
