MiFID II: Markets in Financial Instruments

Directive 2014/65/EU is the master framework governing who may provide investment services in the EU and how organised financial trading must work. It introduced the OTF as a new venue category, imposed sweeping algorithmic trading controls, and overhauled investor protection rules for 27 Member States from 3 January 2018.

Overview

The EU's master framework for investment services and organised financial trading

Why MiFID II was needed

MiFID I (Directive 2004/39/EC) introduced the passport for investment firms, client categorisation, best execution and a three-venue taxonomy of regulated markets, MTFs and systematic internalisers. By 2008, however, the system showed serious weaknesses. Broker crossing networks operated entirely outside all three venue categories, executing trades without transparency obligations. Algorithmic and high-frequency trading had grown dramatically, outpacing the regulatory framework. Commodity derivative markets had experienced price dislocations partly attributed to speculative positions. Investor protection rules were inconsistently applied across Member States.

The Commission proposed MiFID II and its companion MiFIR together in October 2011. Parliament adopted the texts in April 2014 and the Council approved them in May 2014. Both were published in OJ L 173 on 12 June 2014 and signed in Brussels on 15 May 2014 by EP President Martin Schulz and Council Presidency Minister Dimitris Kourkoulas. The legal base is Article 53(1) TFEU (conditions for taking up and pursuing activities of self-employed persons), reflecting that the directive harmonises the conditions under which investment firms operate across the single market.

Application date: 3 January 2018

The original text of Directive 2014/65/EU set a transposition deadline of 3 July 2016 (Art 93) and application from 3 January 2017. Both dates were postponed by exactly one year by Directive (EU) 2016/1034 (and companion Regulation (EU) 2016/1033 for MiFIR), on the grounds that technical standards were not ready and ESMA IT systems needed more time. Accordingly:

  • Transposition deadline: 3 July 2017
  • Application date: 3 January 2018 (general provisions)
  • Non-equity consolidated tape (Art 65(2)): 3 September 2019
  • MiFID I (Directive 2004/39/EC) repealed: 3 January 2018

The directive is a recast: it fully replaces MiFID I rather than amending it, and Annex IV contains the correlation table between the two instruments.

Structure at a glance

The directive is structured into 170 recitals and 97 articles in seven Titles:

  • Title I (Arts 1-4): Scope and definitions
  • Title II (Arts 5-43): Authorisation and operating conditions for investment firms (Chapters I-IV including third-country firms)
  • Title III (Arts 44-56): Regulated markets
  • Title IV (Arts 57-58): Position limits and position management in commodity derivatives
  • Title V (Arts 59-66): Data reporting services (APAs, CTPs, ARMs)
  • Title VI (Arts 67-88): Competent authorities, supervisory powers, sanctions and cooperation
  • Title VII (Art 89): Delegated acts
  • Arts 90-97: Final provisions (reports, amendments to other directives, transposition, repeal)

Four Annexes complete the instrument: Annex I (services, activities and financial instruments), Annex II (professional clients), Annex III (repeals), Annex IV (MiFID I/II correlation table).

The MiFID II / MiFIR split

Directive for the framework; Regulation for transparency. Recital 7: "This Directive should therefore be read together with that Regulation."

Why a directive AND a regulation?

The choice to split the 2014 reform into a directive (MiFID II) and a directly applicable regulation (MiFIR, Regulation (EU) No 600/2014, CELEX 32014R0600) was deliberate. The authorisation framework, organisational requirements and conduct-of-business rules needed flexibility for national legal systems to implement them within their existing legal traditions -- hence a directive. But pre- and post-trade transparency obligations and transaction-reporting requirements must operate identically in every Member State to create a level playing field -- hence a regulation, directly applicable without transposition.

Recital 7 of the directive makes the relationship explicit: "This Directive should therefore be read together with that Regulation." The two instruments are published in the same Official Journal issue (OJ L 173, 12 June 2014).

Title I: Scope and definitions (Arts 1-4)

Art 1(7) closes the system. Art 4 provides 63 defined terms.

Art 1(7): the closed-system principle

Article 1(7) is architecturally pivotal: all multilateral systems in financial instruments must operate as a regulated market, MTF or OTF. There is no legitimate space for unregulated multilateral trading. This provision directly addresses the broker crossing networks that had proliferated outside the MiFID I venue framework. Any multilateral arrangement where multiple third-party buying and selling interests interact to produce a contract in a financial instrument must be authorised under one of the three venue regimes.

The directive applies to investment firms, market operators, data reporting services providers, and third-country firms providing investment services through EU branches. Credit institutions providing investment services are also bound (Art 1(3)). Structured deposits are covered when sold with advice (Art 1(4)).

Art 2: Key exemptions including the ancillary activity exemption

Article 2 exempts: insurance undertakings; persons providing services only within their group; incidental service providers; operators of commodity derivative or emission allowance positions ancillary to their main group business (the "ancillary activity exemption" under Art 2(1)(j)); ETS compliance operators; ESCB members; collective investment undertakings; pension funds; transmission system operators; and CSDs regulated under Union law.

The ancillary activity exemption is commercially significant for non-financial firms active in energy, commodity and carbon markets. It requires: (a) the activity is ancillary to and does not constitute a significant part of the main group business; (b) the firm does not apply high-frequency algorithmic trading techniques; (c) annual notification to the competent authority. ESMA develops the RTS methodology for what constitutes "ancillary" to the group's main business.

Art 4: 63 defined terms

Article 4(1) contains 63 definitions forming the precise vocabulary of EU financial markets law. Key definitions include:

  • Investment firm (no. 1): a legal person whose regular occupation or business is the professional provision of investment services or activities.
  • Regulated market (no. 21): a multilateral system operated by a market operator, bringing together multiple third-party buying and selling interests under non-discretionary rules, resulting in a contract, authorised under Title III.
  • MTF (no. 22): same structure as a regulated market but authorised under Title II, operated by an investment firm or market operator.
  • OTF (no. 23): a multilateral system that is not a regulated market or MTF, covering bonds, structured finance products, emission allowances and derivatives, where third-party interests interact under discretionary execution. New in MiFID II.
  • Trading venue (no. 24): a regulated market, MTF or OTF.
  • Systematic internaliser (SI) (no. 20): an investment firm which, on an organised, frequent, systematic and substantial basis, deals on own account when executing client orders outside a regulated market, MTF or OTF.
  • Algorithmic trading (no. 39): trading where a computer algorithm automatically determines individual order parameters with limited or no human intervention. Post-trade processing is excluded.
  • High-frequency algorithmic trading technique (no. 40): characterised by all three of: (a) latency-minimising infrastructure (co-location, proximity hosting, high-speed DEA); (b) system-determination of orders without human intervention; (c) high intraday message rates of orders, quotes or cancellations.
  • Direct electronic access (DEA) (no. 41): arrangement whereby a venue member permits a third party to use its trading code to transmit orders directly to the trading venue.
  • Professional client (no. 10): a client meeting the Annex II criteria (per se: credit institutions, investment firms, large corporates; elective: clients opting in after meeting two of three tests -- balance sheet over EUR 500,000, portfolio over EUR 500,000, or one year's financial sector experience).
  • Retail client (no. 11): any client who is not a professional client.
  • Eligible counterparty (no. 18): investment firms, credit institutions, insurance companies, UCITS, pension funds and other authorised financial institutions -- transactions with them benefit from reduced conduct-of-business obligations.
  • SME (no. 13): for this directive, companies with average market capitalisation below EUR 200 million over the previous three calendar years.

Title II, Chapter I: Authorisation (Arts 5-15)

Prior authorisation by the home Member State is the gateway to providing investment services across the EU single market

5
Art 5 - Requirement for authorisation
All professional investment service provision must have prior authorisation from the home Member State competent authority. Market operators may also be authorised to operate MTFs or OTFs. ESMA maintains the Union-wide register of authorised investment firms.
6
Art 6 - Scope of authorisation
Authorisation is activity-specific, specifies the permitted investment services and activities, and is valid throughout the Union (the single-market passport). Authorisation solely for ancillary services is not permitted.
7
Art 7 - Procedure
The competent authority must inform the applicant within six months of a complete application whether authorisation is granted or refused. ESMA develops RTS on information requirements (submitted by July 2015) and ITS on standard forms (by January 2016).
9
Art 9 - Management body
Investment firms must comply with CRD IV management body requirements (Arts 88 and 91 of Directive 2013/36/EU): fitness and propriety, governance arrangements, remuneration policy oversight, and product and service policy. At least two persons must effectively direct the business (four-eyes principle). The competent authority must refuse authorisation if the management body fails fitness and propriety.
10
Arts 10-13 - Qualifying holdings
Proposed acquisitions above 10%, and increases to 20%, 30% or 50%, must be notified. Assessment period: 60 working days from written acknowledgement, extendable by 20 days for an information request. Five assessment criteria (Art 13): acquirer reputation; proposed directors' reputation and experience; acquirer's financial soundness; continued prudential compliance; money laundering risk.
14
Art 14 - Investor compensation scheme
Membership of an investor compensation scheme is a mandatory condition of authorisation. Art 15 further requires sufficient initial capital consistent with Regulation (EU) No 575/2013 (CRR), calibrated to the nature of the investment service.

Third-country firms: Articles 39-43

Member States may require third-country firms to establish a branch before providing services to retail and certain professional clients. Branch authorisation conditions under Art 39 include: home country authorisation and adequate supervision; a cooperation agreement between the home country supervisor and the Member State competent authority; sufficient initial capital; management fitness and propriety; a tax information exchange agreement; and investor compensation scheme membership.

Branches must comply with the same conduct-of-business and organisational requirements as EU investment firms (Art 41(2)). No third-country firm may be treated more favourably than Union firms. Where a client approaches a third-country firm on its own exclusive initiative, no branch requirement applies (Art 42). The directly applicable third-country equivalence regime is in MiFIR Arts 46-49, not the directive.

Title II, Chapter II: Operating conditions (Arts 16-38)

Article 16 sets the organisational backbone. Product governance, call recording, client asset safeguarding and SME growth markets all live here.

Article 16: Organisational requirements

Article 16 is the backbone of firm-level obligations. Key sub-requirements include:

  • Art 16(2): compliance policies, procedures and personal transaction rules
  • Art 16(3) first paragraph: conflicts of interest prevention; Art 16(3) second to sixth paragraphs: product governance -- manufacturers must run a product approval process, define a target market, review products regularly, and make target market information available to distributors; distributors must obtain and understand product information and not recommend outside the target market
  • Art 16(4): business continuity arrangements
  • Art 16(5): outsourcing conditions (functions may be outsourced subject to defined conditions; the firm retains full responsibility)
  • Art 16(6): record keeping of all services, activities and transactions for at least five years, up to seven years at the competent authority's request
  • Art 16(7): telephone and electronic communications recording -- all calls that result or may result in transactions must be recorded; clients must be notified in advance; five-year (up to seven-year) retention
  • Arts 16(8)-(9): client asset safeguarding -- client funds and financial instruments must be kept separate from firm assets; no use of client assets for own account without express consent
  • Art 16(10): prohibition on title transfer financial collateral arrangements with retail clients
Article 33: SME growth markets

An MTF operator may apply to the home competent authority to be registered as an SME growth market. Conditions: at least 50% of issuers on the market must be SMEs at the time of registration and, assessed annually, for each subsequent calendar year; appropriate admission criteria; sufficient public information at admission; ongoing periodic financial reporting; compliance with the market abuse regulation; and effective systems for market abuse prevention.

ESMA publishes and maintains the list of registered SME growth markets. The registration does not alter the MTF's authorisation but provides a marketing designation and lighter touch under various EU rules, particularly for smaller issuers seeking to access capital markets.

Article 17: Algorithmic and high-frequency trading

Art 17 is the foundational provision for algo and HFT regulation -- a direct response to the 2010 Flash Crash and European market structure concerns

Why Art 17 was needed

By 2010, algorithmic and high-frequency trading accounted for a significant share of European equity market volume. The 2010 Flash Crash in the United States and several European market dislocations highlighted the risks of erroneous orders, cascading liquidity withdrawal, and the potential use of automated systems for market manipulation. Recitals 59-68 of MiFID II set out the legislative rationale for Art 17's comprehensive requirements.

Art 17(1): System requirements

Investment firms engaged in algorithmic trading must have resilient and capacity-sufficient trading systems with appropriate thresholds and limits. Systems must prevent sending erroneous orders, creating or contributing to disorderly market conditions, and must not be used for market abuse purposes. Firms must have business continuity arrangements for any failure of their trading systems.

Art 17(2): Notification and record keeping

Firms must notify their home competent authority and the trading venue(s) on which they trade. The competent authority may require a description of the nature of the firm's algorithmic trading strategies, details of trading parameters or limits, and key compliance and risk controls. HFT firms specifically must store accurate, time-sequenced records of all placed orders including cancellations in an approved form and make them available to competent authorities.

Arts 17(3)-(4): Market making obligations

Where a firm pursues a market making strategy by algorithmic trading, it must carry out market making continuously during a specified proportion of the trading venue's trading hours (save under exceptional circumstances). The firm must enter a binding written agreement with the trading venue specifying its obligations, and have systems capable of fulfilling those obligations at all times.

Art 17(5): Direct electronic access (DEA)

Firms providing DEA to clients must assess client suitability before granting access; impose pre-set trading and credit thresholds; monitor client trading in real time; retain the right to stop transmitting client orders; retain full and unconditional responsibility for client orders transmitted under their trading code; and enter a binding written agreement with each DEA client specifying rights and obligations.

Level 2: Commission Delegated Regulation (EU) 2017/589

The detailed organisational requirements for investment firms engaged in algorithmic trading are set out in Commission Delegated Regulation (EU) 2017/589, which implements Art 17 in full. It specifies: governance arrangements; annual review of the trading systems; pre-trade and post-trade controls; real-time market-data monitoring; kill-switch requirements; DEA policies; log-file and record-keeping requirements; conformance testing; and the form and content of required notifications. Firms active in algorithmic or HFT strategies must comply with both Art 17 of the directive and the technical detail in Delegated Regulation 2017/589.

The venue taxonomy: RM, MTF and OTF

Three authorised venue categories, each with distinct governance requirements. The OTF is the critical new addition in MiFID II.

Regulated Market (RM)

Title III (Arts 44-56). A multilateral system operated by a market operator. Execution is non-discretionary: rules are transparent and pre-set. Admission of members (Art 53) follows transparent, non-discriminatory, objective criteria. The market operator may not execute client orders against proprietary capital or engage in matched principal trading (Art 47(2)).

Regulated markets must have resilient trading systems (Art 48), circuit breakers for significant price movements (Art 48(5)), order-to-trade ratio limits for algorithmic trading (Art 48(6)), tick size regimes calibrated to liquidity (Art 49), and clock synchronisation (Art 50). ESMA maintains the Union list of regulated markets (Art 56).

Multilateral Trading Facility (MTF)

Title II, Arts 18-19. A multilateral system operated by an investment firm or market operator under Title II. Like regulated markets, execution is non-discretionary: the rules determine which orders interact and how contracts are concluded. MTFs may not execute client orders against proprietary capital or engage in matched principal trading (Art 19(5)).

MTFs must have at least three materially active members or users (Art 18(7)). They may be registered as SME growth markets (Art 33). The transparency obligations (pre- and post-trade) for MTFs are set out in MiFIR, not the directive.

Organised Trading Facility (OTF) -- New

Title II, Art 20. The OTF is the new venue category introduced by MiFID II to capture broker crossing networks and other hybrid arrangements. It covers bonds, structured finance products, emission allowances and derivatives (not equity instruments). Execution on an OTF is discretionary: the operator decides whether to place or retract an order and whether to match a specific client order (Art 20(6)).

Critical constraints: the OTF operator may not execute against its own or any group entity's proprietary capital (Art 20(1)). Matched principal trading is permitted only in bonds, structured finance products, emission allowances and certain derivatives with client consent, and is prohibited for cleared derivatives (Art 20(2)). Dealing on own account (other than matched principal) is permitted only in sovereign debt for which there is no liquid market (Art 20(3)). An OTF and a systematic internaliser cannot operate within the same legal entity; an OTF cannot connect with an SI (Art 20(4)).

Investor protection: Arts 23-30

Suitability and appropriateness (Art 25), best execution (Art 27), product governance, conflicts and inducements regime

Art 24: General principles -- the headline conduct rule

Article 24(1) states the overarching obligation: investment firms must act honestly, fairly and professionally in the best interests of clients. Article 24(2) imposes product governance obligations on both manufacturers (design instruments for an identified target market) and distributors (ensure recommendations are in clients' best interests and stay within the target market).

All information, including marketing communications, must be fair, clear and not misleading (Art 24(3)). Pre-service disclosure requirements cover: the nature and independence of advice; financial instruments and strategies proposed; execution venues; and -- critically -- all costs and charges including third-party payments, aggregated to show the cumulative effect on return, with an itemised breakdown available on request (Art 24(4)).

The inducements regime (Art 24(9)) permits a firm to receive or pay a fee or commission from or to a third party only if three conditions are met: (a) the payment enhances service quality; (b) it does not impair the duty to act in the client's best interests; (c) it is disclosed. For independent advice and portfolio management, the firm must additionally assess a sufficiently diverse range of products not limited to group products, and must not accept or retain any third-party fee or commission (Arts 24(7)-(8)).

Art 25: Suitability and appropriateness

For investment advice and portfolio management, the suitability assessment must cover three dimensions: (a) knowledge and experience of the client relevant to the investment type; (b) financial situation including ability to bear losses; (c) investment objectives including risk tolerance. A written suitability statement must be provided to the client before the transaction is executed (Art 25(6)).

For other investment services (execution of orders etc.), an appropriateness test assesses the client's knowledge and experience in relation to the specific product. If the product is inappropriate, the firm must issue a warning (Art 25(3)).

The execution-only concession (Art 25(4)) removes the appropriateness requirement for non-complex instruments (shares, bonds, money-market instruments, standard UCITS) where the service is provided at the client's own initiative, the client is clearly warned, and the firm meets its conflict-of-interest obligations. This is the basis for execution-only stockbroking platforms.

Art 27: Best execution

Investment firms must take all sufficient steps to obtain the best possible result for clients, taking into account: price; costs; speed; likelihood of execution and settlement; size; nature; and any other relevant consideration. For retail clients, best possible result is determined by total consideration (price plus all execution costs), not price alone.

Firms must establish and implement an order execution policy (Art 27(4)-(5)). Prior client consent to the execution policy is required; separate express consent is required before executing outside a trading venue. Firms must publish annually the top five execution venues per financial instrument class and a quality of execution summary (Art 27(6)). The execution policy must be reviewed at least annually (Art 27(7)).

Client categorisation: retail, professional and eligible counterparty

MiFID II's three-tier client categorisation determines the level of protection each client receives. Retail clients (any client who is not a professional client) receive the highest protection: full suitability/appropriateness obligations, all disclosure requirements, execution only with their express consent. Professional clients (per Annex II: credit institutions, investment firms, large corporates with balance sheet over EUR 20 million or net turnover over EUR 40 million or own funds over EUR 2 million, and other institutional investors; elective professionals meeting two of three tests -- balance sheet EUR 500,000+, portfolio EUR 500,000+, one year's financial sector experience) have reduced protection on certain provisions. Eligible counterparties (investment firms, credit institutions, insurance companies, UCITS, pension funds, national governments, central banks and other authorised financial institutions -- Art 30) are exempt from Arts 24 (save paragraphs 4 and 5), 25 (save paragraph 6), 27 and 28(1). The basic obligation to act honestly, fairly and professionally and communicate in a clear manner remains.

Art 23: Conflicts of interest

Investment firms must take all appropriate steps to identify, prevent and manage conflicts of interest between the firm, its managers, employees, tied agents and their clients, and between clients inter se. Where management through organisational arrangements is not sufficient to prevent harm to client interests, prior disclosure to the client in a durable medium is required. The disclosure must explain the nature and source of the conflict clearly enough for the client to make an informed decision (Art 23(2)). Disclosure is a last resort, not a substitute for adequate organisational measures.

Title III: Regulated markets (Arts 44-56)

Authorisation, governance, systems resilience, circuit breakers, tick sizes and admission to trading

Authorisation and governance (Arts 44-47)

Only systems complying with Title III may be authorised as regulated markets (Art 44). The public law governing trading is that of the home Member State. Market operators of significant regulated markets must have a nomination committee and comply with directorship limits: one executive plus two non-executive directors, or four non-executive directors (Art 45(4)). Diversity policies are required.

Regulated markets must manage conflicts of interest, have sound risk management, maintain sound technical operations, ensure fair and orderly trading rules, provide for efficient settlement, and maintain adequate financial resources (Art 47). Market operators may not execute client orders against proprietary capital (Art 47(2)).

Systems resilience and circuit breakers (Art 48)

Regulated markets must have trading systems with sufficient capacity to handle peak order volumes and survive extreme market conditions. Written market making agreements are required (Art 48(2)-(3)). Circuit breakers (automatic trading halts on significant price movements) are mandatory (Art 48(5)). Regulated markets must implement order-to-trade ratio limits to prevent algorithmic trading strategies from flooding the order book (Art 48(6)). DEA controls (Art 48(7)) mirror Art 17(5) requirements. Co-location services must be offered on a transparent, fair and non-discriminatory basis (Art 48(8)).

Tick sizes (Art 49) and clock synchronisation (Art 50)

Regulated markets must adopt tick size regimes calibrated to the liquidity profiles and average bid-ask spreads of the relevant instruments. The tick size regime prevents artificial price improvement below minimum price increments, reducing certain latency-arbitrage strategies. ESMA develops the minimum tick size RTS. All trading venues and their members must synchronise business clocks to a level of accuracy specified in ESMA RTS (Art 50).

Admission to trading (Art 51) and CCP access (Art 55)

Regulated markets must apply clear, transparent and non-discriminatory rules for admission of financial instruments to trading (Art 51). For derivative contracts, admission rules must ensure orderly pricing and effective settlement. Regulated markets must verify that issuers of transferable securities comply with EU disclosure obligations. Secondary trading admission without issuer consent is permitted (Art 51(5)).

Regulated markets may not restrict the use of CCPs or clearing systems from other Member States unless necessary for orderly functioning (Art 55). ESMA maintains the Union list of regulated markets (Art 56).

Title IV: Commodity derivative position limits and reporting (Arts 57-58)

A direct response to concerns about speculative positions in agricultural and energy commodity derivatives

Art 57: Position limits

Competent authorities must establish and apply position limits on the maximum net position in commodity derivatives traded on trading venues and economically equivalent OTC contracts. Limits apply at aggregate group level. Two stated purposes: (a) prevent market abuse; (b) support orderly pricing and settlement conditions, including convergence between derivative delivery-month prices and spot prices.

Limits do not apply to positions held by non-financial entities that objectively reduce risks directly relating to the commercial activity of that entity. ESMA develops the RTS methodology taking into account at least: contract maturity; deliverable supply; open interest; volatility; number and size of market participants; characteristics of the underlying commodity market; and development of new contracts.

Where the same derivative is traded significantly in more than one jurisdiction, the competent authority of the venue with the largest volume is the central competent authority and sets the single position limit for all trading in that contract (Art 57(6)). ESMA publishes a database of position limits and position management controls (Art 57(10)). Competent authorities must notify ESMA at least 24 hours before measures take effect.

Position management controls must empower trading venues to: monitor open interest; access information on the size and purpose of positions; require position reduction or termination; and require liquidity provision back to the market on a temporary basis (Art 57(8)).

Art 58: Position reporting

Trading venues that trade commodity derivatives must publish a weekly aggregated position report by category of holder: investment firms and credit institutions; investment funds; other financial institutions; commercial undertakings; and ETS compliance operators. The report must specify long and short positions, changes since the previous report, percentage of total open interest, and number of persons in each category.

Investment firms trading OTC commodity derivatives must provide daily complete breakdowns of their positions to the competent authority. All reports must distinguish positions qualifying for the commercial hedge exemption from other positions.

Title V: Data reporting services (Arts 59-66)

Three authorised service types: APAs publish trade reports, CTPs consolidate them, ARMs report transactions to regulators

APA: Approved Publication Arrangement

An APA publishes post-trade reports on behalf of investment firms pursuant to MiFIR Arts 20-21. APAs must publish trade information as close to real-time as technically possible. Data must be available free of charge 15 minutes after publication (Art 64(3)). APAs must prevent conflicts of interest and maintain sound security mechanisms.

CTP: Consolidated Tape Provider

A CTP collects trade reports from all regulated markets, MTFs, OTFs and APAs and consolidates them into a continuous electronic live data stream (Art 65). Real-time publication (free after 15 minutes). For equity instruments, the stream must include an algorithmic trading indicator (Art 65(1)(h)). Application of Art 65(2) for non-equity instruments was deferred to 3 September 2019. If commercial CTPs fail, the Commission may initiate public procurement (Art 90(2)-(3)) -- a backstop triggered under the 2024 review.

ARM: Approved Reporting Mechanism

An ARM reports transaction details to competent authorities or ESMA on behalf of investment firms pursuant to MiFIR Art 26. ARMs must report as quickly as possible and no later than close of business on the following working day (Art 66). Market operators that operate regulated markets may also be authorised to provide APA, CTP or ARM services within their existing authorisation (Art 59(2)).

Title VI: Competent authorities, sanctions and cooperation (Arts 67-88)

Designation, supervisory powers, minimum maximum fines, name-and-shame publication, whistleblowing and cross-border cooperation

Art 69: Supervisory powers -- minimum list

Article 69(2) sets out the minimum supervisory powers that competent authorities must possess. The list includes: access to documents and records; power to summon and question persons; conduct on-site inspections; require recordings of telephone and electronic communications; freeze or sequester assets; temporarily ban professional activity; refer to criminal prosecution; require provision of commodity position information; require cessation of practices; suspend or remove financial instruments from trading; require position reduction; limit commodity derivative positions (Art 69(2)(p)); issue public notices; suspend marketing or sale of financial instruments or structured deposits; remove a natural person from a management board (Art 69(2)(u)).

Art 70: Administrative sanctions -- minimum maximum levels

Member States must ensure effective, proportionate and dissuasive administrative sanctions for all infringements. For the most serious infringements the minimum maximum fine levels are:

  • Legal persons: maximum fines of at least EUR 5,000,000 or 10% of total annual turnover (whichever is higher)
  • Natural persons: maximum fines of at least EUR 5,000,000
  • Benefit-based: maximum fines of at least twice the benefit derived

Other sanctions include public statements identifying the person and the infringement; withdrawal or suspension of authorisation; temporary or permanent ban on management functions; and temporary bans on regulated market membership.

Art 71: Publication of decisions

Sanctions must be published on the competent authority website without undue delay, identifying the natural or legal person and describing the infringement. Minimum retention: five years. Anonymous or delayed publication is permitted where publication would cause disproportionate harm, jeopardise financial market stability, or prejudice an ongoing investigation. Sanctions not published publicly must be reported to ESMA's central database.

Art 73: Whistleblowing

Competent authorities must establish effective mechanisms for reporting potential or actual infringements, with appropriate protection for employees who report against retaliation, discrimination and unfair treatment. Member States may provide for financial incentives for whistleblowers under national law. Reported information must be held in confidence subject to criminal procedure rules.

Key definitions (Art 4)

The 63 definitions in Art 4(1) form the vocabulary of EU financial markets law

Investment firm
A legal person whose regular occupation or business is the professional provision of one or more investment services or activities. Non-legal persons may qualify if subject to equivalent prudential rules (Art 4(1)(1)).
Regulated market (RM)
A multilateral system operated by a market operator, bringing together multiple third-party buying and selling interests under non-discretionary rules, resulting in a contract, authorised under Title III (Art 4(1)(21)).
MTF
Multilateral trading facility: same structural definition as RM but authorised under Title II and operated by an investment firm or market operator. Non-discretionary execution rules (Art 4(1)(22)).
OTF
Organised trading facility: a multilateral system that is not an RM or MTF, covering bonds, structured finance products, emission allowances and derivatives, where third-party interests interact under discretionary execution. New in MiFID II (Art 4(1)(23)).
Systematic internaliser (SI)
An investment firm which, on an organised, frequent, systematic and substantial basis, deals on own account when executing client orders outside an RM, MTF or OTF (Art 4(1)(20)).
Trading venue
A regulated market, MTF or OTF -- the three authorised venue categories (Art 4(1)(24)).
Algorithmic trading
Trading in financial instruments where a computer algorithm automatically determines individual order parameters with limited or no human intervention. Post-trade processing is excluded (Art 4(1)(39)).
HFT technique
High-frequency algorithmic trading technique: all three of (a) latency-minimising infrastructure; (b) system-determined orders without human intervention; (c) high intraday message rates of orders, quotes or cancellations (Art 4(1)(40)).
DEA
Direct electronic access: an arrangement whereby a member of a trading venue permits a person to use its trading code to transmit orders electronically directly to the trading venue (Art 4(1)(41)).
Professional client
A client meeting the criteria in Annex II: per se (credit institutions, investment firms, large corporates) or elective (two of three tests: balance sheet EUR 500,000+, portfolio EUR 500,000+, or one year's financial sector experience).
Eligible counterparty
Investment firms, credit institutions, insurance companies, UCITS, pension funds, national governments and central banks. Reduced conduct rules apply: exempt from Arts 24 (save paras 4-5), 25 (save para 6), 27 and 28(1) (Art 30).
SME (for this directive)
Companies with average market capitalisation below EUR 200 million based on end-year quotes for the previous three calendar years. Relevant for SME growth market eligibility (Art 4(1)(13)).

Legislative timeline

From Council Directive 93/22/EEC to the 2024 MiFID II review

10 May 1993
Council Directive 93/22/EEC (Investment Services Directive) -- the predecessor, creating the investment firm passport across the EU single market. Applied from 1 January 1996. Did not create a best execution obligation nor a level playing field for equities trading.
21 April 2004
Directive 2004/39/EC (MiFID I) published. Applied from 1 November 2007. Introduced best execution, client categorisation, the regulated market / MTF / systematic internaliser venue taxonomy, and passporting for investment services. Implemented by Commission Directive 2006/73/EC (organisational requirements and conduct of business) and Commission Regulation 1287/2006 (transparency).
20 October 2011
Commission proposes MiFID II and MiFIR -- the reform package responding to the financial crisis, the growth of algorithmic trading, commodity market concerns, and broker crossing network arbitrage. Recitals 4-6 set out the post-crisis rationale.
15 April 2014
European Parliament adopts MiFID II and MiFIR in plenary. ECON committee rapporteur: Markus Ferber (EPP/Germany).
15 May 2014
MiFID II (Directive 2014/65/EU) and MiFIR (Regulation (EU) No 600/2014) signed in Brussels by EP President Martin Schulz and Council Presidency Minister Dimitris Kourkoulas.
12 June 2014
Publication in OJ L 173, pp. 349 and 84 respectively. Entry into force: 2 July 2014 (20th day after publication). Original transposition deadline: 3 July 2016. Original application: 3 January 2017.
30 June 2016
Directive (EU) 2016/1034 published, postponing transposition deadline by one year to 3 July 2017 and application date to 3 January 2018. Companion Regulation (EU) 2016/1033 does the same for MiFIR. Reason: ESMA technical standards not complete; National IT systems not ready.
3 January 2018
MiFID II and MiFIR apply. MiFID I (Directive 2004/39/EC) is repealed. The EU's financial markets framework is modernised across 27 Member States simultaneously. ESMA Level 2 framework (Delegated Regulation 2017/565, 2017/567, 2017/589 etc.) also applies from this date.
3 September 2019
Non-equity consolidated tape obligations (Art 65(2) of the directive, as applied by Regulation (EU) 2016/1033) take effect. Commercial CTP market for bonds remained underdeveloped, contributing to the eventual 2024 review mandate for a procurement-based tape.
7 March 2022
Commission proposes MiFID II / MiFIR review package as part of the Capital Markets Union action plan. Key proposals: mandatory consolidated tape; payment for order flow ban; revised equity transparency waivers; updated systematic internaliser regime; partial re-bundling of research for SMEs.
28 March 2024
Directive (EU) 2024/790 and Regulation (EU) 2024/791 published -- the 2024 MiFID II and MiFIR amendments. Transposition deadline for Directive 2024/790: 29 September 2025. Key changes: payment for order flow ban; mandatory consolidated tape for bonds and equities via ESMA procurement; revised consolidated tape provider selection; updated systematic internaliser provisions; equity transparency waiver reform.

Glossary

Common terms and abbreviations used across MiFID II and MiFIR

APA
Approved Publication Arrangement. A data reporting services provider authorised to publish post-trade transparency reports on behalf of investment firms pursuant to MiFIR Arts 20-21 (Art 4(1)(52)).
ARM
Approved Reporting Mechanism. A data reporting services provider authorised to report transaction details to competent authorities or ESMA on behalf of investment firms pursuant to MiFIR Art 26 (Art 4(1)(54)).
CTP
Consolidated Tape Provider. A data reporting services provider authorised to collect post-trade reports from all trading venues and APAs and consolidate them into a continuous electronic live data stream (Art 4(1)(53)).
ESMA
European Securities and Markets Authority. The EU supervisory authority for financial markets, mandated by MiFID II to develop RTS and ITS, maintain registers (investment firms, regulated markets, data reporting services providers) and publish guidance.
MiFIR
Markets in Financial Instruments Regulation. Regulation (EU) No 600/2014 (CELEX 32014R0600), the companion directly applicable regulation covering transparency and transaction reporting. Published in OJ L 173 on the same date as MiFID II.
Level 2
The delegated regulations and implementing regulations adopted by the Commission on the basis of MiFID II's technical standard mandates, forming the detailed technical framework. Principal texts: CDR 2017/565 (organisational requirements), CDR 2017/589 (algorithmic trading), CDR 2017/590 (transaction reporting).
Product governance
Obligations under Arts 16(3) and 24(2) requiring manufacturers of financial instruments to define a target market, design products for that market, and make target market information available to distributors; and requiring distributors to understand products and stay within the target market.
Inducements
Fees, commissions or non-monetary benefits paid or received by an investment firm from or to a third party in connection with providing services to clients. Permitted only if they enhance service quality, do not impair the best-interest duty, and are disclosed (Art 24(9)). Prohibited for independent advice and portfolio management.
PFOF
Payment for order flow. The practice of brokers receiving payments from market makers or other liquidity providers in return for routing orders to them. Prohibited in several Member States under national law and banned EU-wide by Regulation (EU) 2024/791 (the 2024 MiFIR amendment).

Related canon entries

The eu_financial_markets and eu_insurance families in the Brubru EU Canon

eu_insurance family
Solvency II (Directive 2009/138/EC)
Directive 2009/138/EC is the prudential framework for the EU insurance and reinsurance industry: risk-based capital (SCR and MCR), valuation of technical provisions at market-consistent values, ORSA, supervisory review (Pillar II), and public disclosure (Pillar III). Solvency II applies to insurers providing insurance-based investment products (IBIPs); the distribution of IBIPs is subject to the IDD (formerly the Insurance Mediation Directive amended by Art 91 of MiFID II).
eu_telecoms family
European Electronic Communications Code (Directive (EU) 2018/1972)
Directive (EU) 2018/1972 codified and modernised the EU telecoms framework into a single instrument. Its connectivity infrastructure rules are relevant to trading venues and financial infrastructure operators that depend on electronic communications to run market systems and data reporting services.
eu_financial_markets family
MiFIR -- Regulation (EU) No 600/2014
The companion directly applicable regulation: pre- and post-trade transparency for equity and non-equity instruments, transaction reporting to competent authorities (Art 26), trading obligations for shares and derivatives, systematic internaliser obligations, third-country firm equivalence access regime, and product intervention powers. Published in the same OJ as MiFID II. The 2024 amendment (Regulation (EU) 2024/791) introduces a mandatory consolidated tape and the payment for order flow ban.
