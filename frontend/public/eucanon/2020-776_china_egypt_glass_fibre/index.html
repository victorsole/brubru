EU's countervailing duties on glass fibre fabrics from China and Egypt

Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) 2020/776 imposes definitive anti-subsidy duties on woven and stitched glass fibre fabrics from China and Egypt, finding that CNBM and Yuntianhua received extensive state subsidies, and establishing a landmark cross-border attribution doctrine for Chinese-financed production in Egypt's Suez Economic and Trade Cooperation Zone.

What this regulation does

Plain-English summary of four operative effects

Imposing definitive countervailing duties

Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) 2020/776, adopted on 12 June 2020 and published in OJ L 189 on 15 June 2020, imposes definitive countervailing duties (CVDs) on imports of certain woven and/or stitched glass fibre fabrics (GFF) originating in the People's Republic of China and the Arab Republic of Egypt. The investigation was initiated on 16 May 2019 following a complaint lodged on 1 April 2019 by Tech-Fab Europe, the association of Union GFF producers. The legal basis is Regulation (EU) 2016/1037, specifically Articles 15 and 24(1).

CVDs are anti-subsidy measures applied at the EU's external border to offset subsidies granted by the government of an exporting country to its producers, thereby restoring a level playing field for Union industry. The duties are expressed as ad valorem percentages of the customs value of the imported product and apply in addition to any applicable customs tariff and, in this case, the parallel anti-dumping duties imposed by Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) 2020/492.

Establishing a cross-border subsidy attribution doctrine

The most legally significant element of this regulation is the Commission's treatment of Egyptian producers. Both Egyptian companies under investigation, Jushi Egypt for Fiberglass Industry SAE and Hengshi Egypt Fiberglass Fabrics SAE, are subsidiaries of China's CNBM Group. They operate inside the China-Egypt Suez Economic and Trade Cooperation Zone (SETC-Zone), a special economic zone near Port Said established under Egyptian Law 83/2002 and developed by a Chinese state-controlled joint venture.

The Commission found that subsidies provided by both the Government of China and the Government of Egypt to companies in the SETC-Zone were countervailable. It drew on Articles 4 and 5 of the 2016 Cooperation Agreement between China's Ministry of Commerce and the Egyptian Suez Canal Economic Zone Authority, and on Article 11 of the ILC Articles on State Responsibility (acknowledgement and adoption), to attribute Chinese government support to production on Egyptian soil. This cross-border attribution doctrine was novel in EU trade defence practice at the time of the regulation's adoption.

Amending the parallel anti-dumping regulation

Article 2 of this regulation amends Implementing Regulation (EU) 2020/492 (the parallel anti-dumping regulation on GFF from China and Egypt) to insert TARIC additional codes and update the combined duty table to ensure no double-counting of benefits, as required under Articles 24(1) of the Basic Anti-Subsidy Regulation and 14(1) of the Basic Anti-Dumping Regulation. The two regulations operate as a coordinated pair.

Discontinuing registration; no retroactive collection

Article 3 discontinues the registration of imports established under Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) 2020/44 of 22 January 2020. The Commission had decided on 24 January 2020 not to impose provisional measures, citing the need for additional time to analyse the novel cross-border subsidy questions related to the SETC-Zone. Because no provisional measures were imposed, there is no basis for retroactive collection of duties under Article 16 of the Basic Regulation; duties apply only from entry into force.

Product scope

CN codes, product types, and downstream use

CN and TARIC codes

The product under investigation is classified under the following CN codes, with the specific TARIC suffixes in parentheses:

  • ex 7019 39 00 (TARIC: 7019390080)
  • ex 7019 40 00 (TARIC: 7019400080)
  • ex 7019 59 00 (TARIC: 7019590080)
  • ex 7019 90 00 (TARIC: 7019900080)
Woven rovings (WR)
Fabrics woven from rovings (bundles of glass filaments). Used primarily in composites manufacturing.
Non-crimp fabrics (NCF)
Multi-axial fabrics in which fibres are stitched together without weaving, providing directional strength properties.
Complex materials (CM)
Combinations of GFF layers with other materials such as foam or other fibre types.
Principal downstream use: wind turbine blades

GFF is a key input in the manufacture of composite materials used in wind turbine blades, rotor nacelles, and other structural components. Wind turbine manufacturers were among the principal downstream users and were active participants in the investigation (recital 1079). Notably, Vestas and SGRE together consumed more than 20% of Union GFF demand and imported more than 30% of all imports from the countries concerned (recital 1079). The Commission acknowledged their concerns but found that GFF represents only 0.1% to 2% of total wind park construction costs (recital 1087), limiting the impact of duties on wind turbine competitiveness.

Product Control Numbers (PCNs) were used to match like-for-like product types when calculating price undercutting and injury margins. The "like product" is GFF manufactured by the Union industry with the same basic physical and chemical characteristics as the imported product (recital 936).

Companies sampled

Union producers, Chinese exporter groups, and Egyptian producers

Union industry (4 sampled)

The broader Union industry comprised 16 producers in total (recital 936). Total Union production was almost 115,000 tonnes during the IP (recital 936). Four producers were selected for sampling, representing more than 40% of total Union GFF production (recital 83):

  • Owens Corning (OC)
  • Chomarat
  • Saertex
  • Ahlstrom
Chinese exporter sample

Eight Chinese producers or groups came forward and requested questionnaires (recital 87). Two groups were selected, together representing more than 79% of reported Chinese exports of GFF to the Union during the IP (recital 88):

  • CNBM Group: comprising China National Building Material Group Co. Ltd, China Jushi Co. Ltd, Jushi Group Co. Ltd, Hengshi Group, Taishan Fiberglass Inc., and Sinoma Science and Technology Co. Ltd. CNBM is controlled by SASAC. CNBM held a 26.97% stake in China Jushi and a 60.24% stake in Sinoma Science and Technology (recital 95).
  • Yuntianhua Group: comprising Yunnan Yuntianhua Co. Ltd, Chongqing Polycomp International Co. Ltd (CPIC), PGTEX China Co. Ltd, and CTM Co. Ltd.
Egyptian producers (both included, no formal sampling)

Both known Egyptian producers, Jushi Egypt for Fiberglass Industry SAE and Hengshi Egypt Fiberglass Fabrics SAE, were included without formal sampling. Together they represented 100% of Egyptian exports of GFF to the Union and 100% of Egyptian GFF production during the IP (recital 107). Both are subsidiaries of the CNBM Group.

Subsidies countervailed: China

Preferential lending, grants, LTAR inputs, and tax preferences

Preferential lending

Thirty-six financial institutions provided financing to the sampled Chinese groups during the IP. Of these, 29 were state-owned (recital 222). Only China Export-Import Bank (EXIM) provided questionnaire replies and submitted to verification (recital 228); all other banks either did not reply or provided incomplete information. The Commission applied facts available under Article 28 of the Basic Regulation where cooperation was withheld.

Because the Chinese banking system is characterised by significant state involvement, lending rates below a market benchmark were treated as conferring a benefit. Both sampled groups were assessed as having a credit quality equivalent to a BB rating (non-investment grade) (recitals 306, 334). To construct a market benchmark, the Commission used US corporate bond yields for BB-rated issuers, adjusted by the spread between US and Chinese government bond yields.

The resulting preferential lending benefit was: CNBM Group 7.39% ad valorem; Yuntianhua Group 2.53% ad valorem (recital 344).

Grants and subsidies
Special purpose funds and industrial development grants from central and local governments, found to confer countervailable benefits to both sampled groups.
Inputs at LTAR
Electricity, natural gas, and other energy inputs provided at less than adequate remuneration. Land-use rights also provided at LTAR.
Tax preferences
Income tax exemptions and reductions under China's Enterprise Income Tax Law, including the 15% reduced rate for high-technology enterprises. Import duty exemptions on capital equipment.

Double-counting avoidance

Because the parallel anti-dumping investigation used the Article 2(6a) methodology to construct normal value (on the basis that domestic PRC prices are distorted), a risk of double-counting benefits existed. Under Articles 15(2) and 24(1) of Regulation (EU) 2016/1037, the Commission ensured that no subsidy benefit was counted twice: where a subsidy had been netted out from the cost of production in the anti-dumping constructed value, the same benefit was not credited again in the CVD calculation (recital 1122).

Subsidies countervailed: Egypt and the SETC-Zone doctrine

The landmark cross-border attribution of Chinese state subsidies to production on Egyptian soil

What is the SETC-Zone?

The Suez Economic and Trade Cooperation Zone (SETC-Zone) is a special economic zone established under Egyptian Law 83/2002 (the Egyptian Law on Economic Zones of Special Nature). It is located near Port Said in Egypt, adjacent to the Suez Canal. The zone covers 7.34 km2, comprising a starting area of 1.34 km2 and an expansion of 6 km2 (recital 647).

The zone was created pursuant to a Memorandum of Understanding signed on 18 April 1997 between China and Egypt (recital 648). The developer and operator is China-Africa TEDA Investment Co. Ltd, a joint venture between TEDA Investment Holding Co. Ltd (a Chinese state-controlled company based in Tianjin) and others. China-Africa TEDA holds 90% of the SETC-Zone management company, with 10% held by the Egyptian government (recital 654).

The Cooperation Agreement: Articles 4 and 5

A key instrument in the legal analysis was the Cooperation Agreement signed on 21 January 2016 between China's Ministry of Commerce (MOFCOM) and Egypt's General Authority for the Suez Canal Economic Zone (recital 656). Two provisions were central:

  • Article 4 states that the zone is entitled to "relevant policy support and facilitation" from the Chinese government (recital 676).
  • Article 5 states that the Government of China "shall support" the zone by "encouraging relevant financial institutions to provide financial facility" to companies operating there (recital 676).

These provisions, combined with the zone's ownership structure and operational arrangements, formed the legal bridge allowing the Commission to treat Chinese government financing channelled to SETC-Zone companies as countervailable subsidies attributed to Egypt as the country of export.

ILC Article 11: Acknowledgement and adoption

The Commission invoked the Articles on the Responsibility of States for Internationally Wrongful Acts (ILC Articles), specifically Article 11, which provides that conduct not otherwise attributable to a state is nonetheless attributed to that state if the state acknowledges and adopts the conduct as its own. The Commission found that both the Government of China and the Government of Egypt acknowledged and adopted the subsidy arrangements within the SETC-Zone through their active participation in establishing, operating, and formalising the zone's legal framework (recital 676 and surrounding analysis).

This invocation of public international law (ILC Articles) within an EU trade defence proceeding was a novel development in EU practice at the time of adoption.

Chinese government benefits to SETC-Zone
  • Preferential financing channelled through Chinese state-owned banks and policy banks, consistent with Article 5 of the Cooperation Agreement
  • Other support measures flowing from the "relevant policy support and facilitation" commitment in Article 4 of the Cooperation Agreement
Egyptian government benefits to SETC-Zone
  • Land within the SETC-Zone at less than adequate remuneration. The initial land allocation was made at less than USD 1 per square metre (recital 818), using the legal basis of Article 38(bis A) of Law 83/2002 as amended in 2015 (recital 818 footnote)
  • Other benefits under Law 83/2002, including tax exemptions and reduced charges applicable to zone-based manufacturers
Egypt subsidy rate: 10.9% (recital 934)

Both Egyptian producers, Jushi Egypt and Hengshi Egypt, are subsidiaries of the CNBM Group. Their combined subsidy rate was determined at 10.9% ad valorem (recital 934). Because both companies together account for 100% of Egyptian GFF exports to the Union, a single rate applied to all Egyptian producers.

Definitive duty table

CVD rates as set out in Article 1(2) of the Regulation

No lesser duty rule

Regulation (EU) 2018/825 removed the obligation to apply the lesser duty rule in anti-subsidy proceedings (i.e. to cap duties at the lower of the subsidy margin and the injury elimination level). Accordingly, CVDs in this case are set at the full subsidy rate without reference to an injury margin (recital 1115).

Injury, causation, and Union interest

Key numbers from the Union industry injury analysis

Market share deterioration

Union consumption grew from 153,287 tonnes in 2015 to 168,270 tonnes in the IP (+9.8%) (Table 2, recital 940). Despite growing demand, the Union industry's market share fell from 73.7% in 2015 to 63.4% in the IP, a loss of 10.3 percentage points (Table 6, recital 994).

Combined import market share of the two countries concerned rose from 22.6% in 2015 to 31.4% in the IP (+8.8 percentage points) (Table 3, recital 949). The increase was particularly sharp for Egypt: Egyptian exports grew from 108 tonnes in 2015 to 15,334 tonnes in the IP, reflecting the ramp-up of SETC-Zone production capacity (Table 3).

Price-cost squeeze and financial deterioration

Union ex-works selling prices fell from EUR 2.20/kg in 2015 to EUR 2.05/kg in the IP (-7%), while unit cost of production rose from EUR 2.18/kg to EUR 2.25/kg (+3%) (Table 8, recital 1001). This price-cost squeeze drove a severe deterioration in financial performance:

  • Profitability: +2.5% in 2015 to -3.4% in the IP (Table 11)
  • Return on investment: +15% in 2015 to -17% in the IP (Table 11)
  • Cash flow: EUR 8,928,397 in 2015 to EUR 1,257,171 in the IP, a collapse of approximately 86% (Table 11)
Price undercutting margins

Using PCN-matched comparisons, the Commission calculated the following undercutting margins (recital 966):

  • PRC: range 15.4% to 55.8%, weighted average 32.3%
  • Egypt: range 30.5% to 54.9%, weighted average 31.5%

Imports from both origins declined in price over the period. Chinese average import price fell from EUR 1.78/kg in 2015 to EUR 1.63/kg in the IP (-8.4%); Egyptian prices were consistently below Chinese prices at EUR 1.36/kg in 2015 and EUR 1.32/kg in the IP (Table 4).

Union interest and wind energy context

Under Article 31 of the Basic Regulation, the Commission assessed whether imposing duties was in the broader Union interest. The kitting sector (downstream GFF processors for wind turbine blades and nacelles) employed more than 2,000 persons in the Union (recital 1106). Wind turbine manufacturers argued duties would harm the EU's wind energy programme.

The Commission was not persuaded, finding that GFF represents only 0.1% to 2% of total wind park costs (recital 1087). Even full duty pass-through would have a very limited impact on wind park economics. Union interest did not preclude the imposition of definitive CVDs (recital 1115).

Causation: key link

Imports from China and Egypt were assessed cumulatively under Article 8(3) of the Basic Regulation. The Commission found a direct causal link between the subsidised imports and the material injury. The combined market share of subsidised imports rose by 8.8 percentage points while the Union industry's market share fell by 10.3 percentage points (recital 949, recital 994). Other potential causes (imports from India growing from 0.7% to 1.8% of Union consumption; imports from Russia at around 1.4% stable; demand conditions; raw material costs) were examined and found not capable of breaking the causal link with subsidised imports.

Why this case is a landmark

The SETC-Zone doctrine and its significance for EU trade defence practice

Cross-border subsidy attribution: a novel doctrine

Before this regulation, EU anti-subsidy practice operated on the assumption that subsidies are attributable to the country of export on the basis of benefits provided by that country's government. The SETC-Zone raised a structurally different situation: a Chinese state-controlled operator managing a special economic zone on Egyptian soil, channelling Chinese government financing to Chinese-owned factories while the product was legally exported from Egypt.

The Commission's response was to apply ILC Article 11 (acknowledgement and adoption) to attribute Chinese state conduct to the subsidy analysis for Egyptian exports. By demonstrating that both the Government of China and the Government of Egypt had acknowledged and adopted the subsidy arrangements through the Cooperation Agreement, Egyptian Law 83/2002, and the zone's operational framework, the Commission treated benefits flowing from both governments as countervailable for the purposes of Egyptian exports (recital 676 and surrounding analysis).

This approach addressed a structural feature of Chinese overseas investment in state-managed zones: the production is geographically located outside China, but the capital, management, and state support flow from Chinese state entities. Treating the country-of-production label as dispositive would have allowed circumvention of anti-subsidy measures simply by relocating production to a Chinese-managed zone abroad.

Subsequent influence

The cross-border attribution doctrine established in this regulation has been applied in subsequent EU trade defence investigations involving Chinese-managed special economic zones and related structures. The legal template, centred on the Cooperation Agreement instruments and ILC Article 11 acknowledgement and adoption, provides a replicable framework for situations where Chinese state capital and management are present in third-country production facilities that supply the EU market.

Sister case -- anti_subsidy_trade_remedies
Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) 2024/2754: Definitive Countervailing Duties on Chinese Battery Electric Vehicles

The BEV CVD regulation is the direct successor case to this GFF regulation within the anti-subsidy trade remedies family. Both proceedings apply the same Basic Anti-Subsidy Regulation framework (Regulation (EU) 2016/1037), follow the same investigation architecture (complaint by Union industry, Commission investigation, subsidy findings, injury analysis, Union interest test, definitive duty table), and apply the no lesser duty rule introduced by Regulation (EU) 2018/825.

GFF (Reg 2020/776) is the 2020 precedent that established the cross-border subsidy attribution doctrine via the SETC-Zone, using ILC Article 11 acknowledgement and adoption principles. The BEV case (Reg 2024/2754) is the 2024 application of the same Basic Regulation to a far higher-profile sector, with five undertaking offers rejected and duties ranging from BYD 17.0% to SAIC 35.3%. Reading the two cases together illustrates both the continuity of the legal framework and how the doctrine has been extended.

Successor case -- anti_subsidy_trade_remedies
Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) 2025/500: Countervailing Duties on Aluminium Road Wheels from Morocco

Countervailing duties on Moroccan aluminium wheels; the cross-border subsidy doctrine applied to Chinese Belt-and-Road financing. The 2025 regulation extends the attribution logic pioneered in this GFF case to the automotive components sector, finding that Chinese state financing channelled to Moroccan producers constituted countervailable subsidies under the same Basic Anti-Subsidy Regulation framework (Regulation (EU) 2016/1037).

Regulation (EU) 2022/433: India and Indonesia Stainless Steel Countervailing Duties

Same legal family. The 2022 anti-subsidy case on stainless steel cold-rolled flat products from India and Indonesia. It applied the same Basic Anti-Subsidy Regulation (Reg (EU) 2016/1037) and extended the cross-border subsidy doctrine to Chinese financing channelled to Indonesian producers through the Morowali Industrial Park. Countervailing duties range from 4.3 % to 21.4 %, reaching 42.8 % combined with anti-dumping.

Where it sits in EU trade-defence law

The legislative instruments that frame Regulation (EU) 2020/776

Regulation (EU) 2016/1037 (Basic Anti-Subsidy Regulation)
The legal basis and substantive rules throughout. Articles 15 and 24(1) are cited in the operative provisions. Article 28 governs the use of facts available where cooperation is withheld. Article 31 governs the Union interest test.
Regulation (EU) 2018/825 (no lesser duty amendment)
Amended Regulation (EU) 2016/1037 to remove the obligation to apply the lesser duty rule in anti-subsidy proceedings. This directly determines the duty levels in this case: duties are set at the full subsidy rate without an injury margin cap (recital 1115).
Regulation (EU) 2020/492 (parallel AD regulation)
Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) 2020/492 of 1 April 2020 imposed definitive anti-dumping duties on the same GFF product from the same countries. This CVD Regulation amends it via Article 2 to insert TARIC codes and update the combined duty table to eliminate double-counting.
Regulation (EU) 2020/44 (registration regulation)
Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) 2020/44 of 22 January 2020 made imports subject to registration pending the investigation outcome (recital 74). Article 3 of this CVD Regulation discontinues that registration. Because no provisional measures were imposed, the registration had no retroactive collection effect.
Regulation (EU) 2016/1036 (Basic AD Regulation)
The parallel anti-dumping investigation used Article 2(6a) to construct normal value on the basis that PRC domestic prices are distorted. This methodology affected the double-counting analysis between the two proceedings.
Egyptian Law 83/2002 and ILC Articles
Egyptian Law 83/2002 (Law on Economic Zones of Special Nature) and its 2015 amendment (Article 38(bis A)) form the domestic Egyptian legal basis for the SETC-Zone. The Commission's attribution doctrine also drew on ILC Article 11 (acknowledgement and adoption) as a public international law principle.

Glossary

Key terms as used in Regulation (EU) 2020/776

CVD
Countervailing duty (anti-subsidy duty). An import duty applied to offset subsidies granted by the government of the exporting country to its producers.
GFF
Woven and/or stitched glass fibre fabrics; the product under investigation. Key input in wind turbine blades and other composite structures.
SETC-Zone
China-Egypt Suez Economic and Trade Cooperation Zone. Special economic zone near Port Said, 7.34 km2, developed by China-Africa TEDA (90% Chinese state-controlled). At the centre of the cross-border subsidy attribution doctrine.
CNBM Group
China National Building Material Group Co. Ltd and affiliates. State-owned, controlled by SASAC. Received a CVD rate of 30.7% (China) via TARIC code C531. Parent of both Egyptian producers.
Yuntianhua Group
Group comprising Yunnan Yuntianhua Co. Ltd, CPIC, PGTEX, and CTM. Received a CVD rate of 17.0% via TARIC code C532.
LTAR
Less than adequate remuneration. The standard for assessing whether goods or services (electricity, land, energy) are provided by government at below-market prices, conferring a countervailable benefit.
ILC Articles
Articles on the Responsibility of States for Internationally Wrongful Acts (International Law Commission, 2001). Article 11 (acknowledgement and adoption) was the public international law basis for attributing Chinese state conduct to Egypt.
IP
Investigation period. 1 January 2018 to 31 December 2018. The period for which subsidy rates and injury indicators are calculated (recital 77).
SASAC
State-owned Assets Supervision and Administration Commission of the State Council (PRC). The state body that controls CNBM.
Tech-Fab Europe
The European association of glass fibre fabric producers that lodged the complaint on 1 April 2019, triggering the investigation.
