DPP harmonised standards: six EN standards that turn the ESPR's passport rules into a testable specification

Commission Implementing Decision (EU) 2026/1736 publishes, in the Official Journal, the references of six harmonised standards that CEN, Cenelec and ETSI drafted for digital product passports at the Commission's request. Conformity with these standards, on identifiers, data carriers, exchange protocols, storage and persistence, APIs and system interoperability, gives manufacturers a legal presumption of conformity with Articles 10 and 11 of the ESPR, Regulation (EU) 2024/1781. It is a short, two-article Decision, but it is the technical layer that makes the digital product passport buildable rather than abstract.

Overview

What the DPP harmonised standards Decision is, why it exists, and how it is structured

A short Decision that publishes six technical standards

Commission Implementing Decision (EU) 2026/1736 does one thing: it publishes, in the Official Journal, the references of six harmonised EN standards for digital product passports. These are not new legal obligations. They are the technical specification, drafted by Europe's standardisation bodies, that gives manufacturers a concrete, testable way to meet the data requirements the ESPR already sets out in its Articles 10 and 11. The Decision itself is short: two Articles, no chapters, and a single Annex listing the six standards.

A Commission act, adopted under the Standardisation Regulation

This is a Commission Implementing Decision, adopted by the Commission alone at Brussels on 14 July 2026 and signed by Commission President Ursula von der Leyen, published in OJ L, 2026/1736 on 15 July 2026. Its legal basis is Article 10(6) of Regulation (EU) No 1025/2012, the Standardisation Regulation, read together with Article 41(2) of the ESPR, Regulation (EU) 2024/1781.

One date, not two: adoption and entry into force are close but distinct

The Decision was adopted at Brussels on 14 July 2026. It entered into force the next day, 15 July 2026, the day of its OJ publication (Article 2). Recital 6 explains why: compliance with a harmonised standard confers a presumption of conformity from the date its reference is published in the Official Journal, so the Decision is deliberately drafted to take legal effect immediately on publication rather than after the usual twenty-day delay.

The technical layer beneath the DPP legal architecture

Brubru tracks 13 acts in the EU Digital Product Passport legal architecture: the ESPR itself, the DPP registry Implementing Regulation, product-specific laws such as the Batteries, Construction Products, Toy Safety and Detergents Regulations, and now this Decision, the standards layer that sits underneath all of them for products covered by the ESPR. It is the piece that turns "the passport must carry certain data" into "here is exactly how that data is structured, carried, exchanged, stored and made searchable".

One standard per functional layer

Read together, the six standards cover the full technical stack a digital product passport needs: how it is uniquely identified (EN 18219), how it is physically or digitally carried (EN 18220), how its data is exchanged between systems (EN 18216), how that data is stored and kept accessible over time (EN 18221), how it is queried and managed through APIs across its lifecycle (EN 18222), and how different systems interoperate with each other (EN 18223). Recital 4 records that the Commission, together with CEN, Cenelec and ETSI, assessed and confirmed that all six comply with the original standardisation request.

What this Decision actually does (Articles 1 and 2)

Two articles, nothing more: this is a publication act, not a rulemaking act

Article 1: the references are published

Article 1 states that the references of the six harmonised standards for digital product passports, drafted in support of Regulation (EU) 2024/1781 and listed in the Annex, are hereby published in the Official Journal of the European Union. That is the entire operative substance of the Decision: it does not create new obligations, it publishes the references that trigger the presumption of conformity mechanism already built into the ESPR.

Article 2: entry into force on the day of publication

Article 2 provides that the Decision enters into force on the day of its publication in the Official Journal of the European Union, that is, 15 July 2026. There is no delayed application, no transition period and no further implementing measure attached to this Decision. Its only legal effect is to make the six EN standards references official from that date onward.

What this Decision does not contain

This is deliberately a narrow, two-article publication Decision. It does not set new deadlines, does not impose Member State obligations, does not create penalties, and does not itself describe the technical content of the six standards beyond their titles in the Annex, the substantive technical requirements live inside the EN standards themselves, published by CEN, Cenelec and ETSI, not inside this Decision's text.

The presumption of conformity mechanism

Why publishing a standard's reference in the OJ matters legally, explained via the ESPR and the Standardisation Regulation

The mechanism lives in the ESPR, not in this Decision

Under Article 41(2) of the ESPR, Regulation (EU) 2024/1781, digital product passports that conform to harmonised standards, or parts of them, whose references have been published in the Official Journal are presumed to conform with the requirements in Articles 10 and 11 of that Regulation covered by those standards. This Decision does not create that presumption mechanism; the ESPR does. What this Decision does is trigger it, by publishing the six standard references that make the presumption available for the first time.

The standardisation request behind the six standards

The standards were not drafted freely by CEN, Cenelec and ETSI on their own initiative. The Commission issued a formal standardisation request, Implementing Decision C(2024) 5423 of 31 July 2024, later amended by Implementing Decision C(2025) 8024 of 28 November 2025, asking the three European standardisation organisations to draft harmonised standards in support of the ESPR's Article 10 and Article 11 requirements. Recital 5 confirms the resulting six standards satisfy those requirements, which is why the Commission judged it appropriate to publish their references.

The practical effect for manufacturers

For a manufacturer, importer or economic operator building a digital product passport for a product covered by the ESPR, building to these six EN standards is now the fast lane: conformity with them gives a legal presumption that the corresponding parts of Articles 10 and 11 are met, without having to demonstrate compliance requirement by requirement from first principles. That presumption is rebuttable, and it only covers the parts of Articles 10 and 11 that the relevant standard actually addresses, but it materially lowers the compliance burden for anyone who adopts the standards.

Where it sits in the DPP stack

The standards layer, underneath the registry, underneath the product-specific laws

Directly tied to the ESPR, Articles 10 and 11

These six standards are drafted specifically in support of Regulation (EU) 2024/1781, the ESPR, and the data requirements it sets in Articles 10 and 11. They apply to digital product passports covered by that Regulation. This Decision does not, on its own text, extend the six standards' presumption of conformity to other product laws.

A companion piece to the DPP registry

Separately, Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) 2026/1778 operationalises the single EU-wide digital product passport registry the ESPR created in its Article 13, the system every passport's unique identifier is ultimately registered in. That Regulation already extends its own registration arrangements beyond ESPR products to batteries, construction products, toys, and detergents and end-user surfactants. This Decision's six standards, and the registry's registration rulebook, are two distinct but complementary layers of the same digital product passport architecture: one gives manufacturers a technical specification and a presumption of conformity, the other gives every passport a shared registration and identity infrastructure.

Other sectoral DPP laws keep their own compliance routes

Product-specific laws such as the Batteries Regulation (EU) 2023/1542, the Construction Products Regulation (EU) 2024/3110, the Toy Safety Regulation (EU) 2025/2509 and the Detergents Regulation (EU) 2026/405 each build their own digital product passport obligations, and each plugs its passport's unique identifiers into the same central registry. Whether or how future harmonised standards for those sectoral passports are drafted and published is a separate exercise from this Decision, which concerns ESPR conformity only.

Glossary

Load-bearing terms as used in Commission Implementing Decision (EU) 2026/1736

Harmonised standard
A standard drafted by a European standardisation organisation, CEN, Cenelec or ETSI, on the basis of a Commission request, whose reference publication in the Official Journal triggers a presumption of conformity with the corresponding legal requirements (recital 6).
Presumption of conformity
The legal effect, set out in Article 41(2) of the ESPR, whereby a digital product passport that conforms to a published harmonised standard is presumed to meet the corresponding ESPR requirements the standard covers.
Standardisation request
The formal instruction the Commission issues to CEN, Cenelec and ETSI asking them to draft harmonised standards on a given topic; here, Implementing Decision C(2024) 5423, as amended by C(2025) 8024.
CEN, Cenelec, ETSI
The European Committee for Standardisation, the European Committee for Electrotechnical Standardisation, and the European Telecommunications Standards Institute, the three bodies recognised under Regulation (EU) No 1025/2012 to draft European harmonised standards.
EN reference
The formal identifier of a European standard, such as EN 18216:2026, giving the standard number and the year it was finalised.
ESPR
Regulation (EU) 2024/1781, the Ecodesign for Sustainable Products Regulation, which sets the digital product passport data requirements in its Articles 10 and 11 that these six standards give a presumption of conformity against.
