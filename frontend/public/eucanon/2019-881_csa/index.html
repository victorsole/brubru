The Cybersecurity Act

Regulation (EU) 2019/881 does two things: it makes ENISA, the EU Agency for Cybersecurity, a permanent agency, and it creates one European framework for certifying ICT products, services, processes and, since 2025, managed security services. Certification is voluntary unless other law says otherwise. A proposed Cybersecurity Act 2 would repeal it, but until that is adopted this Act, as amended, stays the law.

Overview

What it is, what it does, and what it leaves to other laws

What it is

Regulation (EU) 2019/881 of the European Parliament and of the Council of 17 April 2019 is formally titled "on ENISA (the European Union Agency for Cybersecurity) and on information and communications technology cybersecurity certification and repealing Regulation (EU) No 526/2013 (Cybersecurity Act)". It was published in the Official Journal (L 151) on 7 June 2019 and entered into force on 27 June 2019, the twentieth day after publication. Its legal basis is Article 114 of the Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union.

It has two halves. Title II (Articles 3 to 45) turns ENISA into a permanent agency. Title III (Articles 46 to 65) creates the European cybersecurity certification framework. Title IV (Articles 66 to 69) and an Annex of requirements for conformity assessment bodies close the text.

What it does for certification

It does not certify anything itself. It sets up a mechanism: the Commission can adopt European cybersecurity certification schemes, on the basis of a candidate scheme prepared by ENISA, for a given kind of product, service, process or managed security service. A certificate issued under such a scheme is valid in every Member State (Article 56(10)). The Act is the toolbox, and each scheme is a separate implementing act.

Not a product-security law

The Cybersecurity Act creates no obligations on manufacturers by itself. Certification is voluntary by default (Article 56(2)). A duty to certify exists only where another Union act or a Member State law says so. The binding product duties sit in the Cyber Resilience Act, Regulation (EU) 2024/2847, which can point to European certificates as proof of conformity.

Procedural history and amendments

The original procedure is 2017/0225(COD). The Act has been amended once, by Regulation (EU) 2025/37 of 19 December 2024, which added managed security services. A consolidated text of 4 February 2025 is available on EUR-Lex under CELEX 02019R0881-20250204. On 20 January 2026 the Commission proposed a new Cybersecurity Act that would repeal this Regulation. It is still under negotiation.

What it covers

Five areas of the Regulation, in one view

ENISA made permanent
The EU Agency for Cybersecurity is established for an indefinite period as of 27 June 2019, with tasks in Articles 5 to 12 and a governance structure in Articles 13 to 23.
One EU certification framework
A single mechanism to create European schemes for ICT products, services, processes and managed security services, valid in all Member States (Articles 46 to 57).
Three assurance levels
Basic, substantial and high, each with minimum evaluation activities. Self-assessment is allowed only at basic (Articles 52 and 53).
National authorities, peer review and the ECCG
Each Member State designates certification authorities, which face peer review at least every five years. The ECCG brings them together (Articles 58 to 65).
Managed security services added in 2025
Regulation (EU) 2025/37 brings services such as incident handling and penetration testing into the framework, with their own objectives in Article 51a.

Scope and limits

Articles 1, 2 and 56: a voluntary framework that leaves national security to the Member States

What it does
  • Sets ENISA's objectives, tasks and organisation.
  • Provides a framework for European certification schemes, applying without prejudice to specific provisions in other Union acts on voluntary or mandatory certification (Article 1(1)).
  • Makes certification voluntary by default (Article 56(2)), and makes issuing an EU statement of conformity voluntary too (Article 53(4)).
What it does not do
  • It creates no duties for manufacturers or providers by itself.
  • Under Article 1(2), it is without prejudice to Member State competence on public security, defence, national security and the activities of the State in areas of criminal law.
  • It does not itself make any scheme mandatory. The Commission only assesses whether a scheme should become mandatory through other Union law (Article 56(3)).
ICT product

An element or a group of elements of a network or information system (Article 2(12)).

ICT service

A service consisting fully or mainly in the transmission, storing, retrieving or processing of information by means of network and information systems (Article 2(13)).

ICT process

A set of activities performed to design, develop, deliver or maintain an ICT product or ICT service (Article 2(14)).

Managed security service (Article 2, point 14a)

"A service provided to a third party consisting of carrying out, or providing assistance for, activities relating to cybersecurity risk management, such as incident handling, penetration testing, security audits and consulting, including expert advice, related to technical support."

ENISA

Title II, Articles 3 to 45: a permanent agency with a defined mandate and governance

Mandate

ENISA is the Union's reference point for advice and expertise on cybersecurity (Articles 3 and 4). It acts independently and avoids duplicating Member State activity. Article 68(4) establishes it for an indefinite period as of 27 June 2019, and it succeeds the agency created under Regulation (EU) No 526/2013. Its seat is in Greece: recital 18 records that Member State representatives decided on a town in Greece, and Article 44 provides for a headquarters agreement with the host Member State.

Tasks in Articles 5 to 12

Policy and law (Article 5)

Assisting and advising on the development and review of Union policy and law on cybersecurity.

Capacity-building (Article 6)

Supporting Member States and Union bodies, including on vulnerability disclosure policies and national CSIRTs.

Operational cooperation (Article 7)

The secretariat of the CSIRTs network, a regular EU Cybersecurity Technical Situation Report, and a large-scale exercise every two years.

Market, certification, standards (Article 8)

Preparing candidate schemes, providing the secretariat of the Stakeholder Cybersecurity Certification Group and assisting the ECCG secretariat.

Knowledge, awareness, research (Articles 9 to 11)

Information and knowledge, awareness and education, and research and innovation.

International cooperation (Article 12)

Including advice on mutual recognition of certificates with third countries.

The Management Board decides by a majority of its members (Article 18(1)). A two-thirds majority is needed for the single programming document, the annual budget, and the appointment, extension or removal of the Executive Director (Article 18(2)).

Local offices (Article 20(5))

The Executive Director may open local offices, with the prior consent of the Commission and the Management Board. All local offices together stay at or below 40% of ENISA staff in the seat Member State, and each office at or below 10%.

How a scheme is made

Articles 47 to 49a: from the rolling work programme to an implementing act, then review

  1. Union rolling work programme (Article 47). The Commission publishes strategic priorities and a list of products, services, processes and managed security services that could benefit from a scheme. The first was due by 28 June 2020, and it is updated at least every three years.
  2. Request (Article 48). The Commission may ask ENISA for a candidate scheme on the basis of the programme. In duly justified cases the Commission or the ECCG may ask for one that is not in the programme.
  3. Candidate scheme (Article 49). ENISA prepares it after a formal, open, transparent and inclusive consultation, with an ad hoc working group for specific advice.
  4. ECCG opinion (Article 49(5) and (6)). The ECCG adopts an opinion. ENISA takes utmost account of it, but it is not binding.
  5. Implementing act (Article 49(7)). The Commission may adopt the scheme by implementing act under the examination procedure.
  6. Evaluation every five years (Article 49(8)). ENISA evaluates each adopted scheme at least every five years. The Commission or the ECCG may then ask for a revised candidate scheme.
  7. Parliamentary dialogue (Article 49a). The Commission makes its requests public. The European Parliament and the Council may ask for quarterly information on a draft candidate scheme and may invite the Commission and ENISA to discuss how schemes function.
What a scheme contains (Article 54)

At least 22 elements, from points (a) to (v) of Article 54(1). They run from the subject matter and scope, through the standards and evaluation methods, to rules on validity periods, vulnerability reporting and mutual recognition with third countries.

Security objectives (Articles 51 and 51a)

A scheme for products, services or processes aims at ten objectives, including that they are secure by default and by design and free of known vulnerabilities. A scheme for managed security services aims at seven, including staff competence and professional integrity.

Assurance levels

Self-assessment (Article 53) Allowed, only for low-risk items, if the scheme permits it. Not allowed Not allowed
EU statement of conformity (Article 53)

Where a scheme allows self-assessment, the manufacturer or provider may issue an EU statement of conformity and then assumes responsibility for compliance. The documentation goes to the national authority for the period the scheme sets. Issuing the statement is voluntary unless Union or Member State law says otherwise (Article 53(4)).

What an assurance level is not

Article 2(21) says an assurance level indicates the level at which an item has been evaluated, but "as such does not measure the security" of that item. Certified items are presumed to comply with the scheme (Article 56(1)), and certificate holders must report vulnerabilities they detect later (Article 56(8)).

Authorities and bodies

Articles 58 to 65: who supervises certification, who issues certificates, and what remedies exist

National authorities (Article 58)

Each Member State designates one or more national cybersecurity certification authorities, independent of the entities they supervise in organisation, funding, legal structure and decision-making. Under Article 58(8) each has at least the power to request information, carry out audits, take measures to ensure compliance, access premises, withdraw certificates and impose penalties under national law.

Peer review (Article 59)

National authorities are peer reviewed by at least two authorities from other Member States and the Commission, at least once every five years. ENISA may take part. The ECCG examines the outcomes and may issue guidelines or recommendations.

Conformity assessment bodies (Article 60 and Annex)

They must be accredited by a national accreditation body, for a maximum of five years, and meet the 20 requirements of the Annex, such as independence from what they assess and impartiality.

Notification (Article 61)

National authorities notify the Commission of accredited bodies for each scheme. One year after a scheme enters into force, the Commission publishes the list of notified bodies in the Official Journal.

The ECCG (Article 62)

The European Cybersecurity Certification Group is made up of representatives of national certification authorities, chaired by the Commission with ENISA's help. A member may not represent more than two Member States. It advises the Commission and adopts opinions on candidate schemes.

Complaints, remedies, penalties (Articles 63 to 65)

Natural and legal persons may lodge a complaint with the issuer of a certificate (Article 63) and have a right to an effective judicial remedy (Article 64). Member States set penalties, which must be "effective, proportionate and dissuasive" (Article 65).

Application date of Articles 58, 60, 61, 63, 64 and 65

These six articles on authorities, conformity assessment bodies, notification, complaints, remedies and penalties apply from 28 June 2021 (Article 69(2)). The rest of the Regulation applies from entry into force on 27 June 2019.

The first scheme: EUCC

Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) 2024/482, the first scheme adopted under Title III

What it is

The EUCC, the European Common Criteria-based cybersecurity certification scheme, was adopted by Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) 2024/482 of 31 January 2024 and published in the Official Journal on 7 February 2024. It is the first European certification scheme adopted under the Cybersecurity Act, as ENISA announced on 31 January 2024. It builds on the SOG-IS Mutual Recognition Agreement of Common Criteria certificates, as its recitals explain.

When it applies

It applies from 27 February 2025. National schemes that it covers cease to produce effects 12 months after its entry into force, under Article 49 of the Implementing Regulation. This follows Article 57 of the Cybersecurity Act, under which national schemes cease to produce effects from the date set in the implementing act, and existing national certificates stay valid until they expire.

This page does not describe the content or the status of any other scheme.

Managed security services

Regulation (EU) 2025/37: the only amendment to the Act so far

The amending act

Regulation (EU) 2025/37 "amending Regulation (EU) 2019/881 as regards managed security services" is dated 19 December 2024, was published in the Official Journal on 15 January 2025 and entered into force on 4 February 2025. It is built on Article 114 TFEU, like the original.

What it added
  • A definition of managed security service (Article 2, point 14a).
  • Article 51a, with seven security objectives for managed security services, covering staff competence and integrity, quality procedures, data protection, restoration after incidents, access control, record keeping and secure tools.
  • Article 49a, on information and consultation of Parliament and the Council about schemes.
What it changed
  • The wording of Articles 46 to 59 now covers managed security services alongside products, services and processes.
  • The Article 67 evaluation now also assesses Title III and whether essential cybersecurity requirements for market access are needed.
  • The Annex, with the requirements for conformity assessment bodies, was amended.

Link to the Cyber Solidarity Act (context only)

Recitals 5 and 6 of Regulation 2025/37 explain that certifying managed security services could play a role in selecting trusted providers for the EU Cybersecurity Reserve established by Regulation (EU) 2025/38, and that it can also serve as a quality indicator for private and public entities that buy such services. The Cybersecurity Act itself imposes no such selection rule.

How it fits with other acts

The Cybersecurity Act supplies certification and ENISA. Its neighbours set the duties

Instrument Reference Relationship
Cyber Resilience Act Regulation (EU) 2024/2847 Holds the binding product security duties. Under Article 54(3) of the Cybersecurity Act, where a specific Union act so provides, a certificate or EU statement of conformity can be used to show a presumption of conformity with that act. The CRA can point to European certificates in this way.
NIS 2 Directive Directive (EU) 2022/2555 Entity-level risk management and reporting. The Cybersecurity Act still cites Directive (EU) 2016/1148, which NIS 2 repealed from 18 October 2024. Under Article 44 of NIS 2, references to the old Directive are read as references to NIS 2.
Cyber Solidarity Act Regulation (EU) 2025/38 Creates the EU Cybersecurity Reserve. Certification of managed security services can inform the selection of trusted providers (recital 5 of Regulation 2025/37). A Brubru canon entry on it follows.

The proposed Cybersecurity Act 2

COM(2026)0011 would repeal Regulation 2019/881, but it is only a proposal

The law today

The Commission proposed a new Cybersecurity Act on 20 January 2026 as COM(2026)0011, procedure 2026/0011(COD). It would repeal Regulation (EU) 2019/881. Until it is adopted, the 2019 Act as amended by Regulation 2025/37 stays the law. Nothing on this page assumes when, or whether, the proposal will be adopted.

In Parliament

The lead committee is ITRE, with Markéta Gregorová (Greens/EFA) as rapporteur. IMCO has an opinion role and BUDG a budgetary assessment. On the Legislative Observatory the only committee draft report is PE792.222, dated 18 September 2026, and the stage reached is awaiting committee decision.

What the Commission lists
  • ENISA issuing early alerts of cyber threats and incidents.
  • Support, with Europol and CSIRTs, for responding to and recovering from ransomware.
  • A common Union vulnerability management service capacity.
  • Operation of the single entry point for incident reporting proposed in the Digital Omnibus.
  • A trusted ICT supply chain security framework.
  • A renewed certification framework, with scheme development "within 12 months by default".

On the same day, 20 January 2026, the Commission also proposed targeted amendments to NIS 2. The list above follows the Commission's policy page and the Legislative Observatory. It is a description of a proposal, not legal text in force.

Key definition

The term at the centre of the framework

European cybersecurity certificate (Article 2(11))

"A document issued by a relevant body, attesting that a given ICT product, ICT service, ICT process or managed security service has been evaluated for compliance with specific security requirements laid down in a European cybersecurity certification scheme."

A certificate is therefore tied to a scheme. Without an adopted European scheme covering an item, there is no European certificate for it.

Glossary

Terms used on this page

ENISA
The European Union Agency for Cybersecurity, established for an indefinite period as of 27 June 2019 (Article 68(4)).
European cybersecurity certification scheme
A comprehensive set of rules, technical requirements, standards and procedures established at Union level for specific items (Article 2(9)).
European cybersecurity certificate
A document attesting that an item has been evaluated against the requirements of a European scheme (Article 2(11)).
Assurance level
Basic, substantial or high. Indicates how deeply an item was evaluated, not how secure it is (Article 2(21)).
Conformity self-assessment
An evaluation carried out by the manufacturer or provider itself. Permitted only at assurance level basic (Article 53).
EU statement of conformity
A statement by the manufacturer or provider that a scheme's requirements are met. Issuing it is voluntary unless law says otherwise.
ECCG
The European Cybersecurity Certification Group of national authority representatives, chaired by the Commission (Article 62).
National cybersecurity certification authority
The independent authority each Member State designates to supervise certification (Article 58).
Conformity assessment body
An accredited third-party body that evaluates items and issues certificates at basic or substantial level (Article 60 and Annex).
Union rolling work programme
The Commission's list of strategic priorities for future schemes, updated at least every three years (Article 47).
Managed security service
A service such as incident handling, penetration testing, security audits or consulting, provided to a third party (Article 2, point 14a).
EUCC
The European Common Criteria-based scheme, the first adopted under the Act, by Implementing Regulation (EU) 2024/482.
Peer review
The review of national certification authorities by authorities of other Member States and the Commission, at least every five years (Article 59).
Presumption of conformity
Where a Union act so provides, a certificate under a European scheme can be used to show conformity with that act (Article 54(3)).
