ESPR: the framework law behind the EU's Digital Product Passport

Regulation (EU) 2024/1781 sets no ecodesign requirement itself. It empowers the Commission to set binding performance and information requirements product group by product group, by delegated act, while creating four things directly: the Digital Product Passport, a ban on destroying unsold consumer products, mandatory green public procurement, and a working plan that already names eleven priority sectors.

Overview

What ESPR is, why it exists, and how it is structured

An empowering framework, not a requirements catalogue

The Ecodesign for Sustainable Products Regulation is the framework law through which the EU intends to make sustainable products the norm across nearly the entire single market. It replaces the 2009 Ecodesign Directive, which only ever covered energy-related products such as boilers, washing machines and light bulbs, and extends the same delegated-act mechanism to almost any physical good placed on the EU market or put into service.

ESPR sets no product-specific requirement itself. Article 4 gives the Commission the power to adopt delegated acts fixing binding ecodesign requirements, performance requirements, information requirements, or both, for a defined product group, following a mandatory process of impact assessment, consultation and prioritisation. Four headline deliverables are created directly by the operative text rather than deferred to delegated acts: the Digital Product Passport, the destruction ban on unsold consumer products, mandatory green public procurement, and the statutory working plan.

From the Green Deal to a binding Regulation

ESPR sits inside the European Green Deal and the Circular Economy Action Plan of March 2020, which committed to a sustainable products policy framework. The Commission proposed COM(2022) 142 on 30 March 2022, OEIL 2022/0095(COD). The European Parliament adopted its position on 23 April 2024, the Council took its decision on 27 May 2024, and the Regulation was signed at Brussels on 13 June 2024 by President Metsola for Parliament and Minister Lahbib for the Council.

It was published in the Official Journal on 28 June 2024, as OJ L, 2024/1781 (78 pages), and entered into force on 18 July 2024, 20 days after publication, per Article 80. Unlike a directive, it is directly applicable from that date, but the substantive ecodesign requirements only bind once product-specific delegated acts are adopted, and the first such act could not enter into force before 19 July 2025.

What ESPR repeals and amends

ESPR repeals Directive 2009/125/EC, the original Ecodesign Directive, with effect from 18 July 2024, subject to transitional carve-outs: a named list of energy-related product categories stays governed by existing implementing measures until 31 December 2026, and products under other existing implementing measures continue, for technical-update purposes only, until 31 December 2030.

It amends Directive (EU) 2020/1828, the Representative Actions Directive, adding ESPR to the list of instruments covered by collective redress (Art 77), and amends Regulation (EU) 2023/1542, the Batteries Regulation, adding a duty to upload the battery unique identifier to the ESPR registry (Art 78). Note that the OJ-published text of Articles 77 and 78 mistakenly cites the Regulation as (EU) 2024/1726, an error in the Official Journal itself; the correct, citable number throughout is Regulation (EU) 2024/1781.

Complements, not replaces, neighbouring EU law

ESPR complements the Packaging and Packaging Waste Regulation (EU) 2025/40, which covers packaging specifically since ESPR does not treat packaging as its own product category, and interacts closely with the Waste Framework Directive, the Batteries Regulation and the Critical Raw Materials Act. It combines a binding, EU-wide delegated-act mechanism with obligations that already apply directly on economic operators, the DPP market-access condition, the destruction ban, and green public procurement, well ahead of any single product-specific delegated act.

Chapter I: general provisions (Arts 1-3)

Subject matter, an unusually wide scope, and the internal-market pre-emption clause

Subject matter and scope (Arts 1-2)

Article 1(1) states ESPR's aim: making sustainable products the norm, reducing products' carbon and environmental footprint over their life cycle, and ensuring free movement of sustainable products in the internal market. Article 1(2) applies the Regulation to any physical goods placed on the market or put into service, including components and intermediate products, a dramatically broader reach than the 2009 Directive's energy-related-products focus. Digital content that is an integral part of a physical product falls inside scope.

Eight categories are excluded: food, feed, medicinal products for human use, veterinary medicinal products, living plants, animals and micro-organisms, products of human origin, products relating to future reproduction of plants and animals, and vehicles already covered by the agricultural, two/three-wheel and motor vehicle type-approval Regulations, but only for the aspects those regimes already cover. E-bikes and e-scooters are not excluded, and tyres are explicitly in scope as a first working-plan priority. Products serving only defence or national security purposes are excluded from any product group; second-hand products already circulating in the Union do not need to comply with delegated acts adopted after they were first placed on the market, though remanufactured products count as new.

Free movement and pre-emption (Art 3)

Article 3 bars Member States from imposing conflicting national performance or information requirements once an EU delegated act covers the same product parameter, and from restricting the free movement of a product that already complies with the applicable ecodesign requirements. This is what makes ESPR a genuine internal-market harmonisation instrument rather than a minimum-standards directive: once Brussels regulates a parameter, national rules on that same parameter step aside.

Chapter II: ecodesign requirements (Arts 4-8)

The delegated-act architecture that does the actual regulating

The Article 4 empowerment

Article 4 empowers the Commission to adopt delegated acts under Article 290 TFEU that supplement ESPR with binding ecodesign requirements for a specific product group, or, where technical similarities allow, horizontal requirements spanning several product groups. The date of application of a delegated act cannot be earlier than 18 months from its entry into force, except in duly justified cases.

Product aspects and the two requirement types (Arts 5-7)

Article 5(1) lists the product aspects a delegated act may address: durability, reliability, reusability, upgradability, repairability, maintenance and refurbishment potential, presence of substances of concern, energy and water use and efficiency, resource use and efficiency, recycled content, remanufacturing potential, recyclability, material-recovery potential, carbon and environmental footprint, and expected waste generation.

Performance requirements (Art 6) set minimum or maximum levels, or non-quantitative improvement obligations, for a product parameter, and cannot restrict a chemical substance's presence for chemical-safety reasons alone. Information requirements (Art 7) mandate that products be accompanied by specified information, always including, at minimum, Digital Product Passport data and substances-of-concern data. A requirement must meet strict criteria under Article 5(11): no significant negative impact on functionality or affordability, no adverse health or safety effect, no disproportionate impact on operator competitiveness including SMEs, no imposed proprietary technology, no disproportionate administrative burden, and an impact assessment is mandatory before any delegated act.

What every delegated act must contain (Art 8)

Article 8 lists the minimum content of every delegated act: the product group definition and CN commodity codes, the requirements themselves, test/measurement/calculation methods, the conformity assessment module, manufacturer information duties, the transitional period, and a scheduled review date.

Chapter III: Digital Product Passport (Arts 9-15)

The most structurally novel element of ESPR, a persistent digital record that follows the product

A market-access condition, not a self-executing rule (Arts 9-11)

Article 9(1) makes the DPP a market-access condition: a covered product can only be placed on the market or put into service if a digital product passport is available. But like ecodesign requirements generally, the DPP is not self-executing, the substantive content, data carrier choice, layout, model/batch/item level, and access rights for each product group are all fixed by the relevant delegated act. Essential requirements (Art 10) include a physically present data carrier, standards-compliant unique identifiers, open-standard and machine-readable data without vendor lock-in, and no customer personal data without explicit consent. The operator placing the product on the market must give dealers a digital copy within 5 working days of request, and keep a back-up copy via a DPP service provider.

Article 11 requires full interoperability between DPPs, free-of-charge access for manufacturers, importers, dealers, repairers, recyclers, market surveillance authorities and civil society according to their access rights, and continued accessibility even after the responsible operator's insolvency.

Registry, web portal and customs controls (Arts 12-15)

Unique operator and facility identifiers follow the Annex III standards (Art 12). The Commission had to set up the DPP registry by 19 July 2026 (Art 13), storing the unique identifiers; the Commission is the data controller, and a registration identifier is issued automatically, explicitly not proof of compliance with ESPR or any other Union law.

A public web portal (Art 14) lets stakeholders search and compare DPP data. From the moment the registry is operational, customs authorities must verify the unique registration identifier before releasing a covered product for free circulation (Art 15); this becomes automatic once the registry interconnects with the EU Customs Single Window Certificates Exchange System, due within four years of the relevant implementing act.

Chapter IV: labels (Arts 16-17)

Rules for any label a delegated act may require, and a ban on mimicking them

Label content and the mimicry ban

Where an information requirement calls for a label, the relevant delegated act fixes its content, layout, display method and, where relevant, electronic generation (Art 16). For energy-related products already carrying an energy label under Regulation (EU) 2017/1369, the Commission may substitute an ESPR label only where the extra information is more relevant and comprehensive. Mimicking an Article 16 label, or otherwise misleading customers about it, is prohibited outright (Art 17), a market-access bar rather than merely an enforcement matter.

Chapter V: prioritisation, planning and consultation (Arts 18-22)

The working plan, its named priority sectors, self-regulation as an alternative route, and SME support

The working plan and its 11 statutory priorities (Art 18)

The working plan is the Commission's rolling prioritisation document, covering at least three years and updated regularly, presented in draft to Parliament first. The first working plan was statutorily due by 19 April 2025 and had to prioritise these product groups, or justify any deviation:

Consultation bodies (Arts 19-20)

Consultation runs through the Ecodesign Forum, a balanced expert group covering Member State representatives and interested parties, and its Member States Expert Group subgroup.

Self-regulation measures (Art 21)

As an alternative to a Commission delegated act, at least two economic operators together can submit a self-regulation measure, a voluntary agreement or code of conduct, covering products not yet in scope of a delegated act or the working plan. To be accepted and listed, it must cover signatories representing at least 80% of the market by volume, include a transparent monitoring plan with an independent inspector, and demonstrably achieve ESPR's objectives faster or more cheaply than regulation would. Non-compliant signatories must be removed after three months without corrective action, and the Commission can delist the whole measure if the criteria stop being met.

SME support (Art 22)

The Commission must support SME, and especially microenterprise, participation with digital tools and tailored guidance accompanying delegated acts, and Member States must run at least a one-stop-shop-type mechanism to help SMEs adapt.

Chapter VI: destruction of unsold consumer products (Arts 23-26)

One of ESPR's most publicly visible provisions, and it applies directly, with no delegated act required

Prevention and disclosure (Arts 23-24)

Article 23 sets a general principle: economic operators must take reasonably expected measures to prevent the need to destroy unsold consumer products. Article 24 requires operators that discard unsold consumer products to publicly disclose, at least annually on their website, the number and weight discarded by type, the reasons, the proportion sent to reuse, recycling or disposal, and prevention measures taken or planned. This duty does not apply to micro and small enterprises, and applies to medium-sized enterprises only from 19 July 2030.

The ban itself (Art 25)

From 19 July 2026, destroying unsold consumer products listed in Annex VII is prohibited for large enterprises, starting with textiles and footwear; medium-sized enterprises follow from 19 July 2030. Micro and small enterprises are exempt entirely. Operators outside the ban's scope cannot accept supplied products for the purpose of circumventing it. The Commission can extend Annex VII by delegated act and must adopt delegated-act derogations, health/hygiene/safety, unrepairable damage, refused donations, or where destruction has the least negative environmental impact. Brubru's reading copy of the operative text cannot verify Annex VII's exact initial product list beyond textiles and footwear; verify the full list directly against EUR-Lex before quoting further categories.

Consolidated reporting (Art 26)

By 19 July 2027, and every 36 months thereafter, the Commission must publish consolidated information on the prevalence and comparative environmental impact of unsold-goods destruction by product group, based on operators' Article 24 disclosures.

Chapter VII: obligations of economic operators (Arts 27-38)

New Legislative Framework style duties across the whole value chain, once a delegated act covers a product

Manufacturers, importers and distributors

Manufacturers must design to the performance requirements, ensure the accompanying information and DPP exist, run the specified conformity assessment procedure, draw up the EU declaration of conformity, affix CE marking, keep technical documentation for 10 years unless the delegated act sets a different period, and provide digital instructions accessible online for at least 10 years after placing on the market. Importers must verify manufacturer compliance before placing products on the market and cannot do so if they doubt conformity. Distributors must exercise due care and check for CE marking, required documentation and labelling before making a product available.

Dealers, fulfilment providers and online marketplaces

Dealers must ensure customers can access DPP and label information, including in distance selling. Fulfilment service providers must not let warehousing or dispatch conditions jeopardise compliance. Online marketplaces must cooperate with market surveillance authorities and maintain a single contact point.

Chapter VIII: conformity of products (Arts 39-47)

The New Legislative Framework pattern, plus a hard ban on testing defeat devices

Test methods, presumption of conformity and CE marking

Test, measurement and calculation methods must be reliable, accurate and reproducible. Harmonised standards and Commission common specifications each give rise to a presumption of conformity. Conformity assessment follows a module-based approach, tied to Decision No 768/2008/EC modules or ESPR's own Annex IV, ending in the EU declaration of conformity (Art 44) and CE marking (Arts 45-46).

Anti-circumvention: no defeat devices

Article 40 bars any behaviour, contractual, commercial or technical, that circumvents compliance, explicitly including defeat-device-style test detection and post-sale software updates that worsen performance without the customer's explicit consent. This closes a loophole familiar from the automotive emissions scandal and applies it to the entire ecodesign framework.

Chapter IX: notification of conformity assessment bodies (Arts 48-63)

The full New Legislative Framework regime for notified bodies, where third-party assessment is required

Notification, independence and monitoring

Where a delegated act requires third-party conformity assessment for a product group, Chapter IX governs the notification, independence, competence, subcontracting and ongoing monitoring of the notified bodies that carry it out, the longest single chapter in the Regulation by article count.

Chapter X: incentives (Arts 64-65)

Rewarding top performers, and a mandatory buying rule for public contracts

Member State incentives for top performers (Art 64)

Member States may offer incentives for top-performing products, aimed at the top two populated performance classes or products holding the EU Ecolabel.

Mandatory green public procurement (Art 65)

Once a product group is covered by a delegated act, contracting authorities and entities under Directives 2014/24/EU and 2014/25/EU must award public contracts complying with Commission-set minimum requirements, technical specifications, award criteria or contract performance conditions, set by implementing act. Award criteria must carry a minimum weighting of 15% to 30% in the tender evaluation; procurement targets must reach a minimum of 50% of relevant procurement, benchmarked against the two highest performance classes or best available performance levels.

Chapter XI: market surveillance (Arts 66-68)

National strategies, ADCO coordination and a four-year Commission benchmarking cycle

National strategies and benchmarking

Market surveillance runs through national strategies coordinated via the ADCO group, with a Commission benchmarking report due every four years, first by 19 July 2028, and cost-recovery rights for authorities that test non-compliant products.

Chapter XII: safeguard procedures (Arts 69-71)

National corrective action escalating, where needed, to a binding Commission decision

National-level and Union safeguard procedures

The safeguard procedure follows the standard EU pattern: a national risk evaluation and corrective-action procedure, escalating, where objections arise, to a Union safeguard procedure and a binding Commission decision via implementing act on whether the national measure was justified. Formal non-compliance triggers, missing declarations, incomplete technical documentation, mislabelling, are also set out here.

Chapter XIII: delegated powers and committee procedure (Arts 72-73)

A five-year delegation, tacitly renewable, exercised under Article 290 TFEU

Five years, tacitly renewable

The power to adopt delegated acts under Articles 4, 10(1), 11, 12(4) and 25(3)/(5) is conferred on the Commission for 5 years from 18 July 2024, tacitly renewable unless Parliament or Council object at least three months before expiry; either institution can revoke at any time, and either can object within two months, extendable by two more, of notification of any individual delegated act. Comitology implementing acts, registry arrangements, common specifications, disclosure format, green public procurement requirements, follow the standard examination procedure (Art 73(3)) or, for self-regulation-measure listing and delisting, the advisory procedure (Art 73(2)).

Chapter XIV: final provisions (Arts 74-80)

Penalties, evaluation, consumer redress, amendments and the repeal timetable

Penalties and evaluation (Arts 74-75)

Member States must set effective, proportionate, dissuasive penalties, at minimum fines and time-limited exclusion from public procurement, calibrated to gravity, intent, financial situation, economic benefit gained and environmental damage caused (Art 74). The Commission must evaluate the whole Regulation by 19 July 2030 and every six years thereafter (Art 75(2)), and separately evaluate whether to extend ESPR to social sustainability requirements by 19 July 2028 (Art 75(4)).

Consumer redress and amendments (Arts 76-78)

Article 76 makes the manufacturer, or failing EU establishment the importer, authorised representative or fulfilment service provider, liable for consumer damage caused by non-compliance with ecodesign requirements, without prejudice to other consumer remedies. Article 77 amends Directive (EU) 2020/1828 and Article 78 amends the Batteries Regulation (EU) 2023/1542, as described in the Overview above.

Repeal, transition and entry into force (Arts 79-80)

Directive 2009/125/EC is repealed with effect from 18 July 2024, but narrow parts stay alive on transitional timetables: until 31 December 2026 for a named list of energy-related product categories still governed by existing implementing measures, and until 31 December 2030 for products under other existing implementing measures, for technical-update purposes only. Market surveillance, anti-circumvention and safeguard provisions of ESPR itself already apply to products still regulated under the old Directive's implementing measures.

The Regulation entered into force on 18 July 2024, 20 days after its 28 June 2024 OJ publication (Art 80).

Key definitions (Art 2)

Article 2 contains 58 defined terms plus cross-references to GDPR, REACH and the Waste Framework Directive. The load-bearing concepts that drive the rest of the Regulation:

Six definitions worth memorising

Ecodesign requirement
A performance requirement or information requirement, or both, aimed at making a product, including its value-chain processes, more environmentally sustainable (Art 2(7)).
Performance requirement
A quantitative or non-quantitative requirement for a product to reach a given performance level on a product parameter listed in Annex I (Art 2(8), Art 6).
Information requirement
The obligation for a product to be accompanied by specified information, detailed in Article 7(2) (Art 2(9)).
Digital Product Passport
A set of product-specific data, defined by the applicable delegated act, accessible electronically via a data carrier under Chapter III (Art 2(28)).
Working plan
The Commission's rolling, at-least-three-year plan listing prioritised product groups and their estimated requirement-setting timelines (Art 18(3)).
Destruction of unsold consumer products
Intentional damaging or discarding of an unsold consumer product as waste, excluding discarding solely for preparing for reuse, refurbishment or remanufacturing (Art 2(34)).

ESPR vs the 2009 Ecodesign Directive

Regulation (EU) 2024/1781 versus the Directive 2009/125/EC it repeals

Feature ESPR (Reg (EU) 2024/1781) Directive 2009/125/EC
Instrument type Regulation, directly applicable in all Member States, no national transposition required Directive, required transposition into national legal orders
Scope Any physical goods placed on the market or put into service, with eight narrow exclusions Energy-related products only: energy-using products and products with an indirect impact on energy use
Headline deliverables Digital Product Passport, destruction ban on unsold goods, mandatory green public procurement and a statutory working plan, all created directly by the operative text No equivalent deliverables; purely an empowerment to adopt product-specific implementing measures for energy-related products
Binding on Economic operators directly once a delegated act applies, plus Member States for penalties and market surveillance Primarily Member States, who then set binding rules for operators through national transposition
Enforcement Harmonised New Legislative Framework conformity assessment, a full notified-body regime, CE marking, and coordinated ADCO market surveillance with a four-year benchmarking cycle Enforcement varied by Member State transposition, product-by-product implementing measures set the applicable conformity route

Same delegated-act mechanism, far wider reach

ESPR keeps the 2009 Directive's core mechanism, binding requirements set by delegated act, but extends it from a narrow list of energy-related products to almost any physical good, while adding four deliverables the old Directive never had: the Digital Product Passport, the destruction ban, mandatory green public procurement and a statutory working plan.

Glossary

Load-bearing terms as used in Regulation (EU) 2024/1781

Ecodesign requirement
A performance requirement or information requirement, or both, aimed at making a product, including its value-chain processes, more environmentally sustainable (Art 2(7)).
Performance requirement
A quantitative or non-quantitative requirement for a product to reach a given performance level on a product parameter listed in Annex I (Art 2(8)).
Information requirement
The obligation for a product to be accompanied by specified information, as detailed in Article 7(2) (Art 2(9)).
Digital Product Passport (DPP)
A set of product-specific data, defined by the applicable delegated act, accessible electronically via a data carrier under Chapter III (Art 2(28)).
Product group
A set of products serving similar purposes, similar in use or functional properties, and similar in consumer perception (Art 2(5)).
Delegated act
The Article 290 TFEU instrument through which the Commission supplements ESPR with binding, product-group-specific ecodesign requirements (Art 4).
Working plan
The Commission's rolling, at-least-three-year plan listing prioritised product groups and their estimated requirement-setting timelines (Art 18(3)).
Destruction of unsold consumer products
Intentional damaging or discarding of an unsold consumer product as waste, excluding discarding solely for preparing for reuse, refurbishment or remanufacturing (Art 2(34)).
Substance of concern
A substance meeting REACH SVHC criteria, certain CLP hazard classes, POPs Regulation coverage, or that negatively affects reuse or recycling of the product's materials (Art 2(27)).
Self-regulation measure
A voluntary agreement or code of conduct that economic operators submit as an alternative to a delegated act, subject to Commission vetting and an 80% market-share threshold (Art 21).
Economic operator
Collective term covering the manufacturer, authorised representative, importer, distributor, dealer and fulfilment service provider (Art 2(46)).
Premature obsolescence
A design feature, action or omission that makes a product non-functional or worse-performing other than through normal wear and tear (Art 2(21)).
