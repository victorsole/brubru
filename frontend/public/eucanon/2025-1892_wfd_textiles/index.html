Overview

What the Directive is, why it exists, and how it is structured

Two subjects, one targeted revision of the Waste Framework Directive

Directive (EU) 2025/1892 of 10 September 2025 amends Directive 2008/98/EC, the Waste Framework Directive, inserting seven new articles: Articles 9a, 22a, 22b, 22c, 22d, 29a and 41a. It runs to 60 recitals, 4 articles and a new Annex IVc in two parts, across 29 pages of the Official Journal.

It does two distinct things in one instrument: it creates the EU's first harmonised extended producer responsibility regime for textiles, textile-related products and footwear, and it sets the EU's first legally binding food waste reduction targets.

Why this Directive closes a gap

Directive 2008/98/EC already required Member States to collect textiles separately from 1 January 2025, but it said nothing about who pays for that collection or what happens to the textiles afterwards. The result was 27 different national approaches, with some Member States building extended producer responsibility schemes and others leaving municipalities to absorb the cost.

Directive (EU) 2025/1892 closes that gap. It makes producers financially responsible for the textiles they place on the market, harmonises the scope by customs code across the whole Union, and puts the entire regime on a single EU timetable, while separately introducing binding food waste targets that did not previously exist anywhere in EU law.

A Directive, not a Regulation

Unlike the Packaging and Packaging Waste Regulation, this is a Directive: it does not apply directly. Each Member State must transpose it into national law by 17 June 2027, and must have textile extended producer responsibility schemes established and operational by 17 April 2028, a date ten months later than the transposition deadline. The two dates are frequently, and wrongly, collapsed into one.

Articles 22a to 22d apply to micro-enterprises, businesses with fewer than 10 people and turnover or a balance sheet of EUR 2 million or less, only from 17 April 2029 (amended Article 41).

Procedure and signature

The Directive was negotiated under the ordinary legislative procedure, file 2023/0234(COD), lead committee ENVI in the European Parliament and the Environment Council on the Council side, with DG Environment leading for the Commission. It was signed at Strasbourg on 10 September 2025 by Roberta Metsola for the European Parliament and Magnus Bjerre for the Council, under the Danish presidency, published in OJ L, 2025/1892 on 26 September 2025, and entered into force on 16 October 2025, the twentieth day after publication.

Common error to avoid

The transposition deadline (17 June 2027) and the date textile EPR schemes must exist (17 April 2028) are ten months apart. Any answer that collapses them into a single "by 2027" date is wrong.

Part 1: extended producer responsibility for textiles

Who pays, who is a producer, and how the money and the material flow

Scope: Annex IVc, by customs code

Scope is defined by Combined Nomenclature (CN) codes under Regulation (EEC) No 2658/87, not by description. Annex IVc has two parts.

Part I, textile products and articles of apparel: Chapter 61 (knitted or crocheted apparel and accessories), Chapter 62 (apparel and accessories, not knitted or crocheted), 6301 blankets and travelling rugs (excluding 63011000), 6302 bed, table, toilet and kitchen linen, 6303 curtains, interior blinds and valances, 6304 other furnishing articles (excluding heading 9404), 6309 worn clothing and other worn articles, and 6504 and 6505 hats and other headgear.

Part II, footwear and non-textile apparel: 4203 articles of apparel and clothing accessories of leather or composition leather, and 6401 to 6405 footwear, all categories.

The Annex IVc headings cover products for household use or other uses where such products are similar in nature and composition to those for household use. Recital 28 is explicit that other uses includes professional uses, unless dedicated separate collection and treatment obligations for those products already exist under other Union or national law. The exclusion is narrow: products for professional use, including military use, that can pose safety, health and hygiene risks or raise security concerns, are excluded.

This scope is not the same as the ESPR unsold-goods ban

Professional-use garments are inside this Directive's Annex IVc scope unless the narrow safety, hygiene or security carve-out applies. The Ecodesign for Sustainable Products Regulation ban on destroying unsold goods, by contrast, is limited to "consumer products" under Article 2(36) of Regulation (EU) 2024/1781, meaning products primarily intended for consumers. A garment can therefore sit inside textile EPR and outside the ESPR destruction ban at the same time. See the comparison table below, and never reason from one scope to the other.

Who is a producer (new Article 3(4b))

Any manufacturer, importer, distributor or other natural or legal person that, whatever the selling technique including distance contracts, either:

  • is established in a Member State and manufactures Annex IVc products under its own name or trademark, or has them designed or manufactured and supplies them first under its own name or trademark, in that Member State
  • is established in a Member State and resells there, under its own name or trademark, products made by others whose name or trademark does not appear
  • is established in a Member State and supplies Annex IVc products there for the first time on a professional basis from another Member State or a third country
  • sells Annex IVc products by distance contract directly to end users in a Member State while established in another Member State or a third country

Excluded from the definition: persons supplying used Annex IVc products assessed as fit for re-use, persons supplying products derived from used or waste products or their parts, and self-employed tailors producing customised products. Producers under the fourth limb must appoint an authorised representative established in the Member State concerned (Article 22a(3)).

What producers must pay for (Article 22a(8))
  • Collection of used products for re-use and separate collection of waste products for preparing for re-use and recycling
  • Transport for subsequent sorting, preparing for re-use and recycling
  • Sorting, preparing for re-use, recycling, other recovery and disposal
  • Collection, transport and treatment of waste arising from operations carried out by social economy entities and other actors in the collection system
  • Compositional surveys of collected mixed municipal waste
  • Information campaigns on sustainable consumption, waste prevention, re-use, repair, recycling and disposal
  • Data gathering and reporting to competent authorities
  • Research and development on product design under Article 5 of the ESPR and on waste prevention and management, with a view to scaling up fibre-to-fibre recycling

Costs must not exceed what is necessary to provide those services cost-efficiently (Article 22a(12)). Member States may also make producers cover the costs of Annex IVc waste ending up in mixed municipal waste (Article 22a(9)).

Producer responsibility organisations (Article 22c)

Membership is mandatory: producers must entrust a producer responsibility organisation (PRO) to discharge their obligations (Article 22c(1)). PROs need authorisation from a competent authority and must demonstrate expertise in waste management and sustainability.

Fee modulation must be based on weight and, where appropriate, quantity, and modulated on the ecodesign requirements adopted under Regulation (EU) 2024/1781, the ESPR, that are most relevant to waste prevention and to treatment in line with the waste hierarchy. Member States may additionally modulate fees to address ultra-fast and fast fashion practices, based on product life span, useful life beyond the first user, and contribution to closing the loop (Article 22c(6)); recital 40 suggests criteria such as the width of the product range, the frequency of offers, and repair incentives. The Commission must adopt implementing acts laying down fee modulation criteria where needed to avoid internal-market distortion (Article 22c(7)). Fees must ensure equal treatment regardless of origin or size, without disproportionate burdens on SMEs.

The formal hinge to the ESPR

The requirement that PRO fee modulation follow ESPR ecodesign requirements is the one point in the text where textile EPR and the Ecodesign for Sustainable Products Regulation are formally linked in law. This Directive does not create ecodesign requirements itself, it consumes the ones the ESPR adopts.

Social economy entities are protected, not displaced

The Directive gives charities, cooperatives and social enterprises unusually strong protections (Article 22c(10) to (13)):

  • PROs may not refuse the participation of local public authorities, social economy entities or other re-use operators in the collection system
  • Social economy entities may maintain and operate their own collection points and must receive equal or preferential treatment in the siting of collection points
  • They are not required to hand over collected textiles to the PRO
  • They report annually on quantities collected, split by fit-for-re-use, preparing-for-re-use and recycling, and other recovery or disposal, with exports indicated where possible; Member States may exempt them totally or partially where reporting would be a disproportionate burden
Register of producers (Article 22b)

Each Member State establishes a register. Producers must apply for registration in each Member State where they first make Annex IVc products available. Member States may only allow products to be made available where the producer, or its authorised representative, is registered. The Commission maintains a website linking all national registers; registers must be publicly accessible, free, machine readable, sortable and searchable.

Online platforms and fulfilment service providers

Online platforms falling within Chapter III, Section 4 of the Digital Services Act (Regulation (EU) 2022/2065) must obtain, before allowing a producer to use their services, the producer's registration number in the register of the Member State where the consumer is located, plus a self-certification of compliance (Article 22a(13), tied to DSA Article 30).

Fulfilment service providers, as defined in Article 3(11) of Regulation (EU) 2019/1020, must receive the same information, make best efforts to verify that it is reliable and complete, request correction where it is not, and suspend service where the producer fails to correct. The producer is liable for accuracy. Suspended producers have a right to challenge the suspension before a court (Article 22a(15) to (17)).

Waste status and the re-use carve-out (Article 22d(2) and (3))

Separately collected used and waste textiles are waste upon collection, with one exception: products directly handed over by end users and directly professionally assessed as fit for re-use at the collection point, by a re-use operator or social economy entity, are not waste. Recital 48 stresses the assessment must be professional, because the end user is not trained to distinguish re-usable from recyclable items.

This matters for shipment: everything separately collected is treated as waste, and subject to Union waste shipment law, until it has been sorted by an operator trained in sorting for re-use and recycling.

Sorting and export controls (Article 22d(5) and (7) to (10))

Sorting must generate products for re-use and preparing for re-use, prioritising local sorting and local re-use where appropriate; sort at a granularity allowing item-to-item separation targeting a specific re-use market; sort non-re-usable items for remanufacturing and recycling, prioritising remanufacturing over recycling; and produce outputs meeting end-of-waste criteria under Article 6.

Shipments of used textiles assessed as fit for re-use must carry: a copy of the invoice and contract stating they are destined for and fit for direct re-use, evidence of a prior sorting or direct professional assessment with records for every bale, and a declaration that no material in the consignment is waste. Records must be fixed securely but not permanently to the packaging and must describe the items and name the company responsible for final sorting. Where a shipment is suspected of being waste, the costs of analysis, inspection and storage may be charged to the producers or to whoever arranged the shipment.

Mattresses: an optional extension

Member States may set up an EPR scheme for mattress producers (Article 22a(2)). This is optional, not required. Recital 29 notes the Commission will assess extending EPR to further streams such as mattresses and carpets in the context of the forthcoming Circular Economy Act.

Part 2: food waste

The EU's first legally binding food waste reduction targets

The two binding targets (new Article 9a(4))

By 31 December 2030, measured against the annual average for 2021 to 2023:

  • 10% reduction in food waste generated in processing and manufacturing
  • 30% reduction in food waste per capita, jointly across retail and other distribution of food, restaurants and food services, and households

These are the EU's first legally binding food waste targets. They are deliberately below UN Sustainable Development Goal Target 12.3, halving per capita food waste by 2030; recital 15 explains the step-wise approach.

Baselines, corrections and review

A Member State may use 2020 or an earlier year as baseline where it can show measurements consistent with Delegated Decision (EU) 2019/1597, notifying the Commission by 17 April 2027. The Commission may reject the proposed baseline within six months.

The Commission adopts a tourism correction factor by implementing act by 17 October 2027, because tourists are not counted in the resident population used for the per-capita target. The Commission reviews the targets by 31 December 2027, including whether to set binding 2035 targets and whether to extend targets to other supply-chain stages.

Prevention measures (Article 9a(1))

Member States must at least: develop behavioural change interventions and awareness campaigns; identify and address inefficiencies in the food supply chain, which may include tackling market practices that cause food waste; encourage food donation and redistribution for human consumption, prioritising human use over animal feed and non-food reprocessing; support training, skills and access to funding, particularly for SMEs and social economy entities; and encourage innovation and technological solutions.

Member States must, after consulting food banks, take measures where appropriate so that economic operators with a significant role in food waste propose donation agreements to food banks and other redistribution organisations at a reasonable cost. Article 11 of Directive 2019/633 on unfair trading practices is expressly protected: food waste measures must not reduce the bargaining power of agricultural suppliers (recital 11).

Governance

Member States designate a competent authority to coordinate food waste action and inform the Commission by 17 January 2026 (Article 29a(2)). Food waste prevention programmes are evaluated, adapted and communicated to the Commission by 17 October 2027 (Article 29a(1)).

What this Directive does NOT do

  • Sets no recycling, collection or re-use targets for textiles. Recital 59 and Article 41a defer that question to the Commission's 2029 evaluation
  • Does not set end-of-waste criteria for textiles. The Commission will propose an implementing act based on Joint Research Centre work (recitals 48 and 49)
  • Does not create the Digital Product Passport. That is the ESPR, Regulation (EU) 2024/1781. This Directive links to it only through fee modulation and R&D funding obligations
  • Does not ban the destruction of unsold textiles. That is Article 25 of the ESPR, applying from 19 July 2026 for large enterprises and 19 July 2030 for medium-sized, micro and small enterprises, which are exempt

How the Member States are transposing it

A directive that is not yet due, and not yet transposed anywhere

A directive is not directly applicable: each Member State must write it into its own national law by a transposition deadline. For this Directive, the EU transposition deadline is 17 June 2027, and textile EPR schemes must be established and operational by 17 April 2028. Because the Directive entered into force only on 16 October 2025, no Member State has yet notified a national transposing measure to the Commission. Key definitions

The load-bearing concepts that drive the rest of the Directive

Six definitions worth memorising

Producer
Under new Article 3(4b), any manufacturer, importer, distributor or other person who first supplies Annex IVc products in a Member State, resells them under their own name, supplies them there for the first time from another Member State or third country, or sells them by distance contract directly to end users there.
Producer responsibility organisation (PRO)
Under Article 22c, the body producers must entrust to discharge their extended producer responsibility obligations collectively, subject to authorisation by a competent authority and expertise requirements in waste management and sustainability.
Annex IVc products
The Directive's scope test: textile products, articles of apparel, footwear and leather accessories defined by Combined Nomenclature code across two Annex IVc parts, covering household and similar professional uses.
Waste, upon separate collection
Under Article 22d(2) and (3), separately collected used and waste textiles are waste from the moment of collection, except items directly handed over by an end user and directly professionally assessed as fit for re-use at the collection point.
Fulfilment service provider
As defined in Article 3(11) of Regulation (EU) 2019/1020, and reused here to impose verification and suspension duties on any business that warehouses, packs, addresses or ships Annex IVc products without owning them.
Food waste reduction target
Under new Article 9a(4), the two-part binding target: a 10% cut in processing and manufacturing food waste and a 30% per-capita cut across retail, food service and households, both measured against the 2021-2023 average and due by 31 December 2030.

This Directive vs the ESPR unsold-goods ban

Two different scopes that are easy, and wrong, to conflate

Feature Textile EPR, Directive (EU) 2025/1892 ESPR unsold-goods ban, Reg (EU) 2024/1781 Art 25
Instrument type Directive amending Directive 2008/98/EC, requires national transposition Regulation, directly applicable, no transposition required
What it does Extended producer responsibility funding collection, sorting, re-use, recycling and R&D for Annex IVc textile, textile-related and footwear products Prohibits destroying unsold consumer products, subject to phased application by enterprise size
Scope test Annex IVc products defined by customs code; covers household and similar professional uses (recital 28), unless a safety, hygiene or security carve-out applies Limited to "consumer products" under Article 2(36) ESPR, meaning products primarily intended for consumers
Compliance date Transposition by 17 June 2027; EPR schemes operational by 17 April 2028 Ban applies from 19 July 2026 for large enterprises, 19 July 2030 for medium, small and micro enterprises, which are exempt
Relationship A garment can sit inside textile EPR and outside the ESPR destruction ban at the same time. The two scopes are not identical and should never be inferred from one another.

Glossary

Load-bearing terms as used in Directive (EU) 2025/1892

EPR
Extended producer responsibility: producers bear the collection, sorting, re-use, recycling and reporting costs of the Annex IVc products they place on the market (Article 22a).
PRO
Producer responsibility organisation: the body through which producers must collectively discharge their EPR obligations, subject to authorisation (Article 22c).
CN code
Combined Nomenclature: the customs classification system under Regulation (EEC) No 2658/87 that defines the exact scope of Annex IVc, rather than a plain-language description.
Annex IVc
The new annex, in two parts, listing every CN code covered by textile extended producer responsibility: apparel, household textiles, footwear and leather accessories.
DSA
Digital Services Act, Regulation (EU) 2022/2065: supplies the rule that online platforms must check a producer's registration number before letting them sell Annex IVc products (Article 22a(13)).
ESPR
Ecodesign for Sustainable Products Regulation, Regulation (EU) 2024/1781: the source of the ecodesign requirements that PRO fee modulation must track, and of the separate unsold-goods destruction ban.
Fulfilment service provider
Any business that warehouses, packs, addresses or ships products without owning them, as defined in Article 3(11) of Regulation (EU) 2019/1020, and now subject to producer-verification duties.
Social economy entity
Charities, cooperatives and social enterprises collecting textiles for re-use, given the right to keep their own collection points and preferential siting under Article 22c(10) to (13).
End-of-waste criteria
The Article 6 conditions a sorted output must meet to stop being waste; not yet set for textiles, pending a future Commission implementing act based on Joint Research Centre work.
Fee modulation
The practice of setting a producer's EPR fee according to how their product is designed, tied here to ESPR ecodesign requirements and, optionally, to fast-fashion business practices (Article 22c(6)).
Micro-enterprise
Under the amended Article 41, a business with fewer than 10 people and turnover or a balance sheet of EUR 2 million or less; Articles 22a to 22d apply to them only from 17 April 2029.
Waste Shipment Regulation
Regulation (EU) 2024/1157: governs cross-border movement of used and waste textiles until they have been sorted, and is reinforced by the export documentation rules in Article 22d.
