Overview

What EHDS is, why it exists, and how it is structured

The first sector-specific European data space to become binding law

The European Health Data Space is the first of the Commission's proposed common European data spaces to move from concept into a fully adopted Regulation. It follows directly from the Commission's post-COVID-19 commitment to build a European Health Union: a pandemic exposed how fragmented, paper-bound and non-interoperable health records across the 27 Member States slowed both patient care and public-health response. EHDS answers that gap with binding law rather than a voluntary framework.

Legally, EHDS rests on Article 16 TFEU (data protection) and Article 114 TFEU (internal market), reflecting its dual purpose: strengthening the rights natural persons already have under the GDPR over a specific, sensitive data category, while building the internal-market conditions for electronic health record systems and health datasets to move safely between Member States.

Two lanes: primary use and secondary use

EHDS organises everything around a single distinction. Primary use (Chapter II) is processing electronic health data to deliver healthcare to the person the data concerns: viewing a patient summary, issuing an electronic prescription, receiving a discharge report from another Member State. Secondary use (Chapter IV) is processing that same data, once anonymised or pseudonymised and cleared through a health data access body, for research, policymaking, regulation, statistics or care-quality improvement, purposes other than the original reason the data were collected.

Each lane gets its own cross-border infrastructure, its own governance body, and its own access mechanism, MyHealth@EU for primary use and HealthData@EU for secondary use, and each lane is deliberately staggered into force at a different date so health systems and manufacturers are not asked to comply with everything at once (Art 105).

What EHDS amends

EHDS amends Directive 2011/24/EU on patients' rights in cross-border healthcare: Article 14, which created the voluntary eHealth Network, is deleted with effect from 26 March 2031 once EHDS's own governance structure (the EHDS Board and MyHealth@EU) takes over that coordination role in full.

It also amends Regulation (EU) 2024/2847, the Cyber Resilience Act, aligning the conformity-assessment route for EHR systems that also qualify as products with digital elements, so manufacturers face one coherent certification path rather than two overlapping ones.

Chapter I: general provisions (Arts 1-2)

Subject matter, scope and the definitions that carry the rest of the Regulation

Subject matter and scope (Art 1)

Article 1 establishes EHDS as covering three things at once: rights for natural persons over their own electronic health data, rules for placing EHR systems and wellness applications on the market, and a framework governing the secondary use of electronic health data for research, innovation, policymaking, patient safety, and regulatory and statistical purposes.

  • Applies to health data holders, health data users, manufacturers and providers of EHR systems and wellness applications, and natural persons in the Union
  • Does not affect Union law on data protection, in particular the GDPR, which continues to apply in full alongside EHDS
  • Does not create new grounds for restricting the free movement of health data beyond what EHDS itself specifies
Definitions (Art 2)

Article 2 sets out the vocabulary the rest of the Regulation depends on: personal electronic health data, primary use, secondary use, EHR system, health data holder, health data user, data permit, and around forty further terms. See the key definitions section below for the load-bearing concepts.

Chapter II: primary use (Arts 3-24)

The six rights, the priority categories, the exchange format, and MyHealth@EU

The six rights of natural persons (Arts 3-10)

Every natural person in the Union has, over their own electronic health data:

  • Right of access (Art 3): free, immediate, in an easily readable and consolidated format, in the European electronic health record exchange format on request, covering at least the six priority categories
  • Right to insert information (Art 5): a patient's own entries stay distinguishable from health-professional entries, and cannot alter them
  • Right to rectification (Art 6): exercised online, aligned with Article 16 GDPR
  • Right to data portability (Art 7): all or part of the data transmitted to another healthcare provider immediately, free of charge, without hindrance; cross-border transmission runs through MyHealth@EU
  • Right to restrict access (Art 8): the patient can restrict all or parts of their data, and the restriction itself stays invisible to healthcare providers
  • Right to information on access (Art 9): automatic notifications and an access log retained for at least three years, showing who accessed which data and when

Member States may additionally provide a reversible right to opt out of EHR registration altogether (Art 10), provided vital-interest overrides remain available.

Priority categories and the exchange format (Arts 14-15)

Six priority categories, expandable by delegated act, drive everything else in the primary-use lane: patient summaries, electronic prescriptions, electronic dispensations, medical imaging studies and reports, medical test results, and discharge reports. Their main characteristics are set out in Annex I.

By 26 March 2027, the Commission must adopt implementing acts laying down the European electronic health record exchange format: a commonly used, machine-readable format supporting both structured and unstructured health data, with harmonised datasets, coding systems and interoperability specifications for the six priority categories.

Governance and MyHealth@EU (Arts 19-24)

Each Member State designates one or more digital health authorities (notified by 26 March 2027) responsible for implementing Chapters II and III nationally, and publishes a biennial activity report covering EHR access rates, requests handled and cross-border data volumes. Complaints under Articles 3 and 5-10 route to the GDPR supervisory authority, since those rights mirror existing GDPR rights.

The Commission establishes MyHealth@EU as the central interoperability platform for digital health. Each Member State designates a national contact point for digital health; all healthcare providers must connect to it, and pharmacies must be able to dispense electronic prescriptions issued in other Member States. National contact points act as joint controllers, with the Commission as processor.

Chapter III: EHR systems and wellness applications (Arts 25-49)

EHR systems become regulated products, on the CE-marking pattern used for medical devices

Two mandatory harmonised software components (Art 25, Annex II)

Every EHR system placed on the market or put into service must include the European interoperability software component, which provides and receives priority-category data in the European exchange format, and the European logging software component, which records which health professional accessed which priority-category data and when. In-house systems developed and used within Union health institutions count as "put into service" and are covered too (Art 26).

Conformity assessment and CE marking (Section 3, Arts 36-42)
  • Common specifications set the interoperability and logging requirements (Art 36)
  • Manufacturers hold technical documentation (Art 37) and issue an EU declaration of conformity (Art 39)
  • A European digital testing environment, developed by the Commission as open source, and national digital testing environments, feed test results into that documentation (Art 40)
  • CE marking (Art 41) signals conformity with EHDS and any other applicable Union law, following Regulation (EC) No 765/2008
  • Manufacturers of medical devices, in vitro diagnostic medical devices, and high-risk AI systems that claim interoperability with EHR systems must separately prove compliance with Annex II Section 2 (Art 27)
Wellness applications and the EU database (Arts 47-49)

Wellness applications, consumer-facing software for health or wellbeing purposes other than healthcare delivery, are not certified the same way EHR systems are. If a manufacturer claims interoperability with EHR systems, the application must instead carry a self-issued label, valid for a maximum of three years (Art 47). Interoperability never implies automatic data sharing: the natural person's consent under Article 5 is always required (Art 48).

The Commission maintains a public EU database for registration of EHR systems and wellness applications; manufacturers must register before placing a product on the market (Art 49).

Chapter IV: secondary use (Arts 50-81)

17 minimum categories, 6 permitted purposes, 5 prohibited uses, and HealthData@EU

17 minimum categories (Art 51) and 6 permitted purposes (Art 53)

Health data holders must make at least 17 categories of electronic health data available for secondary use, spanning EHR data, socioeconomic and environmental determinants, administrative and reimbursement data, genomic and other omics data, clinical trial data, registry data, and biobank data. Individual researchers and microenterprises acting as health data holders are exempt unless a Member State extends the obligation to them (Art 50).

That data may only be processed for six purposes: public-interest public and occupational health activities; policymaking and regulatory activities supporting public bodies or Union institutions; official statistics; education and training in the health and care sectors; scientific research, including AI development and testing; and improving delivery of care based on other people's health data. Access for the first three purposes is reserved to public sector bodies and Union institutions.

Five prohibited uses (Art 54)
  • Decisions detrimental to a natural person or group, with legal, social or economic effects
  • Discrimination in job offers, goods, services, insurance or credit
  • Advertising or marketing activities
  • Developing products or services that may harm individuals, public health or society, including illicit drugs, alcohol, tobacco and weapons
  • Activities contrary to national ethical provisions
Access mechanisms and HealthData@EU (Arts 55-76)

Each Member State designates one or more health data access bodies (notified by 26 March 2027), which decide on data permits, provide access in secure processing environments, and monitor compliance. Access runs through a data permit (Art 68, full access in a secure processing environment where raw data cannot be downloaded), a health data request (Art 69, an anonymised statistical answer only, no access to underlying data), or, where a Member State designates one, a trusted health data holder (Art 72, a simplified pre-assessment route).

Every natural person has an unconditional, reversible right to opt out of secondary use at any time (Art 71), with narrow public-interest research exceptions. Each Member State designates a national contact point for secondary use, connecting to HealthData@EU, the Union-level cross-border infrastructure for research, policy and statistics (Art 75); third-country participation follows from 26 March 2035.

Fines (Art 64)

€10,000,000 or 2% of worldwide annual turnover (whichever is higher), for infringing health data holder or health data user duties.

€20,000,000 or 4% of worldwide annual turnover (whichever is higher), for processing data for a prohibited use, extracting personal electronic health data from a secure processing environment, or re-identifying or attempting to re-identify a natural person.

Chapter V: additional actions (Arts 82-91)

Capacity building, cooperation and indirect availability of data

Capacity building and cooperation

Chapter V supports the operational side of EHDS: Union and national capacity-building activities to help digital health authorities, health data access bodies and healthcare providers implement Chapters II to IV, cooperation between Member States on cross-border healthcare and data-sharing practices, and provisions on indirect availability of electronic health data for secondary use where a health data holder cannot make data directly available in a required format.

Chapter VI: European governance and coordination (Arts 92-96)

The EHDS Board coordinates both lanes across all 27 Member States

The EHDS Board (Art 92)

The European Health Data Space Board is composed of two representatives per Member State, one from the national digital health authority and one from the national health data access body, plus the Commission, which chairs it. The Board advises on implementation, coordinates cross-border operations, and promotes common standards across MyHealth@EU and HealthData@EU.

  • Art 93: sub-groups handle primary use and secondary use separately
  • Art 94: joint controllership arrangements govern MyHealth@EU and HealthData@EU
  • Art 96: a dispute-resolution mechanism applies between health data access bodies of different Member States

Chapters VII-IX: committee procedure, penalties and staggered application

Delegated powers, national penalties, and the deadlines that bring EHDS fully into force

Delegation of powers and committee procedure (Chapter VII, Arts 97-98)

The Commission is empowered to adopt delegated and implementing acts across EHDS, notably to expand the priority categories for primary use and to set the technical specifications for the European exchange format, assisted by a comitology committee.

Miscellaneous (Chapter VIII, Arts 99-104)
  • Art 99: Member States lay down national penalties, effective, proportionate and dissuasive, notified to the Commission by 26 March 2027
  • Art 100: right to compensation for material or non-material damage
  • Art 101: representation by not-for-profit bodies with public-interest objectives
  • Art 102: a targeted evaluation by 26 March 2033, an overall evaluation by 26 March 2035, and an annual Commission progress report to the Council until full application
  • Art 103: Article 14 of Directive 2011/24/EU (the eHealth Network) is deleted from 26 March 2031
  • Art 104: amends Regulation (EU) 2024/2847, the Cyber Resilience Act, aligning conformity assessment for EHR systems classified as products with digital elements

Staggered application (Chapter IX, Art 105)

EHDS enters into force on 25 March 2025 but does not apply all at once. General application begins 26 March 2027. The first three priority categories (patient summaries, e-prescriptions, e-dispensations) and their supporting articles apply from 26 March 2029, the same date Chapter IV on secondary use starts to apply. The remaining three priority categories (imaging, test results, discharge reports) and the EHR-system obligations of Chapter III apply from 26 March 2031. Third-country participation in HealthData@EU applies only from 26 March 2035.

Key definitions (Art 2)

Article 2 contains around forty definitions. The load-bearing concepts that drive the rest of the Regulation:

Six definitions worth memorising

EHR system
Software, or hardware and software together, that stores, intermediates, exports, imports, converts, edits or views personal electronic health data in the priority categories for primary use, intended by the manufacturer for healthcare providers or patients.
Personal electronic health data
Data concerning health and genetic data, relating to an identified or identifiable natural person, processed in electronic form.
Primary use
Processing of electronic health data for the provision of healthcare, to assess, maintain or restore the health of the natural person the data relate to, including prescription, dispensation and reimbursement services.
Secondary use
Processing of electronic health data for the purposes set out in Chapter IV, research, policymaking, statistics, education and care improvement, other than the initial purpose for which the data were collected.
Health data holder
Any natural or legal person, public authority or body in the healthcare or care sectors, or one able to make non-personal electronic health data available through control of its technical design, that processes personal electronic health data for healthcare, public health, reimbursement, research, innovation, policymaking, statistics, patient safety or regulatory purposes.
Data permit
An administrative decision issued by a health data access body to a health data user, authorising processing of specific electronic health data for specific secondary-use purposes.

EHDS vs GDPR vs Data Act

Three overlapping EU data frameworks, and how EHDS fits between them

Feature EHDS (Reg (EU) 2025/327) GDPR (Reg (EU) 2016/679) Data Act (Reg (EU) 2023/2854)
Scope Electronic health data only, sector-specific All personal data, cross-sector Data generated by connected products and services, cross-sector
Data type Personal and non-personal electronic health data Personal data only Personal and non-personal data, mostly IoT-generated
Rights holder Every natural person, over their own health data specifically Every data subject, over any personal data about them Users of connected products, over data those products generate
Cross-border tool MyHealth@EU and HealthData@EU, purpose-built infrastructures No dedicated infrastructure; one-stop-shop cooperation between supervisory authorities No dedicated infrastructure; switching and interoperability obligations on providers
Primary / secondary distinction Central organising principle: separate chapters, rules and infrastructures Not a formal distinction; purpose limitation applies generally Not applicable in the same sense
Penalty tier €10M / 2% or €20M / 4% of turnover (Art 64) €10M / 2% or €20M / 4% of turnover (Art 83 GDPR) Set by Member States; must be effective, proportionate, dissuasive

They apply together, not instead of each other

EHDS does not replace the GDPR: Article 1 is explicit that Union data-protection law continues to apply in full, and complaints about the six patient rights route to the GDPR supervisory authority precisely because those rights mirror existing GDPR entitlements. EHDS adds a sector-specific layer, common exchange formats, dedicated infrastructure, certified EHR systems and a controlled secondary-use pathway, on top of the general data-protection floor the GDPR already sets.

Glossary

Load-bearing terms as used in Regulation (EU) 2025/327

MyHealth@EU
The Union-level cross-border digital infrastructure for primary use, connecting national contact points for digital health so patient summaries and prescriptions follow patients across Member States (Art 23).
HealthData@EU
The Union-level cross-border digital infrastructure for secondary use, connecting national contact points for secondary use so researchers and public bodies can access datasets across Member States through a single channel (Art 75).
EHR system
Software, or hardware and software together, intended for healthcare providers or patients, that stores, intermediates, exports, imports, converts, edits or views priority-category personal electronic health data. Subject to CE-marking under Chapter III.
Wellness application
Software intended for a natural person, processing electronic health data or providing health-related information, for purposes other than the provision of healthcare. Needs only a self-issued interoperability label, not full CE marking (Art 47).
Health data holder
Any natural or legal person, public authority or body processing personal electronic health data for healthcare, public health, reimbursement, research, innovation, policymaking, statistics, patient safety or regulatory purposes, or able to make non-personal health data available.
Health data user
A natural or legal person, including Union institutions, granted lawful access to electronic health data for secondary use under a data permit, an approved health data request, or approval as an authorised participant in HealthData@EU.
Data permit
An administrative decision issued by a health data access body authorising a health data user to process specific electronic health data for a specific secondary-use purpose, accessed only within a secure processing environment (Art 68).
Secure processing environment
A technically and organisationally safeguarded environment in which health data users work with permitted datasets. Only anonymised results can leave; downloads or copies of raw data are prohibited (Art 73).
Data quality and utility label
A graphic diagram, including a scale, describing a dataset's documentation, technical quality, quality management, coverage and conditions of use. Mandatory for datasets funded with Union or national public money (Art 78).
National contact point for digital health
The body each Member State designates to connect its healthcare providers to MyHealth@EU, acting as joint controller alongside the Commission, which serves as processor (Art 23).
Health data access body
The body each Member State designates to decide on data permits, provide access in secure processing environments, and monitor secondary-use compliance. The Commission plays this role for Union institutions (Arts 55-56).
Trusted health data holder
A health data holder that a Member State may designate, meeting technical, expertise and guarantee conditions, allowing it to pre-assess secondary-use applications for its own data through a simplified procedure (Art 72).
European interoperability software component
One of the two harmonised software components every EHR system must include, responsible for providing and receiving priority-category data in the European electronic health record exchange format (Annex II).
European logging software component
The second mandatory harmonised software component, recording logging information on which health professional accessed priority-category data and when, in the format set out in Annex II point 3.2.
EHDS Board
The European Health Data Space Board (Art 92), with two representatives per Member State plus the Commission, advising on implementation and coordinating MyHealth@EU and HealthData@EU across the Union.
