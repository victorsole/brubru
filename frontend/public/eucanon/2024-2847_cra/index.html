The Cyber Resilience Act

Regulation (EU) 2024/2847 sets cybersecurity requirements for every product with digital elements sold in the EU. Its reporting duties under Article 14 have applied since 11 September 2026, including to products already on the market. Everything else applies from 11 December 2027.

Overview

What it is, what it applies to, and why Article 14 already bites

What it is

Regulation (EU) 2024/2847 of the European Parliament and of the Council of 23 October 2024 is formally titled "on horizontal cybersecurity requirements for products with digital elements and amending Regulations (EU) No 168/2013 and (EU) 2019/1020 and Directive (EU) 2020/1828 (Cyber Resilience Act)". It was published in the Official Journal on 20 November 2024 and entered into force on 10 December 2024, the twentieth day after publication.

It is the first horizontal EU framework that makes cybersecurity a condition of placing connected products on the market (recital 4). A product that meets the Regulation carries the CE marking. A product that does not can be withdrawn or recalled, and its manufacturer can be fined.

What it applies to

It applies to products with digital elements made available on the market whose intended or reasonably foreseeable use includes a direct or indirect data connection to a device or network. That covers hardware, software and the remote data processing solutions that belong to the product. It grades products into a default class, important products (Class I and Class II) and critical products, with stricter conformity routes as risk rises.

Why Article 14 already bites on products on the market

Article 71 staggers the dates on purpose. Article 69(2) says products placed on the market before 11 December 2027 are caught only after a substantial modification. Article 69(3) then derogates from that rule for Article 14: the reporting duties apply to all in-scope products, including those already on the market.

The duties started on 11 September 2026. A company that reads only Article 69(2) concludes it has until December 2027 and is wrong by fifteen months. ENISA opened the single reporting platform required by Article 16 on the same day, so there is a channel to report through.

Procedural history

The Commission proposed the text as COM(2022) 0454 on 15 September 2022. It is procedure 2022/0272(COD). The ITRE committee rapporteur was Nicola Danti (Renew). The committee voted on 19 July 2023 (report A9-0253/2023), Parliament confirmed its mandate to negotiate on 13 September 2023, and the committee approved the trilogue text on 23 January 2024. Parliament adopted its first-reading position T9-0130/2024 on 12 March 2024. The Council adopted the act on 10 October 2024 and it was signed on 23 October 2024. Three corrigenda followed, described in the penalties section.

What it covers

Five areas of the Regulation, in one view

Scope and products
Hardware and software with a direct or indirect data connection, including remote data processing. Medical devices, vehicles and aviation products are excluded (Article 2).
Manufacturer duties and support period
Risk assessment, Annex I requirements, a software bill of materials, and security support for at least five years (Article 13).
Reporting: 24, 72 and 14 (live now)
Actively exploited vulnerabilities and severe incidents go to a CSIRT and ENISA through one platform (Articles 14 and 16).
Risk classes and conformity routes
Default, Class I (19 categories), Class II (4) and critical (3), each with its own assessment route (Articles 7, 8 and 32).
Enforcement and penalties
Market surveillance with withdrawal and recall powers, and fines in three tiers up to EUR 15 million or 2.5% (Articles 52 to 64).

Scope and exclusions

Article 2: connected products in, sectoral products out

What is in

Products with digital elements whose intended or reasonably foreseeable use includes a direct or indirect logical or physical data connection to a device or network. The definition of a product includes its remote data processing solutions (Article 3(1) and (2)), and components placed on the market separately.

Free and open-source software is caught only when supplied in the course of a commercial activity (recitals 15 to 20 and Article 3(22)). Contributing code, or hosting it on a repository, is not enough.

What is out
  • Medical devices under Regulations (EU) 2017/745 and (EU) 2017/746.
  • Vehicles under Regulation (EU) 2019/2144.
  • Aviation products certified under Regulation (EU) 2018/1139.
  • Marine equipment under Directive 2014/90/EU.
  • Spare parts identical to the original.
  • Products developed or modified for national security or defence, and products designed to process classified information.

The Commission may limit or exclude other sectoral cases by delegated act (Article 2(5)).

Who is who

Manufacturer, importer, distributor and open-source steward: Chapter II, Articles 13 to 26

Manufacturer

The party that develops or manufactures a product, or has it designed, and markets it under its own name. It carries the main duties in Article 13 and the reporting duties in Article 14.

Importer and distributor

Each has its own checks before a product is made available, set out in Articles 18 to 23. An importer or distributor that sells under its own name, or substantially modifies a product, becomes a manufacturer (Article 21).

Authorised representative and others

A manufacturer may appoint an authorised representative, but that person cannot take over the manufacturer's duties in Article 13(1) to (11). Any other person who substantially modifies a product and makes it available is treated as a manufacturer for the affected part, or for the whole product (Article 22).

Open-source software steward

A distinct actor with a light regime (Article 3(14) and Article 24): a documented cybersecurity policy, cooperation with authorities, and Article 14 reporting only to the extent the steward is involved in development. There is no CE marking and no fine. Article 24(3) applies from 11 December 2027.

Manufacturer duties

Article 13 and Annex I: what has to be done before and after sale

Article 13 in brief
  • A documented cybersecurity risk assessment that feeds design, development and production.
  • Due diligence on third-party components, including open-source ones.
  • A support period of at least five years, unless the product is expected to be used for less.
  • Each security update kept available for 10 years or the support period, whichever is longer.
  • Vulnerability handling under Annex I Part II, including a software bill of materials covering at least top-level dependencies.
  • A single point of contact for vulnerability reports.
  • The end of the support period (month and year) stated at the time of purchase.
  • Technical documentation and the declaration of conformity kept for 10 years, and notice before ceasing operations.
Annex I Part I: 13 product requirements

Summarised, the product must be placed on the market:

  • with no known exploitable vulnerabilities, and secure by default;
  • with security updates, automatic by default with an opt-out;
  • with access control, and protection of confidentiality and integrity;
  • with data minimisation and protection of availability;
  • with a limited attack surface and exploit mitigation;
  • with logging, with an opt-out, and secure data removal.
Annex I Part II: 8 vulnerability-handling requirements

Summarised, the manufacturer must:

  • identify components and keep a software bill of materials;
  • remediate vulnerabilities without delay, and test regularly;
  • disclose fixed vulnerabilities once a fix is available;
  • run a coordinated vulnerability disclosure policy and share information;
  • distribute updates securely, and free of charge.

Reporting duties (live now)

Article 14 applies from 11 September 2026: 24 hours, 72 hours, then 14 days or one month

Two reportable events

A manufacturer must report an actively exploited vulnerability in its product, and a severe incident having an impact on the security of the product. Both go simultaneously to the CSIRT designated as coordinator and to ENISA, through the single reporting platform set up under Article 16. "Becoming aware" starts the clock, not publication and not confirmation.

Early warning: 24 hours

Within 24 hours of becoming aware. It says that the event has happened and, where applicable, in which Member States the product was made available.

Notification: 72 hours

Within 72 hours. It names the product, the general nature of the exploit or incident, the corrective or mitigating measures taken, the measures users can take, and how sensitive the manufacturer considers the information.

Final report: 14 days

No later than 14 days after a corrective or mitigating measure is available. It describes the vulnerability, its severity and impact, the malicious actor where known, and the security update or other measure.

The single reporting platform (Article 16)

ENISA established and operates the platform and deployed its initial operating capability on 11 September 2026, the day Article 14 began to apply. A manufacturer files one notification. The coordinating CSIRT passes it on to the national CSIRTs of the other Member States where the product is available, and it reaches ENISA at the same time. Article 16(2) allows dissemination to be delayed in particularly exceptional circumstances. The platform is at portal.cra-srp.enisa.europa.eu.

Voluntary reporting (Article 15)

Manufacturers and other persons may also report vulnerabilities and cyber threats voluntarily, under Article 15. ENISA says it will keep improving and expanding the platform's functions over the coming months. Open-source stewards are brought into Article 14 by Article 24(3), which applies from 11 December 2027.

Where the 14 days or one month comes from

The final report for an actively exploited vulnerability is due no later than 14 days after a corrective or mitigating measure is available. For a severe incident, the final report is due within one month of the notification. Check the clause that matches the event before setting a deadline in a compliance calendar.

Risk classes and conformity routes

Articles 7, 8 and 32, with the product lists in Annexes III and IV

Annex III, Class I: 19 categories

Identity and privileged access management, browsers, password managers, anti-malware, VPN products, network management, SIEM, boot managers, public key infrastructure, network interfaces, operating systems, routers and switches, security-capable microprocessors, microcontrollers and ASIC or FPGA devices, smart home virtual assistants, smart home security products, connected toys, and health wearables.

Annex III, Class II: 4 categories

Hypervisors and container runtimes, firewalls and intrusion detection or prevention systems, tamper-resistant microprocessors, and tamper-resistant microcontrollers.

Annex IV, critical: 3 categories

Hardware devices with security boxes, smart meter gateways and similar devices, and smartcards and secure elements. The Commission may make European cybersecurity certification mandatory for them by delegated act (Article 8(1)).

The technical description of each category is set by Implementing Regulation (EU) 2025/2392 under Article 7(4), in force since 21 December 2025.

Which conformity route?

Product group Route What it means
Default products Self-assessment (module A) The manufacturer assesses conformity against the Annex I requirements and signs the declaration itself.
Class I important products Self-assessment only with standards Self-assessment is allowed only if harmonised standards, common specifications or a certification scheme at least at "substantial" level are applied. Otherwise third-party assessment (modules B plus C, or H).
Class II important products Always third-party Modules B plus C, or H, or a European certification scheme at least at "substantial" level.
Critical products Certification if the Commission so decides Mandatory EU certification applies only if the Commission decides so by delegated act (Article 8(1)). Otherwise the Class II routes apply.
Open-source manufacturers of Annex III products Any Article 32(1) route They may choose any route in Article 32(1), provided the technical documentation is made public (Article 32(5)).

A certificate at least at "substantial" level removes the need for the matching third-party assessment (Article 27(9)).

Notified bodies

Chapter IV, Articles 35 to 51: the assessors that third-party routes depend on

Already applying since 11 June 2026

Chapter IV, which governs the notification of conformity assessment bodies, applied from 11 June 2026. Bodies can already apply to be notified, so that they exist when the third-party routes start to be used in December 2027.

The 11 December 2026 target (Article 35(2))

By 11 December 2026, Member States "shall strive to ensure" that enough notified bodies exist. This is a best-efforts target for Member States. It is not an obligation on companies.

Market surveillance, withdrawal and recall

Chapter V, Articles 52 to 60: Regulation 2019/1020 supplies the machinery

Evaluation and corrective action (Article 54)

Where a product presents a significant cybersecurity risk, the market surveillance authority evaluates it and can require corrective action, withdrawal or recall. Other Member States and the Commission have three months to object (Article 54(8)). A dedicated administrative cooperation group (ADCO) coordinates the authorities.

Union-level action (Articles 56 and 57)

In exceptional circumstances the Commission can decide at Union level, with an ENISA evaluation and an implementing act (Article 56). Even compliant products can be restricted if they present significant risks to safety, fundamental rights or the services of essential entities (Article 57).

Formal defects (Article 58)

Defects in the CE marking, the declaration of conformity or the technical documentation must be fixed.

Joint activities and sweeps (Articles 59 and 60)

Authorities can run joint activities, and coordinated sweeps of a product category to check compliance.

Does the CRA have a kill switch?

No. The text contains no shutdown or kill-switch requirement, and no rule on stopping a product remotely. The words are not in the Regulation.

Its strongest powers are withdrawal and recall, which a market surveillance authority can order after evaluating a product (Articles 54 to 57). Those are measures against products on the market. They are not a function built into the product.

Penalties

Article 64: three tiers of administrative fines, set in national law up to these ceilings

Tier Infringement Maximum fine, whichever is higher
Top tier The Annex I essential requirements, or the obligations in Articles 13 and 14 EUR 15 000 000 or 2.5% of total worldwide annual turnover
Middle tier Articles 18 to 23, 28, 30(1) to (4), 31(1) to (4), 32(1) to (3), 33(5), 39, 41, 47, 49 and 53 EUR 10 000 000 or 2% of worldwide annual turnover
Lower tier Supplying incorrect, incomplete or misleading information to notified bodies or market surveillance authorities EUR 5 000 000 or 1% of worldwide annual turnover
The exemptions
  • Microenterprises and small enterprises are not fined for missing the 24-hour early-warning deadline.
  • Open-source software stewards are not fined at all.
  • Member States may not substitute other pecuniary penalties for those entities.
Who applies the fines

Market surveillance authorities apply fines under national law, up to the ceilings above. Member States inform each other through the system in Article 34 of Regulation (EU) 2019/1020, and an explicit proportionality rule governs cumulative fines imposed across Member States.

The 2 July 2025 corrigendum to Article 64(10)

The original text of Article 64(10) said "paragraphs 3 to 9", which looked like a gap. The corrigendum of 2 July 2025 makes it read "paragraphs 2 to 9". The exemptions for micro and small manufacturers (missed 24-hour early warning) and for open-source stewards therefore cover the EUR 15 million tier as well.

The other two corrigenda are 5 December 2024, which corrected the title to cite Regulation (EU) 2019/1020, and 17 October 2025, which corrected the cross-reference in Article 67 to point 72.

How it fits with other acts

The CRA obliges products. Its neighbours oblige entities, agencies and AI systems

NIS 2: Directive (EU) 2022/2555

NIS 2 obliges entities that operate essential and important services. The CRA obliges products. An organisation can be in scope of both, reporting an incident under NIS 2 as an operator and under Article 14 of the CRA as a manufacturer.

AI Act: Regulation (EU) 2024/1689

Under Article 12 of the CRA and recital 51, a high-risk AI system that is also a product with digital elements is deemed to meet the cybersecurity requirements of Article 15 of the AI Act where it meets Annex I Parts I and II and the declaration of conformity shows the Article 15 level. The AI Omnibus, Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, now repeats this as new Article 42(3) of the AI Act. Notified bodies competent under the AI Act can also assess the CRA requirements.

Cybersecurity Act: Regulation (EU) 2019/881

A different Regulation. It sets ENISA's mandate and the European cybersecurity certification framework. It carries no product obligations of its own. The CRA relies on ENISA as a recipient of reports and on certification schemes as one route to conformity.

Market surveillance: Regulation (EU) 2019/1020

It supplies the enforcement machinery, and the CRA amends it. Representative actions under Directive (EU) 2020/1828, which the CRA also amends, can be brought for CRA infringements from 11 December 2027.

Key definition

The term that decides whether the Regulation applies

Product with digital elements (Article 3(1))

"A software or hardware product and its remote data processing solutions, including software or hardware components being placed on the market separately."

Remote data processing means data processing at a distance for which the software is designed and developed by the manufacturer, or under its responsibility, and the absence of which would prevent the product from performing one of its functions (Article 3(2)).

Glossary

Terms used on this page

Product with digital elements
A software or hardware product and its remote data processing solutions, including components placed on the market separately (Article 3(1)).
Important product
A product in Annex III, Class I (19 categories) or Class II (4 categories), which faces a stricter conformity route.
Critical product
A product in Annex IV (3 categories), for which the Commission may require European cybersecurity certification.
Open-source software steward
A legal person, other than a manufacturer, that systematically provides sustained support for the development of specific free and open-source products intended for commercial activities. It has a light regime and is never fined.
CSIRT
Computer security incident response team. The CSIRT designated as coordinator receives CRA notifications and passes them on.
ENISA
The EU Agency for Cybersecurity. It operates the single reporting platform and receives every Article 14 notification.
Single reporting platform
The channel established under Article 16 for Article 14 notifications. Live since 11 September 2026.
Software bill of materials
A machine-readable list of the components in a product. The CRA requires one covering at least the top-level dependencies (Annex I Part II).
Notified body
A conformity assessment body notified by a Member State under Chapter IV, which carries out third-party assessments.
