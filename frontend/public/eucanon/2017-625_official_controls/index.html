The Official Controls Regulation

Regulation (EU) 2017/625 created the EU's single enforcement framework for the entire agri-food chain, replacing a patchwork of older instruments with one set of rules on how competent authorities perform official controls, from farm to fork, applied from 14 December 2019.

Overview

the EU's single enforcement framework for the whole agri-food chain

From patchwork to single framework

Before the Official Controls Regulation (OCR), official controls across the EU agri-food chain were fragmented across a large number of sectoral instruments. Regulation (EC) No 882/2004 covered general food and feed controls; Regulation (EC) No 854/2004 applied specifically to products of animal origin; further separate rules governed plant-health controls, residues controls and animal-by-product controls. Each sector had its own inspection regimes, documentation and laboratory accreditation rules.

The OCR swept this patchwork into a single regulation, creating one harmonised framework that applies across the entire "farm to fork" chain. It does not set the substantive standards themselves (those remain in sectoral law), but it harmonises how those standards are checked and enforced across all Member States, at home and at the border, with common rules on competent authorities, financing, laboratories, certification, cross-border administrative assistance, Commission audits and penalties.

Legal bases: three pillars of TFEU authority

The OCR rests on three TFEU legal bases, mirroring the companion Animal Health Law (Reg 2016/429):

  • Article 43(2) TFEU (common agricultural policy): the core basis for rules governing the agri-food production chain
  • Article 114 TFEU (internal market approximation): justifies harmonised enforcement to avoid distortions between Member States
  • Article 168(4)(b) TFEU (public health, veterinary and phytosanitary measures): anchors the food and feed safety dimension, including zoonoses and pesticide residues
Entry into force and application

The regulation was adopted on 15 March 2017 and published in OJ L 95 on 7 April 2017. It entered into force on the twentieth day after publication, namely 27 April 2017. The general application date was 14 December 2019, giving Member States and operators time to adapt. EU reference laboratories began applying the new framework from 29 April 2018. Certain plant-health provisions started applying only from 29 April 2022 (Article 167).

The ten areas of scope

Article 1(2): the fields in which the OCR applies to official controls

a
Food and food safety
Food and food safety, integrity and wholesomeness at every stage, including rules on food-contact materials and labelling.
b
GMOs for food and feed
Deliberate release of genetically modified organisms intended for food or feed production and placing on the market.
c
Feed and feed safety
Feed safety and rules on feed for food-producing animals, including feed business operators and authorised substances.
d
Animal health
Requirements on animal health, including transmissible animal diseases and the Animal Health Law (Reg 2016/429) framework.
e
Animal by-products
Rules on animal by-products and derived products not intended for human consumption (Reg (EC) No 1069/2009).
f
Animal welfare
Requirements on animal welfare, including rules on the protection of animals during transport and at the time of killing.
g
Plant health
Protective measures against pests of plants, including the Plant Health Regulation (Reg (EU) 2016/2031) framework.
h
Plant protection products
Plant protection products (pesticides) and the sustainable use of pesticides, excluding application equipment.
i
Organic production
Organic production and labelling of organic products, including certification bodies and conversion periods.
j
PDO / PGI / TSG
Protected designations of origin (PDO), protected geographical indications (PGI) and traditional specialities guaranteed (TSG) under Reg (EU) No 1151/2012.

What is NOT in scope

The OCR does not apply to controls relating to the common organisation of agricultural markets (Regulation (EU) No 1308/2013), except anti-fraud marketing-standard checks. It also does not apply to animals used for scientific purposes or to veterinary medicinal products.

Official controls vs other official activities

Article 2: two distinct categories of what competent authorities do

Official controls

Activities performed by competent authorities to verify that operators and animals or goods comply with the agri-food rules, including for the purpose of issuing official certificates or attestations. The definition covers all inspection, sampling, testing and audit activities directed at checking compliance.

Examples: farm inspection, slaughterhouse ante-mortem and post-mortem examination, sampling of food products for laboratory testing, documentary checks at the border, audits of organic certification bodies.

Other official activities

Everything else competent authorities do under the Union agri-food chain rules that is not an official control, including disease and pest surveillance; eradication and containment programmes; granting or withdrawing authorisations and approvals; issuing official certificates and attestations when not done as part of an official control; and maintaining official registers.

Examples: maintaining a list of approved slaughterhouses, running a brucellosis eradication programme, issuing a plant health certificate for export, granting organic operator status.

How controls work

Titles II to III: competent authorities, risk-based controls, delegation and laboratories

Competent authorities (Article 4)

Member States designate one or more competent authorities for each of the ten areas in scope. Designated authorities must be impartial, professionally competent and adequately resourced in terms of staff, equipment, laboratory access and legal powers. They may delegate specific tasks to other public bodies, to ISO-accredited bodies or to natural persons, subject to the conditions in Articles 28 to 33. The OCR requires each Member State to maintain a multi-annual national control plan (MANCP) covering all ten areas, which is submitted to and audited by the Commission.

Risk-based, unannounced and documented (Articles 9, 13)

Controls are performed at appropriate and risk-based frequencies, taking into account the operator's compliance history, the risk associated with the animals, goods, substances or activities concerned, and any information suggesting non-compliance. Controls are performed without prior notice except in justified cases. Every control visit must be documented in writing (Article 13): the competent authority must produce a report covering the purpose and method of the control, what was found, and any measures ordered. The operator receives a copy of the report.

Operator cooperation and transparency (Article 15)

Operators must cooperate with competent authorities: granting access to establishments, equipment, animals and records, and providing any requested information. Member States may publish the results of official controls, maintain operator rating schemes and allow consumers and third parties to see inspection outcomes. This transparency requirement is designed to create market incentives for compliance.

Official laboratories and the second expert opinion (Articles 34 to 42)

Samples collected during official controls are analysed by official laboratories that are accredited to EN ISO/IEC 17025 for each method they use (Article 37). Official laboratories may be designated at national or EU reference laboratory level. Operators are entitled to a second expert opinion on the laboratory findings, at their own expense, provided the sample taken permits it (Article 35). Competent authorities performing controls on online trade may use anonymous mystery shopping, including test purchases from online platforms, to check compliance without revealing the control in advance (Article 36).

Delegation to accredited bodies (Articles 28 to 33)

Member States may delegate specific official control tasks to bodies that hold accreditation for those tasks under ISO/IEC 17020 (inspection bodies), provided the body is independent, impartial and free of conflicts of interest, and provided the competent authority retains oversight and the ability to withdraw delegation. Delegation is not permitted for tasks requiring the exercise of public powers (such as enforcement actions) or for tasks in connection with official certificates that must be issued by an official veterinarian.

Border controls

Title II, Chapter V: border control posts, CHED and the unified entry system

Border control posts (BCPs)

Animals and goods from third countries that are subject to official controls at entry must pass through designated border control posts (BCPs) before release for free circulation in the Union. BCPs are designated by Member States and must meet minimum requirements for infrastructure, facilities and staffing. The OCR replaced the previously fragmented system, which had separate veterinary check points and plant-health inspection posts operating under different rules, with a single integrated regime covering all categories of animals, products and goods within its scope.

The three types of check

At BCPs, consignments undergo three types of check:

  • Documentary check: verification that the required certificates, attestations, commercial documents and other accompanying documents are present, authentic and correct
  • Identity check: visual inspection to confirm that the consignment matches the documentation, including official marks and labels
  • Physical check: examination of the animals or goods themselves, which may include temperature checks, sensory testing, sampling and laboratory analysis. Physical checks are performed at risk-based frequencies set by the Commission
The Common Health Entry Document (CHED)

Each consignment that is subject to border controls must be pre-notified by the responsible operator using the Common Health Entry Document (CHED) in the IMSOC system, before the consignment's expected arrival at the BCP. The CHED is then used by the BCP authority to record the outcome of documentary, identity and physical checks. A completed CHED is required for customs clearance, linking the sanitary control outcome directly to the customs process. The CHED replaced a variety of predecessor documents (veterinary entry documents, phytosanitary inspection certificates, etc.) with a single form covering all categories.

Financing through fees

Articles 78 to 83: mandatory fees, cost coverage and permitted reductions

Mandatory fee collection

Competent authorities must collect fees or charges for official controls in the sectors listed in Annex IV Chapter II, and for border controls on certain animals and goods. Fees may be set either at cost (Article 82: calculated against actual costs of the control) or at the Annex IV amounts (Article 79: flat amounts per category published in the Annex). The OCR makes fee collection mandatory, removing the discretion that had existed under the predecessor Regulation (EC) No 882/2004 and that had led to distortions between Member States.

Costs that fees may cover (Article 81)

Fees must cover, but must not exceed, the costs attributable to the official controls in question. Permitted cost elements include:

  • Staff salaries, overheads and equipment
  • Facilities used for official controls
  • Consumables and laboratory costs
  • Costs of delegated body services
  • Training, travel and accommodation
  • Sampling and analysis costs

Permitted reductions

Member States may reduce fees for operators with a good compliance record, for operators in geographically constrained regions (remote areas with high transport costs), for operators using traditional methods (e.g. artisan production) and for operators with low throughput relative to the fixed costs of maintaining control capacity. Direct or indirect refunds of fees are prohibited under the areas covered by Annex IV. Fees do not apply to organic production controls or to PDO/PGI/TSG controls.

Transparency

Member States must ensure that the method for calculating fees is transparent and publicly available, so that operators can understand the basis on which they are charged. The Commission reviews the Annex IV amounts periodically and may update them by delegated act.

Certification, reference labs, IMSOC and assistance

Titles III to VI: official certificates, EU reference laboratories, the integrated information system and cross-border cooperation

Official certificates and attestations

Articles 86 to 91 set out common reliability rules for official certificates, official attestations and other official documents. These include plant passports under plant-health law, organic logos, identification marks for products of animal origin and PDO/PGI marks. Certificates must be issued by authorised or official staff and may be issued electronically through IMSOC. The OCR establishes minimum requirements on the information to be included and the conditions for issuing, amending or withdrawing certificates.

EU reference laboratories and reference centres

The Commission designates EU reference laboratories for each sector (Articles 92 to 101), responsible for providing national reference laboratories with validated methods, reference materials and proficiency testing. The OCR also introduced two new types of institution: EU reference centres for the authenticity and integrity of the agri-food chain (covering fraud detection) and EU reference centres for animal welfare, recognising these as cross-cutting areas that cut across multiple sectoral reference laboratories.

IMSOC: the integrated information system

The Information Management System for Official Controls (IMSOC) is the single computerised platform integrating and upgrading the systems previously operated separately: TRACES (animal movement and certification); the Rapid Alert System for Food and Feed (RASFF) (cross-border food and feed safety alerts); the animal-disease notification system; and the plant-pest notification system. All CHED processing, prior notifications and official certificate exchange run through IMSOC.

Administrative assistance and cooperation (Articles 102 to 108)

Member States designate liaison bodies to cooperate with counterparts in other Member States when non-compliance is suspected of crossing borders, particularly in cases of suspected fraud. The OCR provides for requests for information, requests for controls and follow-up checks. It goes beyond the scope of RASFF, which covers imminent food and feed safety risks, to address the broader area of cross-border non-compliance including commercial fraud and deliberate mislabelling.

MANCP and Commission audits

Each Member State must prepare and implement a Multi-Annual National Control Plan (MANCP) covering all ten areas of the OCR, setting out the organisation of controls, risk-categorisation criteria, training arrangements and contingency plans. Member States submit annual reports to the Commission on implementation. The Commission performs audits in Member States (and in third countries seeking or holding trade access) to verify that the MANCP is being followed and that controls are effective. Commission audit findings may trigger recommendations or, in cases of serious systemic failure, safeguard measures.

Enforcement and penalties

Title VII, Articles 138 to 141: corrective action, proportionate penalties and whistleblower protection

Competent authority response to non-compliance (Article 138)

When competent authorities identify non-compliance, they must investigate the cause and extent and take all measures necessary to ensure the operator remedies the situation and prevents its recurrence. Available measures range from orders to remedy the situation within a set period (for minor non-compliance) through to immediate suspension of activity, withdrawal of approval or authorisation, slaughter or disposal of animals or products, and seizure, for serious or persistent non-compliance. Actions taken must be proportionate to the nature and severity of the non-compliance.

Penalties: effective, proportionate, dissuasive (Article 139)

Member States must lay down penalties for infringements and take all measures to ensure they are applied. Penalties must be effective, proportionate and dissuasive. For infringements motivated by economic gain, including food fraud, financial penalties must be calculated to reflect at least the economic advantage obtained by the infringement, or as a percentage of the operator's turnover. Member States had to notify their penalty rules to the Commission by 14 December 2019. The OCR does not itself set criminal or administrative penalties; it requires Member States to do so.

Whistleblower protection (Article 140)

Member States must establish reporting mechanisms allowing any person to report actual or potential infringements of the agri-food chain rules to competent authorities. Persons who report in good faith must be protected against retaliation: victimisation, discrimination or adverse treatment in the reporting context is prohibited. This provision reflects the growing role of internal and supply-chain whistleblowers in detecting food fraud and animal-welfare violations.

Commission safeguard measures (Article 141)

Where a Member State's control system suffers serious disruption creating a risk to human, animal or plant health or to animal welfare, the Commission may take safeguard measures by implementing act, including ordering that Member State to suspend certain official activities, prohibiting imports from certain third countries or requiring additional controls on goods from that Member State. This power is exceptional and reserved for situations of systemic breakdown.

Repeals and amendments

Article 146 and Recital 92: what the OCR replaced and what it updated

Instruments repealed

The OCR repealed two major regulations and seven Council Directives and Decisions:

  • Regulation (EC) No 882/2004 on official controls for food and feed law, animal health and animal welfare rules
  • Regulation (EC) No 854/2004 laying down specific rules for official controls on products of animal origin intended for human consumption
  • Seven Directives and Decisions covering veterinary checks at border inspection posts, animal health checks on intra-Union trade, and import conditions for certain animals and products
Instruments amended

The OCR also amended a series of sectoral acts to align their control and enforcement provisions with the new framework, including:

  • Regulation (EC) No 999/2001 (TSEs: transmissible spongiform encephalopathies)
  • Regulation (EC) No 1/2005 (protection of animals during transport)
  • Regulation (EC) No 396/2005 (pesticide maximum residue levels)
  • Regulation (EC) No 1069/2009 (animal by-products)
  • Regulation (EC) No 1099/2009 (protection of animals at the time of killing)
  • Regulation (EC) No 1107/2009 (plant protection products)
  • Regulation (EU) No 1151/2012 (quality schemes: PDO, PGI, TSG)
  • Several animal-welfare directives

Key definitions

Essential terms from Article 3 of Regulation (EU) 2017/625

Official control
Any activity performed by a competent authority to verify that operators, animals and goods comply with the Union agri-food chain rules, including for the purpose of issuing official certificates or attestations (Article 3(1)).
Other official activity
Any activity other than an official control performed by competent authorities under the agri-food chain rules, including disease and pest surveillance, eradication programmes, granting authorisations and issuing certificates not done as part of an official control (Article 3(2)).
Competent authority
A central or regional authority of a Member State designated to perform official controls or other official activities in one of the ten areas of scope; or any body to which such tasks have been officially delegated (Article 3(3)).
Border control post (BCP)
A location designated by a Member State for the performance of official controls on animals and goods entering the Union from third countries, meeting the minimum infrastructure and staffing requirements set by the Commission (Article 3(38)).
CHED
Common Health Entry Document: the official document used to pre-notify the arrival of a consignment at a BCP, record the outcome of border checks and obtain customs clearance. A single CHED replaces the multiple predecessor entry documents (Article 3(41)).
IMSOC
Information Management System for Official Controls: the single computerised system that integrates TRACES, RASFF, the animal-disease notification system and the plant-pest notification system into one platform for managing official controls and border notifications (Article 3(31)).
Official laboratory
A laboratory designated by a competent authority to analyse samples taken during official controls, accredited to EN ISO/IEC 17025 for the relevant analytical methods (Article 3(26)).
MANCP
Multi-Annual National Control Plan: the document each Member State must prepare and implement, setting out the organisation and prioritisation of official controls in all ten areas of scope over a multi-year period, submitted to and audited by the Commission (Article 3(62)).

Glossary

Key abbreviations and instruments referenced in the Official Controls Regulation

OCR
The Official Controls Regulation: short name for Regulation (EU) 2017/625, the EU's single enforcement framework for the whole agri-food chain.
RASFF
Rapid Alert System for Food and Feed: the EU cross-border alert system for food and feed safety hazards, now integrated into IMSOC alongside TRACES and the disease-notification systems.
TRACES
Trade Control and Expert System: the EU integrated computerised veterinary system for the notification, certification and traceability of intra-Union movements and third-country entries, now a component of IMSOC under the OCR.
PDO / PGI / TSG
Protected Designation of Origin / Protected Geographical Indication / Traditional Speciality Guaranteed: the three quality scheme categories under Regulation (EU) No 1151/2012, whose controls fall within the OCR's scope.
EFSA
European Food Safety Authority: provides independent scientific risk assessments underpinning EU food and feed safety rules, whose implementation the OCR's official controls verify.
SCoPAFF
Standing Committee on Plants, Animals, Food and Feed: the comitology committee that assists the Commission in adopting implementing acts under the OCR and under much of the sectoral agri-food chain legislation.
GMO
Genetically Modified Organism: the deliberate release of GMOs for food and feed production is one of the ten areas of scope under Article 1(2)(b) of the OCR.
ISO/IEC 17025
The international standard for laboratory testing and calibration competence. All official laboratories designated under the OCR must be accredited to this standard for each method they apply.
TFEU
Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union. The OCR rests on three TFEU legal bases: Article 43(2) (common agricultural policy), Article 114 (internal market) and Article 168(4)(b) (public health, veterinary and phytosanitary).

Timeline

From adoption in 2017 to general application in 2019 and the plant-health provisions in 2022

15 March 2017
Regulation (EU) 2017/625 adopted at Strasbourg. Published in OJ L 95 on 7 April 2017.
27 April 2017
Entry into force: the twentieth day after publication in OJ L 95 (Article 167). The Commission begins adopting the delegated and implementing acts needed to implement the new framework.
29 April 2018
EU reference laboratories start applying the new framework. Member States begin adapting national official laboratory accreditation systems to meet the ISO/IEC 17025 requirement.
14 December 2019
General application: the OCR applies in full across all ten areas of scope. Regulations (EC) No 882/2004 and No 854/2004 are repealed. Member States must have notified their penalty rules to the Commission by this date. CHED replaces predecessor entry documents at BCPs.
29 April 2022
Certain plant-health provisions under the OCR start applying, aligned with the application timeline of the Plant Health Regulation (EU) 2016/2031.

eu_animal_health_sps family
Regulation (EU) 2016/429: the Animal Health Law
The substantive EU framework on transmissible animal diseases: disease categorisation (A to E), operator biosecurity obligations, surveillance, eradication programmes, traceability via TRACES, intra-Union movements and third-country entry conditions. The Official Controls Regulation is the framework through which Member States enforce it. Understanding both instruments together gives the full picture of how the EU controls animal disease from the farm gate to the border.

Sibling instruments coming to the canon

As Brubru analyses other instruments in the eu_animal_health_sps family and the wider agri-food acquis, they will appear here. The most closely related are the Plant Health Law (Regulation (EU) 2016/2031), the General Food Law (Regulation (EC) No 178/2002) and the Animal Welfare transport and killing regulations. Each of those sets substantive standards that the Official Controls Regulation enforces.
