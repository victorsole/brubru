What the KIDS Act is

Two halves that only work together

On 17 September 2026 the Commission tabled a proposal for a Regulation with a name built to make an acronym: the EU KIDS Act, for Keeping Internet Digital Spaces Accountable and Trustworthy. It came with a Communication setting out the wider strategy and a Staff Working Document analysing the impacts. The legal basis is Article 114 TFEU, the single market clause, which matters more than it sounds: the Commission is not claiming a child protection competence it does not have, it is claiming that a patchwork of national age limits is fragmenting the internal market.

Common age rules

One minimum age for the whole Union, replacing what would otherwise become 27 different ones. No account of your own below 15 on the riskiest social and video services, a supervised account a guardian can open from 13, and nothing at all below 13 except a narrow, parent held exception on services built for small children.

Safety by design

A binding list of things a service may not do to a minor: no infinite scroll without breaks, no engagement bait notifications, no streak mechanics, no recommender system optimised for attention, no contact from strangers, no beauty filters that idealise a child's face, no loot boxes. This half applies to every service in scope regardless of size, including the ones a 16 year old is perfectly entitled to use.

Why the second half is the important one

The Commission's own Q&A makes the point bluntly: age limits alone would leave the underlying problem untouched, because the problem is services designed to maximise a child's time and attention. The age rules are what the newspapers will lead on. The design rules are what will actually change the products.

Formally the Regulation does not replace anything. It specifies Article 28 of the Digital Services Act, which already obliges online platforms to ensure a high level of privacy, safety and security for minors, and it complements the AI Act for AI companions and chatbots. In practice it takes the Commission's non-binding July 2025 guidelines on the protection of minors and turns them into hard law with fines attached.

The age staircase

Articles 6 and 7. Four steps, not one cliff edge

The structure everybody gets wrong on first reading is that this is not a single ban at a single age. It is a sequence of four regimes, and the child moves up a step as they grow. Note in particular that between 13 and 15 the account belongs to the guardian, not to the child: the minor is not a party to the contract with the provider.

Under 3
Nothing
Guardian controlled access cannot be enabled at all below the age of 3. The Communication goes further and advises no screens whatsoever, though that is advice, not law.

Accounts that already exist

Within six months of the rules applying, providers must work out whether existing account holders are under 15 and disable the accounts of those who are, and of those whose age cannot be established. That second limb is the sharp one. Where a provider can already tell with high confidence that a holder is an adult, for example from the year the account was created or a long standing credit card on file, no new check is needed, so most adults should notice nothing. Very large platforms must file a plan in advance setting out how they intend to reach that confidence, with accuracy, precision, recall and a full confusion matrix by age bucket.

Which services are caught

Article 2 for the scope, Article 6(1) for the five triggers

Seven categories of service or system fall inside the Regulation, and small and micro enterprises are not exempt. The Commission's reasoning is that in digital markets headcount is a poor proxy for reach, and exempting the small would simply push risky activity towards them.

Article 2(1)(a) and (b)
Social networking and video sharing
Definitions borrowed from the Digital Markets Act. These are the only two categories that carry the minimum age.
Article 2(1)(c)
Software application stores
Age rating duties and a requirement to carry the EU age verification solution.
Article 2(1)(d)
Online games
Both video gaming platforms and individual video games, on PC, mobile or console. Games sold only on physical media with no online component are out.
Article 2(1)(e)
Operating systems
One narrow duty only: share an age signal you already hold, with the user's consent, so nobody is checked twice.
Article 2(1)(f)
AI companions
Systems providing sustained, personalised interaction that simulates a social, emotional or interpersonal relationship.
Article 2(1)(g)
General conversational chatbots
General purpose assistants across multiple domains. Specialised customer service, technical support, educational and industrial applications are expressly excluded.

The five features that trigger the minimum age

The under 15 rule does not apply to every social or video service. It applies to one that has any of five features, listed in Article 6(1). This is the provision lobbyists will spend the next two years on, because a service that can shed all five escapes the age limit entirely while remaining bound by the design rules.

Six carve-outs

Out of scope entirely: not for profit online encyclopaedias; not for profit educational and scientific repositories; services designed primarily for education and run by or for educational establishments; open source software development and sharing platforms, unless the platform is itself an AI system in scope; services built solely for scientific research and development; and services designed and operated by public authorities for their own exclusive use. The Commission may add to or subtract from this list by delegated act.

Safety by design

Articles 8 to 13. The list of things a product may no longer do

Article 8 sets the frame. Every provider in scope must design for a high level of privacy, safety and security by default, whether or not the user has an account, and may only drop those defaults once it has established through age assurance that the person is an adult. Logged out visitors get the child settings.

Article 9: addictive design

Four techniques are deemed by law to encourage compulsive or excessive use, so no argument about intent is available:

  • Automatic play and uninterrupted consumption without real, regular stopping points that let the child decide whether to carry on
  • Undermining the child's decision to stop, including notifications not triggered by anything the child did
  • Rewarding a child for posting or broadcasting to a mass audience
  • Incentivising engagement at regular intervals, including penalties or lost benefits for not coming back, which is streak mechanics named without being named

Providers must also supply effective time limits and usage interruptions designed around school time and core sleep hours, which recital 27 defines as at least eight consecutive hours between 22:00 and 08:00 local time. The recital also insists these tools must not be easily circumvented, because in practice children override them.

Article 10: recommender systems

For minors, the feed must be optimised for safety, quality and mental health outcomes rather than engagement:

  • Explicit, user stated preferences get priority and primary weight
  • Recommendation from implicit engagement signals, meaning dwell time and click through, is off by default
  • No personal data collected from outside the service may feed the system
  • No exposure to content that is harmful when encountered repeatedly, which is the rabbit hole and filter bubble problem written into an obligation

Children must also get a prominent reset that deletes every learned preference, and at least one feed option with no profiling at all. That option must be offered at account creation, must stay reachable from the feed itself, and must not be designed to nudge the child back towards profiling.

Article 11: safe settings

Off by default for minors: geolocation and other tracking; microphone and camera access; recommendations of other accounts and contact synchronisation; push notifications, which in any event must protect sleep and school hours.

Some things are not merely off, they are unavailable: features that increase social comparison, and filters that misrepresent a child's image by disproportionately embellishing or idealising it. Recital 29 is careful to spare the harmless ones that turn you into an animal.

Article 12: contact and visibility

Nobody may open direct contact with a child who has not pre-approved it, and the provider must actively guard against children being manipulated into giving that approval. Children do not appear in contact suggestions, cannot be added to a group without agreeing, and can block anyone without their identity being revealed to the person blocked. Their content is visible only to accepted contacts and never to logged out visitors, their contact details are never disclosed, others cannot download or screenshot their content, and minors cannot host live streams by default.

Article 13: money

Before a transaction happens the child must be told, clearly and in real time, that it is a transaction. Purchases made with virtual currency must display the real value in the currency of the child's country of habitual residence. Services may not be built in a way that leads to excessive, impulsive or unwanted spending, and minors may not be exposed to variable reward systems, which is loot boxes described by their mechanism.

Three further duties round out the chapter. Article 18 requires every control, warning and explanation to be genuinely understandable by a child, and requires very large platforms to provide them in the official languages of the Member States they serve. Article 19 requires child friendly reporting that is handled as a priority, with confirmation, timelines and a route to national helplines and Safer Internet Centres. Article 21 lets a child or guardian mandate a not for profit body to act for them, and adds the Regulation to the annex of the Representative Actions Directive so collective redress is available.

AI companions and chatbots

Article 14. The genuinely new part

This is where the KIDS Act goes beyond codifying existing guidance. The AI Act already prohibits systems that exploit vulnerabilities due to age and requires disclosure that you are talking to a machine, but it says nothing specific about a chatbot that behaves like a friend. Article 14 does.

No manufactured attachment

Providers must avoid design features and system behaviours that simulate interpersonal relations and are likely to create emotional dependencies, including where such dependency would damage the child's relationships with other people or their development. The SWD is explicit about the mechanism it fears: systems optimised to please, agree and mirror, which for a minor reinforces immature or risky beliefs rather than challenging them.

Memory off by default

By default the system may not carry information or analysis from a child's earlier conversations into later ones, except where that is necessary to protect the child's safety. The stated reason is twofold: to stop sensitive data about a minor accumulating, and to stop harmful interaction patterns being reinforced over time.

Tested before launch, watched after

State of the art evaluation and testing for risks to a minor's health, safety, fundamental rights and development before the system is placed on the market, plus post market monitoring including serious incident response afterwards. Micro and small enterprises are exempt from the post market monitoring limb only.

Built into something else

Where a companion or chatbot appears as a feature inside a social network, a video platform or a game, it may not switch itself on, may not be displayed prominently, may not be pushed at minors, and must be easy to turn off at any time. Recital 34 names the specific trick it is aimed at: putting the AI at the top of the child's contact list.

Under 13s may reach these systems only through guardian tools. Addictive design, safe settings and transaction transparency all carry over from Articles 9, 11 and 13. Enforcement runs through the AI Act rather than the DSA, which is why the 6 per cent turnover fine is spelled out explicitly for this category.

Games and app stores

Articles 15 to 17

Games get a tailored subset of the design rules: no compulsive use mechanics, safe default settings, contact safeguards, and mandatory guardian tools with under 13 access only through them. One obligation is specific to the medium: a game must not be usable as a springboard to pull a child onto another, riskier service, which in practice means restricting link outs and warning the player. Where a platform lets users build and publish their own games, the platform must give those creators the software and organisational means to comply.

App stores carry the gatekeeping duty. Every application must be age rated, the methodology, criteria and sources must be published, and a minor must not be able to reach or buy anything rated above their age. Where the store becomes aware that an app consists mainly of content subject to a higher statutory age in a given Member State, it must block minors from that country. Stores must also allow the EU age verification solution to be offered inside the store.

PEGI, named in a recital

Article 17 asks the Commission to facilitate a Union level code of conduct on age rating within a year of application, covering mutual recognition across Member States, criteria for violent, sexual, gambling and self harm content, in-app purchases, contact risk and addictive design, plus an independent appeals route for developers. Recital 39 names the pan-European PEGI system as a benchmark that could form the basis of such a code, provided it reaches the level of protection the Regulation requires. Adherence to an adequate code can evidence compliance with the measure based obligations, but never with the prohibitions.

Proving your age without revealing who you are

Chapter V, Articles 27 to 32

Everything above depends on knowing roughly how old the user is, and that is the part most likely to decide whether this Regulation is remembered as a child protection measure or as an identity mandate. The proposal is built to survive that charge.

Article 27
Seven cumulative criteria
Accuracy, reliability, security, robustness, non-intrusiveness, privacy and data protection, and non-discrimination. Fail one and the solution is not compliant.
Article 27
Self-declaration is dead
Ticking a date of birth box is explicitly excluded from the definition of age assurance. The OECD found only 2 of 50 services studied systematically check age at sign-up.
Article 28
Zero knowledge proof, required
The check may not identify, locate, track, target, advertise to or profile anyone. No combining with other data. The answer transmitted is above or below the threshold, nothing more.
Article 29
Two tiers of check
For the age limit itself, only a certified EU age verification solution from an independent third party. For safety by design, cheaper methods such as age estimation are allowed if they meet Articles 27 and 28.
Article 31
What Member States must build
At least one free EU age verification solution, several routes to a proof of age attestation, and at least one privacy preserving way for a guardian to prove parental responsibility, free of charge and drawn from authentic national sources.
Article 31
Nobody left out
Access must be effective for people with disabilities, people with limited digital skills, and families whose situation is not in ordinary civil status records, expressly including refugee and displaced families and guardians of unaccompanied minors.
Any method employed to check age should uphold the highest privacy and data protection standards and should not lead to the processing of identity documents and biometric data for the purpose of age estimation. Fegert and Melchior, co-chairs of the Special Panel on child safety online, quoted in the Staff Working Document

The architecture rests on the European Digital Identity Wallet. A wallet certified under the eIDAS framework and meeting the EU Age Verification Scheme counts as certified for this Regulation too, and the Commission maintains two public machine readable lists, one of approved attestation providers and one of approved verification solutions. A solution listed by one Member State must be recognised by all of them.

The lesson the Commission took from Australia

Australia's law, in force since December 2025, requires only reasonable steps to prevent under 16s from keeping accounts. The SWD reports that most minors who had accounts still have them, and the reason it gives is blunt: most of them were never asked to prove their age at all. Hence the European choice to require the check before the account is created rather than after a suspicion is raised.

What parents get, and what they are not asked to carry

Articles 20, 26 and 31

Article 20 requires every service in scope to provide guardian tools that are effective, accessible, easy to activate and, importantly, not easily circumvented or undermined by the design of the service itself. Changing a setting must require the same level of authorisation as turning the tool on in the first place, which closes the loophole of a child quietly switching supervision off.

The minimum feature set:

  • Screen time limits, operating through the same mechanism as the Article 9 time management duty
  • Seeing and managing the accounts the child communicates with
  • Managing privacy, contact, visibility, notification, geolocation and camera settings
  • Reporting harmful content, accounts, groups or behaviour on the child's behalf

Recital 43 suggests further features, such as alerting a guardian to repeated searches about suicide, self harm or eating disorders, or to a new contact who is an adult. On accounts for 13 and 14 year olds these tools are permanently on. For very large platforms they must interoperate with third party parental control tools under the conditions of Article 6 of the Digital Markets Act.

Two safeguards pulling in opposite directions

Towards the child. Minors must be told when a guardian tool is active, and given a clear real time indication where monitoring is running. Tools must respect the child's privacy and growing autonomy and must not disproportionately restrict their rights.

Away from the parent. Recital 43 states that because parental oversight is not always possible and guardians may be absent or disengaged, compliance may not depend on guardians. These tools complement the provider's own duty. They never replace it.

Proving parental responsibility is handled in Article 26. A provider may use signals from freely accessible official databases, signals it already holds from past dealings with the family, or, at least until the Commission adopts the relevant delegated act, a self declaration that the provider must make reasonable efforts to check. All of it has to be privacy preserving, with no additional processing that would let anyone locate, track, target or profile the adult or the child.

Enforcement, and who pays for it

Article 5 and Chapter VII

No new agency is created. Supervision rides on the Digital Services Act for platforms, stores and gaming platforms, and on the AI Act for companions and chatbots. The Commission keeps exclusive competence over very large online platforms, national Digital Services Coordinators handle the rest, and for a video game that is not a platform the competent authority is that of the provider's main establishment, with exclusive powers.

The reversed burden: Article 5

The Special Panel asked for the burden of proof to shift onto providers. This is the Commission's answer. A very large online platform must file a compliance plan with the Commission, within four months of designation or within 30 days of the Regulation applying if it is already designated, describing in detail how it meets every obligation in Chapters II to V.

That plan must be audited, at the provider's own expense, by independent auditors who have, or retain experts with, proven expertise across six named fields: child protection and rights, paediatric medicine and child psychiatry, developmental science, age assurance, interface and recommender design, and data protection and security. The auditor sends a draft, the provider has 15 days to comment, and the final report goes to the Commission and the provider simultaneously within two months. A summary must be published.

If the Commission decides the plan has shortcomings, the provider has 30 days to produce corrective measures and no more than 60 days to implement each of them. Crucially, Article 5(8) states that neither the auditor's report nor Commission silence constitutes a finding of compliance. The plan is a floor, not a shield.

A funding gap the Commission admits in writing

The financial statement concedes an inevitable gap between needing the staff on day one and collecting the fee that pays for them, which only arrives after entry into force. It calls this a critical risk for correct implementation and names redeployment as the mitigation. The existing DSA supervisory fee has raised between EUR 40 and 60 million a year since 2024.

Thirteen, fifteen or sixteen

The argument that will define the file

Three institutions did the work and arrived at three different numbers. That is the whole negotiation in one line.

WhoWhenNumberReasoning
Special Panel co-chairs July 2026 Under 13 A developmental approach. Research shows children are especially vulnerable below 13.
European Commission September 2026 15 Adolescents between 13 and 15 are at the peak of developmental vulnerability: still forming cognitive control, highly sensitive to social evaluation, and beyond the reach of effective adult supervision.
European Parliament November 2025 16 A higher precautionary threshold, with 13 as an absolute floor where a guardian authorises access.

The Commission went above its own expert panel and below the Parliament. The SWD sets out why: until 15, adolescents are transitioning into autonomy, supervision by caregivers and educators is becoming less effective, and risk is increasingly shaped by the social architecture of the service rather than by the child's own behaviour.

The quieter fight: full harmonisation

The Special Panel's recommendation 7 was that Member States should be free to add their own precautionary restrictions from 13. The Commission declined it. The SWD says so in terms: the proposed Regulation does not provide flexibility for Member States to introduce additional restrictions, because full harmonisation is what delivers legal certainty, lower compliance costs and an equal level of protection. Capitals that have already legislated, or are about to, will notice that this ceiling is also a floor they cannot build above.

What it will cost

The Staff Working Document's numbers, and their limits

There is no formal impact assessment. The SWD is an analysis of impacts that borrows from the assessments done for the DSA, the DMA, the AI Act and others, plus an external study on age assurance whose results are not due until the fourth quarter of 2026. That is worth stating plainly, because it is an obvious line of attack for the Parliament and for the Regulatory Scrutiny Board.

The Commission's central argument on cost is comparative rather than absolute: without this Regulation, providers would face up to 27 different regimes with different ages, scopes, parental consent rules and technical requirements. It also expects the EU Age Verification Solution, being publicly funded and open source, to end up the cheapest route available, since its development costs do not have to be recovered per check.

Two honest admissions sit in the text. Small and micro enterprises will feel the relative burden more even where the absolute cost is lower, because they have less legal and engineering capacity. And around 80 per cent of EU video game companies are small or micro firms, which is why the games industry is likely to be the loudest voice in the room.

What the evidence does not say

The SWD is more careful than the press material. On the key causal question it notes that the association between delayed access and better outcomes at 13 can be read as an association rather than definitive proof of a causal age-specific effect. It also concedes that effects are not uniform, varying by service, by design feature and by individual circumstance, and that more active use such as posting and interacting tends to correlate with better wellbeing than passive scrolling. Anyone quoting this file should quote that caveat too.

One difference that is not a typing error

The Communication and the Q&A both lead with the line that the Act prohibits social media accounts under 13. The legal text does something stronger: Article 6(1) is a flat prohibition below 15, with a derogation down to 13 that a guardian may choose to use. Both statements are defensible, but the communications team led with the smaller number.
