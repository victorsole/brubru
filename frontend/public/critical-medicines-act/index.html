What is the Critical Medicines Act?

A proposed Regulation to address endemic medicine shortages and heavy API dependence outside the EU

The core proposition

The Critical Medicines Act is a proposed EU Regulation laying a framework for strengthening the availability and security of supply of critical medicinal products, as well as the availability of and accessibility of medicinal products of common interest. It amends Regulation (EU) 2024/795 (the STEP Regulation).

The proposal was adopted by the Commission on 11 March 2025 (COM(2025) 102 final, Strasbourg) under Commissioner Olivér Varhelyi (DG SANTE). The legal basis is Article 114 TFEU, with Article 114(3) requiring a high level of health protection.

The problem
Endemic shortages, external dependence
The EU faces recurrent shortages of antibiotics, insulin, and painkillers. About 70% of medicines dispensed in Europe are generics, whose active pharmaceutical ingredient (API) production has largely moved outside the EU. A pilot of 11 critical molecules identified significant non-EU API dependence for four of them.
The solution
Strategic Projects and procurement reform
The Act incentivises domestic manufacturing through Strategic Project status (fast-track permits, EU funding priority), reforms public procurement to allow favouring EU suppliers under defined conditions, and establishes coordinated joint procurement across Member States.
Economic weight
800,000 direct jobs in EU pharma
About 800,000 people are directly employed by the EU pharmaceutical sector. The sector's resilience is treated as a strategic objective alongside energy and semiconductors, building on the same industrial logic as the Critical Raw Materials Act and the Net-Zero Industry Act.
Scope
Centred on the Union List of Critical Medicines
The Act's scope centres on the Union List of Critical Medicines (276 critical active substances). It also covers "medicinal products of common interest" and introduces a category of "strategic projects" for facilities producing these products or their active ingredients in the EU.

Chapter-by-Chapter Analysis

Structure of the proposed Regulation: eight chapters covering the full policy framework

Chapter I
General provisions
Objectives, subject matter, scope, and definitions. Key defined terms include: critical medicine, medicinal product of common interest, strategic project, and security of supply. The scope centres on the Union List of Critical Medicines.
Chapter II
Strengthening EU security of supply
Makes security of supply of critical medicines an EU strategic objective requiring coordinated action by Member States and the Commission. Establishes the overarching policy commitment underpinning all subsequent chapters.
Chapter III, Section I
Strategic Project recognition
Recognition criteria and procedure: a Member State authority confirms a project meets the criteria (producing a critical medicine or its active ingredients in the EU). Recognition unlocks the benefits in Section II.
Chapter III, Section II
Priority status and fast-tracked permits
Strategic Projects receive highest national significance status (where applicable), access fast-tracked permit-granting, coordinated or joint environmental assessment, and dedicated administrative, regulatory, and scientific support from Member State authorities.
Chapter III, Section III
Financial incentives and EU funding
Strategic Projects gain priority access to EU funding under the MFF. The Critical Medicines Coordination Group facilitates information exchange on available financing instruments, de-risking mechanisms, and potential co-investment opportunities.
Chapter IV, Section I
Procurement award criteria (MEAT and EU preference)
Contracting authorities must apply most-economically-advantageous-tender (MEAT) criteria that go beyond price alone. In specific cases, justified by a vulnerability analysis, procurement requirements may favour suppliers manufacturing a significant portion in the EU (the "EU preference"), respecting international commitments.
Chapter IV, Section II
Collaborative procurement
Enables cross-border collaborative procurement, including procurement on behalf of or in the name of Member States. Lowers barriers to joint purchasing of critical medicines. Complements the existing Joint Procurement Agreement mechanism used during health crises.
Chapter V
Critical Medicines Coordination Group
Commission plus Member States body providing strategic orientation for funding, cooperating on national procurement initiatives, and advising on vulnerability-evaluation priority. Central hub for inter-Member-State coordination and strategic partnership decisions.
Chapter VI
International cooperation
Mandates the Commission to explore strategic partnerships with third countries and international organisations to diversify supply chains for critical active substances.
Chapter VII
Final provisions and STEP amendment
Amends Regulation (EU) 2024/795 (the STEP Regulation) to align Strategic Project status with existing EU funding instruments targeting strategic technologies in Europe.
Chapter VIII
Information, evaluation, and application
Provides for evaluation five years after application, and then every five years. Sets entry into force and application dates. Includes information-sharing obligations on contingency stocks and national programmes.

How Parliament Worked the File

One draft report, 1,144 tabled amendments, three committee opinions, and the one idea that shaped the whole negotiation

The Committee on Public Health (SANT) is the committee responsible. Tomislav Sokol (EPP, Croatia) was appointed rapporteur on 2 May 2025. The rapporteur's draft report, 18 July 2025

Sokol tabled his draft report (PE775.742) on 18 July 2025 with 169 amendments: 130 to the articles and 31 to the recitals. Most of them tighten definitions and procurement wording. One block does something much larger.

Amendments 113 to 122 insert a new Section Ia into Chapter IV and nine new articles, 20a to 20i, creating a Union coordination mechanism for critical medicines. Read together, they would have given the Commission real power over national medicine stocks:

  • Article 20a – national contingency stock rules must not harm supply in other Member States or the internal market.
  • Article 20b – a coordination mechanism operated by the Commission, monitoring availability and distribution across the Union.
  • Article 20c – the Commission adopts a binding decision requiring redistribution from a national stockpile.
  • Article 20d – a digital reporting system with real-time stock status and monthly Member State reporting.
  • Article 20e – Member States must comply, and the Commission may open an infringement procedure against those that do not.
  • Article 20f – the transferring party is entitled to full reimbursement plus transport and a reasonable mark-up.
  • Article 20g – a Union Stockpile as a last-resort mechanism, set up by delegated act.
  • Article 20i – Member States must remove conflicting national rules within one year.

This is the single most consequential idea any institution put on the table for this file, and following what happened to it explains the final text better than any other thread.

1,144 amendments from members, 29 September 2025

Members tabled 1,144 amendments to the draft report, numbered 170 to 1313 and published in three documents on 29 September 2025: PE778.025 (amendments 170-569), PE778.031 (570-969) and PE778.032 (970-1313). For a file of 31 articles, that volume is a measure of how contested the design was.

The committee vote, 15 December 2025

Ten compromise amendments settled 1,144

SANT resolved the amendments into ten compromise amendments circulated on 12 December 2025 and voted on 15 December. Each compromise covers a block of articles, and adopting it causes the individual amendments underneath to fall.

Compromise 7 is the one to watch: it covers Articles 20a to 20h and it kept the Union coordination mechanism, with three changes that show the committee negotiating against its own rapporteur. Redistribution decisions became a last resort, available only after the voluntary mechanisms in Union law have been exhausted. A new appeal mechanism let a Member State ask the Commission to review a decision within ten days. Reporting dropped from monthly to quarterly and had to reuse existing systems rather than build a new one. The infringement threat disappeared; the Union Stockpile and the reimbursement rule survived.

The report (A10-0272/2025) was tabled on 19 December 2025, and Parliament adopted this text as its first-reading position on 20 January 2026 with 503 votes in favour. That is what Parliament's negotiators carried into the trilogues.

What replaced the binding machinery is a cross-reference to another file. Article 20a(4) of the agreed text ties the Critical Medicines Act to the European Voluntary Solidarity Mechanism created by Article 131 of the pharmaceutical package Regulation (procedure 2023/0131(COD)). When that mechanism is activated, Member States provide stock data to the Medicine Shortages Steering Group where reasonably possible, and if it produces no workable option the Steering Group may issue a recommendation, including inviting Member States to consider suspending their own contingency stock requirements.

Recommendation, not decision. The Act now depends on a mechanism defined in a different and still unfinished Regulation, so anyone planning against this file has to read the two together.

Why the procurement article drew 73 amendments

Article 18 of the agreed text requires contracting authorities buying critical medicines to apply resilience requirements through at least one of four routes in the Public Procurement Directive 2014/24/EU: selection criteria (Article 58), technical specifications (Article 42), best price-quality ratio as an award criterion (Article 67), or contract performance clauses (Article 70).

Where a vulnerability evaluation shows high dependency on one or a few third countries, authorities must favour manufacturing in the Union, again through at least one named mechanism: reserving at least one lot representing at least 50 % of the total volume for suppliers whose medicine and active substance are made in the Union, or scoring EU manufacturing share within the award criteria. That is the hardest EU-preference rule in the Act, and it is why the article was the most amended provision in the file.

What the Provisional Deal Settled

Key outcomes of the inter-institutional agreement of 11-12 May 2026

Provisional agreement: May 2026

Core compromises reached between Council and Parliament

  • Resilience requirements in procurement: contracting authorities are obliged to apply resilience requirements when purchasing critical medicines, moving beyond purely price-based criteria.
  • "EU preference" with flexibility: companies that produce medicines and their active ingredients in Europe are favoured in procurement, with sufficient flexibility to avoid conflicts with international trade commitments (WTO GPA and bilateral agreements).
  • Joint-procurement trigger threshold reduced: the minimum number of Member States required to trigger collaborative procurement was lowered from nine to five, making the tool more accessible for smaller groups of countries.
  • Contingency stocks: requirements for national contingency stocks must be transparent, respect the principles of solidarity and proportionality, and operate alongside clarified use of the Voluntary Solidarity Mechanism (VSM), which facilitates information-sharing and voluntary reallocation of medicines between Member States in shortage situations.
  • Orphan medicinal products included: medicines for conditions affecting no more than 5 in 10,000 people in the EU are brought into the scope of Strategic Projects and collaborative procurement, acknowledging the particular vulnerability of rare-disease patients in shortage situations.
Status clarification: not yet law

A provisional agreement is a political deal between the negotiating teams of the Council (representing Member States) and the European Parliament. It is not yet legally binding. The text must be:

  • Reviewed and endorsed by the full Council (COREPER) and the EP committee (SANT), then confirmed by plenary;
  • Subject to legal-linguistic revision in all EU languages;
  • Formally adopted by both institutions before it enters into force.

The distinction between proposal, provisional agreement, and adopted law matters for compliance planning: obligations are not yet enforceable.
