The Affordable Housing Act: Europe regulates the paperwork, not the housing

The Commission has not told a single city what to do about housing. It has told them how to prove they were allowed to.

1. Two instruments, one idea

The Commission proposed two things on the same day, and they do opposite jobs. A Regulation that governs how a public authority must justify restricting the use of housing. And a Recommendation that urges the same authorities to build more.

Neither tells anyone whether to act. The decision to restrict short-term rentals, second homes or long vacancies stays exactly where it was: with national, regional and local authorities.

The EU is not regulating housing. It is regulating the paperwork of restricting housing.

That sounds modest. It is not. Cities across Europe have been restricting short-term rentals for a decade and losing in court, or waiting years to find out whether they would. This gives them a test they can pass.

2. The situation, as the Commission itself measures it

The figures below are the Commission's own, from the impact assessment published with the proposal. They are worth reading before the law, because they explain why the law looks the way it does.

The gap

Demographic change between 2010 and 2024 required 4.6 million more homes than were actually built. Residential building permits remain close to historical lows.

Rents

Up 23% across the EU between 2015 and February 2026. In capitals, far more: Brussels 38%, Berlin 59%, Madrid 75%, Lisbon 103%.

How urgent it feels

40% of Europeans call the lack of affordable housing an immediate and urgent problem where they live. In cities, 51%. In Irish cities, 72%; Spanish, 69%; Luxembourg, 68%.

Homes that are not homes

Nearly 20% of EU dwellings were not primary residences in 2021. Over 30% are second homes in Biarritz, the Algarve and the South Aegean. 18,600 dwellings in Paris had stood empty for more than a year.

Short-term rentals

Just 1.2% of EU housing stock, but over 20% in some neighbourhoods. In central Madrid, roughly 40 short-term rentals for every 100 rented homes. Operators running two or more units are 60 to 70% of listings in Barcelona, Malta, Valencia and the South Aegean.

What it costs a city

In Barcelona, an Airbnb-average neighbourhood sees rents 2% and prices 4.4% higher. In the busiest tenth, 7% and 17%.

STR activity is not the principal driver of housing stress.

The Commission's own consultation finding, in the executive summary of its impact assessment

That sentence reframes the whole file. The Act is not an anti-Airbnb law. The Commission says plainly that the shortage is structural and that restricting non-primary use cannot fix it alone.

3. Why Brussels is involved at all

Housing policy is national, regional and local. So why is there an EU law? Because the moment a city restricts who may let, buy or leave empty a flat, it touches three Treaty freedoms: services, establishment and capital. That has always been EU law.

The Court of Justice has said housing affordability can justify restricting those freedoms. But it said so in general terms, case by case. There has never been a method. The result is a litigation problem, and the Commission has now measured it.

  • Hundreds to low thousands of cases across the Union, on the Commission's own estimate.
  • Nearly 100 cases against a single Barcelona plan, before the High Court of Justice of Catalonia.
  • 420 cases in Paris, frozen for years while everyone waited for one ruling from Luxembourg.
  • €15,000 to €164,000 per case for the authority defending it. A hundred cases can reach €5 million.
  • Paris approved a 150-person housing enforcement brigade in April 2026.

And the political fact that explains the timing. In March 2024, 43 MEPs wrote to the Commission asking it to stop enforcement against local short-term rental rules, after a group of Member States had asked the same thing three months earlier. Mayors asked too. Those enforcement actions are on hold.

This Act is the way out of that standoff. It gives the Commission a rule it can apply and cities a test they can pass.

4. The housing stress test

This is the operative core. Before restricting anything, an authority must show its area is under housing stress, and there is now one European way to show it.

Condition 1

The price-to-income ratio is 8 or higher. That is eight years of one person's disposable income to buy an average dwelling in the area.

Condition 2

The ratio has risen over the last ten years of available data.

Condition 3

Stress is unlikely to ease within three years, judged on population, housing supply and housing demand.

The shortcut

If the ratio reaches 10 or higher, condition 2 is dropped. A market that unaffordable does not need to prove it is also getting worse.

Meeting the test does not oblige anyone to act. It only opens the door.

A measurement caveat worth knowing

Prices are measured at NUTS 3 level and incomes at NUTS 2. A wealthy city inside a poorer region and a poor town inside a rich one are therefore not measured symmetrically. Authorities may use finer local data, provided it reaches the same thresholds. The Commission supplies the baseline series through its Mapadomo housing database.

5. What a city must then prove

Passing the stress test is the beginning. Every measure must then clear a checklist.

  • It must not discriminate, directly or indirectly, on nationality or place of establishment.
  • The activity must have had a significant adverse effect for at least three years before the measure.
  • No less restrictive measure would work as well. Caps, grandfathering, hardship exceptions and tax incentives must be considered first.
  • The territory must be drawn no wider than the evidence allows, down to a district if that is what the data shows.
  • For short-term rentals, the measure must be aimed at scale, frequency or commercial character, not at occasional letting.
  • For short-term rentals, the authority must actually be enforcing the EU short-term rental Regulation, including removing unregistered listings. You cannot ban what you have not first tried to register.
  • For acquisition rules, they never apply retroactively, and transitional arrangements are required.
  • For vacancy, there must be exemptions where non-occupation is justified: work, study, health, ongoing succession, legal impediment or a home that cannot be lived in.
  • Everything must be published before it takes effect, including the assessment itself, the exact territory and the duration.
  • Measures last a maximum of five years, are reviewed, and must be withdrawn without delay once the conditions lapse.

6. What the Act does not touch

This is where most misreadings will happen, so it is worth being exact. The Regulation covers only restrictions on non-primary use, justified by housing affordability. Outside its scope:

  • Rent regulation in tenancy contracts
  • Housing benefits
  • Taxation of general application
  • Building standards
  • General zoning and spatial planning
  • Inheritance, gifts and family transfers

And one hard limit that matters to every ordinary host: a host's own primary residence can never be restricted under this instrument. Letting the home you live in does not take a home off the market, so the Act leaves it alone. Cities may go further under their own law, but not through this route.

Measures already adopted before the Regulation enters into force are not caught by it, though authorities may choose to review them against it.

7. The other half: build more

The Recommendation, adopted the same day, exists because the Commission does not believe restrictions are enough. It says so directly.

Restrictions alone cannot solve housing shortages.

Its instrument is the housing acceleration plan: a local plan covering the stressed area and its commuting zone, built on real data, resourced, and agreed with the people it affects. Around it sit concrete asks.

  • 60-day permitting deadlines where feasible, tacit approval for some steps, and digital one-stop shops.
  • openBIM recognised as a legal submission format, with machine-readable permitting rules.
  • Local targets for housing below market price, inclusive zoning and density bonuses.
  • A 20-year minimum for keeping publicly subsidised housing affordable.
  • Land banks, public land agencies and long-term leasehold models where the authority keeps the land.
  • Revolving funds, and prioritised permits for renovation, repurposing and subdivision.

The number that frames everything

The Commission puts the need at an extra €150 billion a year for the next decade. Against that, the EU has mobilised €47.7 billion across the current budget period, with €4.7 billion more reprogrammed from cohesion funds. The gap is the story.

8. The Regulation, article by article

Seventeen articles in three chapters. This is what each one actually says, in the order the proposal sets them out.

    Chapter 1 · General provisions (Articles 1 to 4)

  • Article 1
    Subject matter

    A common framework for measures that safeguard housing affordability and availability, built on the internal market legal basis. It requires that any such measure be justified, transparent, proportionate and open to review.

  • Article 2
    Scope

    It catches exactly two families of measure, and only where housing affordability is the reason for them: restrictions on short-term accommodation rental services, and restrictions on acquiring or using land and residential property that is not a primary residence. A measure taken solely for some other overriding public-interest reason falls outside.

  • Article 3
    Relationship with other Union acts

    Short-term rental measures are lifted out of the Services Directive, expressly including its obligation to notify the Commission. Measures that do no more than apply the registration chapter of the 2024 short-term rental Regulation are left alone.

  • Article 4
    Definitions

    Ten definitions carry most of the weight. A competent authority may be national, regional or local. Acquisition deliberately excludes inheritance, matrimonial property regimes, the division of an estate, corporate reorganisations that do not change beneficial ownership, and a creditor realising security. A primary residence is where a person habitually resides and has the habitual centre of their interests, as further defined in national law.

    • Chapter 2 · Measures to safeguard housing affordability and availability (Articles 5 to 13)

  • Article 5
    Requirements for measures

    Sets out the two measures an authority may take, and adds the first hard limit: a short-term rental restriction justified on housing grounds may only reach accommodation that is not provided by the host in their own primary residence.

  • Article 6
    Assessment of housing stress

    The test itself. The price-to-income ratio must be at or above eight years of disposable income per capita; it must have risen over the most recent ten years of available data; and, absent the measure, the stress must be unlikely to ease within three years, judged on population dynamics, housing supply and housing demand. At a ratio of ten or above, the ten-year trend test falls away. The assessment must rest on objective, transparent and verifiable data and sound statistical principles. The Annex fixes the method, and authorities may use more granular local data provided the result stays consistent with the thresholds.

  • Article 7
    Territorial scope of an area under housing stress

    The area may be no wider than necessary. It can be a district, a municipality, a metropolitan area, a functional urban area, an agglomeration, a rural area, or any part of one.

  • Article 8
    Non-discrimination

    No direct or indirect discrimination between nationals of Member States on grounds of nationality, nor between legal persons on grounds of their Member State of establishment.

  • Article 9
    Necessity

    Measures are only available in an area that passes the Article 6 test. For both families, the authority must demonstrate a significant adverse effect on housing availability or affordability in that area over at least three years before adoption, drawing on short-term rental registration data where it exists. Vacancy measures cannot bite where non-occupation is justified: temporary absence for work, education or training, health, ongoing succession proceedings, a legal impediment, or a dwelling that is uninhabitable.

  • Article 10
    Proportionality

    The longest article, and the real constraint. A measure must be suitable and go no further than necessary, and must satisfy seven cumulative requirements: territorial limitation, and calibration to different levels of stress inside the area; an assessment that no less restrictive measure would work as well within a reasonable period; compliance with legal certainty and legitimate expectations; for short-term rentals, calibration to activity that by scale, frequency or commercial character is more likely to remove long-term housing; for short-term rentals, that the authority actually applies and enforces the 2024 Regulation in that area, including suspending and withdrawing registration numbers and ordering removal of unregistered listings; for acquisition rules, application only to property and land acquired after the measure applies; and for long-term vacancy, calibration on objective criteria such as the number of dwellings one owner holds, the length of non-occupation, or acquisition by legal persons. Where existing lawful situations are affected, transitional arrangements are required.

  • Article 11
    Procedural safeguards and judicial review

    Anyone affected gets appropriate procedural safeguards and effective judicial review, expressly including the ability to challenge the assessments the authority carried out under Articles 6 to 10.

  • Article 12
    Duration and review of measures

    Five years maximum, extendable only where a review finds the Article 6 to 10 requirements still met. The first review falls within five years of adoption, and a measure that no longer qualifies must be withdrawn without undue delay.

  • Article 13
    Publication of the measures

    Everything goes public before the measure enters into force, and that covers adoption, extension, amendment and withdrawal alike. What must be published: the assessments themselves, the precise territorial scope of both the stress area and the measure, and the duration.

    • Chapter 3 · Final provisions (Articles 14 to 17)

  • Article 14
    Existing measures

    The Regulation does not apply to measures adopted before it enters into force. An authority may nevertheless choose to review those measures against it.

  • Article 15
    Exercise of the delegation

    The delegated power runs for an indeterminate period from entry into force, may be revoked at any time by Parliament or the Council, and requires the Commission to consult Member State experts first. Each act is subject to a two-month objection period, extendable by a further two months.

  • Article 16
    Evaluation

    Five years after entry into force, the Commission must evaluate the effect on the functioning of the internal market and on housing affordability and availability, and report to Parliament, the Council, the Economic and Social Committee and the Committee of the Regions.

  • Article 17
    Entry into force and application

    The twentieth day following publication. Binding in its entirety and directly applicable in all Member States.

A cross-reference that does not work

Article 15 points the delegated power at Article 6(3) four times over, and Article 15(1) reads ‘the conditions laid down in this Article 6(3)’. But Article 6(3) is the duty to use objective and verifiable data. The actual empowerment sits in Article 6(5). This is a drafting slip in the published proposal, not a reading of ours, and it is the kind of thing a co-legislator corrects at first reading.

9. The Recommendation, point by point

Sixteen numbered points in seven parts. None of it binds anyone, and all of it took effect the day it was adopted.

  • 1. Subject matter

    Guidance, in full respect of subsidiarity, for authorities in areas under housing stress or at risk of it. The aim is place-based responses that increase supply, with the focus on housing below market prices.

  • 2. Housing acceleration plans

    The organising device of the whole Recommendation. A plan tailored to local and regional conditions, or the same content folded into plans that already exist. It should rest on real data about local shortages and territorial disparities, including housing for young people, students and apprentices; cover commuting zones and functional urban areas rather than only the stressed area itself, so that underused housing nearby can be made viable through transport investment; join up with planning, financing, sustainable tourism, transport, essential services, health and energy renovation, including heat resilience and future cooling needs; and carry enough money and staff. Authorities should build it with other tiers of government, public and private providers, and citizens.

  • 3. Permitting, procurement and modern methods of construction

    Prioritised approval for affordable and social housing, and for renovation, energy performance improvement, adaptation, repurposing and subdivision, allowing deviations from local zoning where substantive standards are still met. Maximum processing times, a 60-day permitting timeframe where relevant, and tacit approval for certain steps. Interoperable digital permitting, Building Information Modelling workflows, openBIM as a legally recognised submission format, and permitting requirements in machine-readable form. Faster dispute resolution, including standing restrictions and time limits on judicial review where legally feasible. In procurement: prefabrication and off-site construction, framework contracts, joint procurement and accelerated procedures. Alongside that, upskilling and reskilling, administrative capacity at local and regional level, and easier cross-border provision of construction and renovation services.

  • 4. Increasing the supply of housing below market prices

    Legally recognise the different public and private providers of affordable and social housing and use the 2025 state aid Decision to support them. Set local targets for below-market housing as a share of the stock, to meet identified need, achieve social mix and prevent spatial segregation. Use inclusive zoning, dedicated plots and density bonuses to hit them. Where public resources have paid for the housing, designate it for that purpose for long enough, meeting at least the twenty-year minimum where the Decision requires it. And try new models, such as long-term public leasehold in which the authority keeps the land while providers finance, build and manage the housing.

  • 5. Better use and adaptation of the existing building stock

    Analyse and if needed revise building codes, standards and zoning so that renovation, densification, extension and subdivision become possible. Revise tax systems and rental law to make better use of what already stands, for instance by incentivising long-term residential use of vacant buildings. Revise co-ownership law so renovation decisions are easier to take. Where the rental market is underdeveloped, encourage market-rate rental and build-to-rent with appropriate safeguards, and reduce the tax distortion between owner-occupied and rented housing. Make it easier to let out rooms, particularly to students and apprentices, and support voluntary right-sizing schemes.

  • 6. Investing in land and buildings

    Authorities should build the tools and capacity to invest in land and buildings: acquiring private land or land held by other public bodies, readjusting and reassembling plots including by negotiation, separating ownership of land from building, imposing resale restrictions such as capital gains limitation clauses or mandatory buy-back, and requiring providers to contribute to infrastructure costs. They can create public land agencies, land banks or development corporations. Where they make their own land and buildings available, they should retain ownership, through long-term leases or by separating land from building.

  • 7. Mobilising funding and financing

    National hubs within the pan-European investment platform for affordable and sustainable housing, to support locally identified investment pipelines with technical assistance and aggregation. Full use of Union funding under the current and future multiannual financial framework and the Social Climate Fund, including blended public-private finance and de-risking tools. Revolving funds that must reinvest all profits in the service. Public-private partnerships, co-investment structures and mixed-tenure projects. And specific instruments, equity and very long-term debt, developed with national and regional promotional banks, the Council of Europe Development Bank, the European Bank for Reconstruction and Development and the European Investment Bank Group.

10. What happens next, and what will be fought over

The Regulation now goes to the European Parliament and the Council under the ordinary legislative procedure. It enters into force twenty days after publication, with no deferred application date. Four things are worth watching.

  • The thresholds. Eight and ten can be changed by the Commission through delegated acts. Parliament and Council rarely hand over a number that decides who is covered without a fight.
  • Who wanted what. Member States leaned towards soft guidance and maximum flexibility. Mayors, local communities and small businesses wanted the binding framework. The Commission chose the mayors' option.
  • The platforms. Short-term rental platforms and hosts said in writing that no new legislation was needed, then warned that guidance alone would not stop disproportionate local rules.
  • The impact assessment was rejected once. The Regulatory Scrutiny Board gave a negative opinion in June 2026; the revised version passed with reservations in July.

11. What this means for you

If you are a city or region

You gain a defensible method. Run the price-to-income test, document three years of harm, show you considered gentler options, draw the map tightly, publish everything, and review in five years. In exchange you lose the old freedom to improvise, and you must genuinely enforce the short-term rental register before restricting.

If you host or own

Your own home is protected from restriction under this Act. If you run several units commercially, you are precisely who the framework expects to be targeted, but only with evidence, only in a defined area, only for five years at a time, and never retroactively for property you already own.

If you rent or are buying

Nothing here builds you a home. What it does is make local restrictions more likely to survive a court challenge, and oblige the authority to pair them with a plan to increase supply. The Commission is explicit that the second half is the one that lasts.
