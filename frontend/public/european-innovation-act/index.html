1. Two instruments, and a third act next door

On 9 September 2026 the Commission put two separate instruments on the table under one banner, and they are not the same kind of thing. A Regulation, the European Innovation Act, which is binding and goes to the Parliament and the Council. And a proposal for a Council Recommendation on regulatory sandboxes, which is not binding, is adopted by the Council alone, and never reaches the Parliament.

Most coverage folded the sandboxes into the Act as a third pillar. They are not in it. The Act does not mention a sandbox in its operative provisions, and the Commission's own wording is careful: in parallel, it says, it will help innovators through a specific proposal on regulatory sandboxes.

One is a law. The other is advice the Council gives Member States about their own laws. Reading them as one reform gets both wrong.

There is a third act in the same week, and the boundary between them is deliberate. The Public Procurement Act, COM(2026) 590, rewrites the general procurement rules, and it excludes research and development services, including pre-commercial procurement, expressly to avoid overlaps. Those contracts are governed here instead. The difference is what is being bought: R&D procurement buys the research, the Public Procurement Act also buys the finished product.

The Innovation Act

COM(2026) 567 final, procedure 2026/0264(COD), legal basis Article 114 TFEU. A Regulation: binding, directly applicable, ordinary legislative procedure. 41 articles in four chapters.

The sandboxes Recommendation

COM(2026) 568 final, procedure 2026/0267(NLE), legal basis Article 292 TFEU with Article 173(3). A Council Recommendation: not binding, no transposition, applies from adoption. 13 points, 11 recitals.

The Public Procurement Act

COM(2026) 590 final, procedure 2026/0265(COD). Same day, different file. R&D services are carved out of it and land in the Innovation Act, except for the R&D elements inside its own new innovation procedure.

2. What it actually does

Strip away the framing and the Regulation does three things, in two chapters.

It gives Europe a common way to value an idea

A voluntary methodology for valuing intellectual property, built by the EU Intellectual Property Office, plus a voluntary certification scheme for the people who do the valuing. The point is finance: a bank cannot lend against an asset nobody can price.

It builds a place to buy and sell that idea

A multilingual matchmaking platform run by EUIPO, plus a secondary market for disposing of IP nobody is using. It is not a regulated market and it does not clear trades: the deals are concluded off the platform.

It writes a legal code for buying research

Twenty-eight articles governing how a public buyer runs a research and development procurement, who may bid, who owns what comes out, and how several countries can buy together. This is the bulk of the Act.

Pre-commercial procurement gets a legal definition, for the first time

Article 3(2) defines it as R&D procurement that involves risk-benefit sharing under market conditions and competitive development in phases. The technique has been used in Europe since 2007 without ever being defined in EU law. Its central feature survives the codification: in pre-commercial procurement the IP stays with the provider, and that is why it sits outside the WTO Government Procurement Agreement, which in turn is why a place-of-performance condition is lawful there and would not be elsewhere.

3. The numbers are ranges, and everyone quotes the ceiling

The Joint Research Centre modelled three of the reforms and published a range for every result. The press release quotes the top of each range. So did we, in the first version of our own guide.

Reported everywhere

Up to €452 billion in GDP. Half a million jobs. €10.2 billion a year in new IP-backed finance.

What the study says

€256bn to €452bn cumulative over ten years. 238,000 to 507,000 jobs at peak in year ten. €2.7bn to €10.2bn a year in finance. GDP 0.24% to 0.42% above the no-policy baseline.

The floor of the finance range is less than a third of the figure in the factsheet, which carries no range at all. The Commission is careful in places, writing "up to 507,000". The reporting is not.

These are not forecasts of actual future economic outcomes but rather scenario analyses, a plausible envelope of outcomes rather than point predictions.

Joint Research Centre, JRC Working Papers on Territorial Modelling and Analysis No. 05/2026

The study adds the sentence that matters most for anyone quoting it: weaker behavioural responses or implementation shortfalls would proportionally scale these effects down.

The mechanism, in the model's own terms: investment triggered by the IP valuation measure drives most of the GDP gain through capital deepening, reinforced by productivity gains from R&D procurement. Supply-side channels dominate, and administrative costs stay modest against the efficiency gains.

The model assumed a stricter rule than the Act contains

The JRC models the EU-preference measure as public buyers requiring more than 70% of R&D and at least 20% of commercial-scale production in the EU. The Act, and the Commission's own questions and answers, require 50% of the R&D activities and say nothing about production. The economics were costed on the impact assessment's preferred option; the proposal that emerged is looser. Anyone citing the GDP figure alongside the 50% rule is joining two things the study did not join.

4. The problem, in one number

In the EU, R&D procurement represents €17 billion or around 0.6% of annual total public procurement expenditure, while other leading economies in the world invest 5 to 8 times more and already dedicate 3% to 5% of their annual public procurement expenditure to R&D procurement.

European Commission, Questions and answers on the European Innovation Act, QANDA/26/1811, 9 September 2026

That gap is the whole case for the R&D procurement chapter. The multiplier the case rests on is that every euro of R&D procurement returns about three euros to suppliers, applied to a base of roughly €17.3 billion a year.

The legal reason the gap exists is more technical, and it is why a new Regulation was needed rather than an amendment. Most R&D procurement falls outside the 2014 procurement directives, typically because the benefits do not accrue exclusively to the buyer for its own use. So it is governed by fragmented national rules, or by nothing but the Treaty principles as the Court of Justice has interpreted them. A public buyer who wants to fund research faces twenty-seven different answers, or no answer at all.

The proposal cites the European Court of Auditors' Special Report 28/2023 on the low level of competition in EU public procurement as part of its evidence base.

5. Who owns the results

Article 19 is the heart of the Act, and the article a company should read first. Its title, risk-benefit sharing under market conditions, does not advertise what it contains.

  • The contractor keeps its intellectual property. That is the default and it is the opposite of the classic public-contract rule.
  • The buyer gets at least free access to the results, for itself and for its other contractors.
  • The contractor owes duties: to protect the IP, to use best efforts to commercialise it, and not to use it against the public interest.
  • In an emergency the buyer can compel a licence, non-exclusive and on fair, reasonable and non-discriminatory terms.
  • Failure to commercialise has two answers. With legitimate reasons, the buyer must first offer more time. Without them, or where results are used contrary to the public interest, the buyer may licence the results or require transfer of ownership, without any financial compensation.
  • The buyer may object to an exclusive licence or a transfer, including where the results would end up under third-country control.
  • Non-PCP procurements may derogate and take ownership, with justification. If they take it for exclusivity and then fail to exploit it within four years, they must offer to transfer or licence on FRAND terms.

Read together, this is a deliberate trade. The public buyer gives up ownership, which is what makes the contract attractive to a company with something to protect. In exchange it keeps a set of levers that only fire if the company sits on the result or points it somewhere the buyer did not intend.

6. How an R&D procurement runs

Section 1 of Chapter 2 is a procurement code in miniature. Here is the shape of a procedure under it, with the numbers that bind.

Consult the market first, and you must

Article 9 makes market consultation mandatory before the call, announced by a prior information notice in the Official Journal with the innovation field ticked and "Pre-Commercial Procurement" in the title where it applies. Only pure R&D consultancy is exempt, by three named CPV codes.

Describe a problem, not a product

Article 10 lists twenty things the procurement documents must state, from (a) to (t), and requires the need to be framed as a problem to be solved. Article 18 then imposes an order of precedence for requirements: legal, then functional, then performance, then design, applied characteristic by characteristic, with no brand or patent references.

Do not ask a startup for a track record

Article 15 forbids requiring prior experience unless it is justified, and forbids failing an operator solely for lacking it. Minimum financial capacity is capped at 50% of the estimated contract value. A startup may prove itself with a business plan plus evidence of IP assets or venture capital, and may file it after the award notification and before signature. Full documentary evidence is demanded only from the highest-ranked tenders.

Weight quality, not price

Article 17 requires quality to carry at least 50% of the total weighting and innovation-related criteria at least 15%, with three of those criteria capped at 15% so there is a ceiling as well as a floor. Five innovation criteria are mandatory, one of them the share of value carried out in the Union. Evaluation must finish within two months.

Buy from more than one supplier

Article 22 makes multiple sourcing the default. A buyer may depart from it only for consultancy contracts, or in a non-PCP procurement where the market is too small, the benefits do not outweigh the costs, or security requires it.

Develop in phases, and pay as you go

Article 24 codifies the phased mechanism: design, prototype, development, testing, first deployment. Phases may be fast-tracked. A contractor dropped at a phase gate still gets paid for work satisfactorily completed. New entrants may join later phases if competition collapses, and the buyer may continue with a single contractor as a last resort. Article 25 makes advance payment the default, and lets a subcontractor ask the public buyer to pay it directly.

Share the savings

Article 21 obliges buyers to insert a value engineering clause above a threshold that falls from €10 million to €5 million over five years, and contractors to mirror it in their subcontracts. Where the contractor proposes a change that cuts cost, the saving is shared. Article 20 makes modifications under 15% non-substantial, with a notice within 20 calendar days and prior publication above 50% of initial value.

Guarantees, halved for small companies

The financial guarantees article caps tender guarantees at 2%, with a ceiling of €500,000 above €5 million of contract value and €100,000 below it. Performance and retention guarantees are capped at 5%, rising to 10% only for highly complex or risky contracts on a documented risk analysis, and no guarantee at all may be required below €500,000. Every one of those values is halved for SMEs, innovative startups and innovative scaleups. This is the article the published text misnumbers: see section 11.

Two obligations run underneath all of this. Any official EU language may be used for documents, tenders and deliverables (Article 4(3)). And documents must remain freely accessible until three years after award, delivered through European Business Wallets, a Regulation that is itself still going through the Parliament.

7. Made in Europe, at fifty per cent

Article 11 is the article trade lawyers will read. Public buyers shall require that contractors perform at least 50% of the R&D activities under the contract in eligible countries, including the work of the principal research staff with scientific responsibility.

Eligible means the EU, plus third countries with a relevant agreement. In practice, today, that is the EEA and the Western Balkans, and nothing else, because EU trade agreements do not cover R&D procurement. The reach of the rule is therefore narrower than it looks and wider than it sounds.

  • A 20% price-difference escape hatch keeps the preference from becoming an absolute bar.
  • A security carve-out applies.
  • Defence is exempt. The R&D procurement rules are not mandatory for defence public buyers.
  • Article 26 allows data-localisation clauses confining processing, storage and access to eligible countries, and forbidding relocation without written authorisation.
  • Following the Act's R&D procurement rules gives buyers certainty on State aid: in most cases such support is either no aid, or compatible aid.

The security articles sharpen the point. Article 29 lets a buyer terminate or exclude an operator mid-performance on security grounds, fenced by a written assessment, a proportionality test, a duty to consider alternatives such as amending the contract or increasing monitoring, and not less than 10 calendar days for the contractor to respond. Article 30 imports the Cyber Resilience Act's Annex I essential requirements wholesale, including the manufacturer's ability to handle vulnerabilities, and requires buyers to exclude, at any time, anyone identified as a high-risk supplier of ICT components used in key ICT assets.

8. EUIPO's new job

Chapter 3 hands the EU Intellectual Property Office a new mandate and amends its founding Regulation to make room for it. The vehicle is a Union Competence Centre for intellectual property-backed finance and commercialisation, and its limits are as important as its tasks.

What it will do

Build a voluntary IP valuation and disclosure framework. Run a multilingual matchmaking platform. Develop a secondary market for disposing of IP. Keep an evidence database on IP-backed finance. Operate a voluntary certification scheme for IP valuators, and a Union-level helpdesk. It explicitly covers regulatory exclusivities for pharmaceuticals.

What it will not be

It has no separate legal personality, no financial autonomy and no decision-making power. The platform is not a regulated market and the deals are concluded off it. Certification is not a professional licence and confers no binding or presumptive legal effect on any valuation, and EUIPO is expressly not liable for individual valuations.

Two deadlines bind: both the valuation framework and the matchmaking platform must exist within four years of entry into force. Set-up may draw on EUIPO's accumulated budgetary surplus, which is the quiet answer to how it gets paid for. Article 33 also contemplates a possible Union-wide register of pledges over IP assets, which would be a substantial change to how IP is used as collateral if it ever arrives.

The costs are small and precisely stated. EUIPO set-up runs to EUR 2,423,520 to EUR 2,566,080, about 17 to 18 full-time posts over two years, then EUR 736,000 a year for roughly 10.3 posts. Public authorities face EUR 45 million in additional adjustment costs, driven by the training needed to run R&D procurement and apply the EU preference, plus EUR 2.4 million for the public sector to build the valuation framework, the centre and the marketplace. Inside the JRC model, public buyers carry EUR 26.1 million a year in adjustment costs, netted against efficiency gains of EUR 64.8 million to EUR 1,166.4 million a year. Against all of that, the impact assessment expects the administrative effort an IP-rich firm spends to fall about 75%, from 40 to 80 hours a year to 10 to 20.

9. The Regulation, article by article

Forty-one articles in four chapters, with Chapter 2 split into three sections. This is what each one says, in the order the proposal presents them.

Chapter 1 · General provisions (Articles 1 to 3)
  • Article 1
    Subject matter

    A framework of measures for strengthening the Union innovation ecosystem. Two strands: how public bodies buy research and development, and how intellectual property is valued and commercialised.

  • Article 2
    Scope

    Chapter 2 applies to R&D services procurement that falls outside Directives 2014/23/EU, 2014/24/EU and 2014/25/EU, at or above the threshold in Article 4(c) of Directive 2014/24/EU. Innovation agencies get the lower threshold of Article 15(a) of Directive 2014/25/EU, provided they act on their own mandate and respect transparency, non-discrimination and equal treatment. Procurement inside the three directives, and defence procurement under or excluded from Directive 2009/81/EC, is out.

  • Article 3
    Definitions

    Twenty-eight of them. The one that matters most is paragraph 2: pre-commercial procurement, defined for the first time in EU law as R&D procurement involving risk-benefit sharing under market conditions and competitive development in phases. Also defined: value engineering, the value engineering change proposal and the value engineering clause; emergency situation; and innovative startup, innovative scaleup and innovative enterprise, by reference to Commission Recommendation (EU) 2026/720 of 18 March 2026.

Chapter 2, Section 1 · R&D procurement procedure and contract performance (Articles 4 to 27)
  • Article 4
    General principles

    Equal treatment, transparency and proportionality, plus four things a reader would not expect in a principles article. Any official EU language may be used for documents, tenders and deliverables. All documents must be freely accessible until three years after award, delivered through European Business Wallets. Procurement must take four strategic priorities into account.

  • Article 5
    Economic operators

    Who may take part, alone or with others, and the rule that legal form cannot be required at the point of tendering.

  • Article 6
    Groups of economic operators

    Joint tendering, the responsibilities of the members, and when a group may be required to take a particular legal form. The published text numbers this article's paragraphs 4 to 8 instead of 1 to 5.

  • Article 7
    Reliance on the capacity of other entities

    When a tenderer may lean on the capacity of a third party, and the commitments that must be produced.

  • Article 8
    Subcontracting

    What the buyer may require about subcontracting, and the limits on requiring the work be done in-house.

  • Article 9
    Market consultation

    Mandatory before the call. A prior information notice goes in the Official Journal, with the innovation field ticked and "Pre-Commercial Procurement" in the title where applicable. Exempt only for pure R&D consultancy, by three named CPV codes: 73200000-4, 73210000-7 and 73220000-0.

  • Article 10
    Procurement documents

    A twenty-point checklist, (a) to (t), of what the documents must state, including exclusion grounds, selection criteria and financial guarantees "in accordance with the obligations laid down in Articles 13, 14, 15 and 16". Paragraph 4 requires the need to be framed as a problem to be solved. Paragraph 6 creates a priority right to purchase a predefined volume ahead of others, as a contractual obligation.

  • Article 11
    Conditions for access to R&D procurement procedures and place of performance

    The 50% rule. Contractors must perform at least half the R&D activities in eligible countries, including the work of the principal research staff with scientific responsibility. Eligible means the EU and third countries with a relevant agreement, which today is the EEA and the Western Balkans. A 20% price-difference escape hatch and a security carve-out apply.

  • Article 12
    Determination of origin

    How the origin of an economic operator and of the work is established for the purposes of Article 11.

  • Article 13
    Mandatory exclusion grounds

    Eleven grounds, (a) to (k), running from participation in a criminal organisation to child sexual abuse material, on a five-year window, with a defined "key person" whose conduct is attributed to the operator. Tax and social-security breaches are included, with a proportionality escape where the amount is minor.

  • Article 14
    Optional exclusion grounds

    Eight grounds, including one that is new to a procurement text: having benefited from foreign subsidies distorting the internal market within the meaning of Regulation (EU) 2022/2560.

  • Article 15
    Selection criteria

    Prior experience may not be required unless justified, and an operator may not be failed solely for lacking it. Qualifications must be expressed in European Qualifications Framework levels. Minimum financial capacity is capped at 50% of the estimated contract value. A startup may prove standing with a business plan plus evidence of IP assets or venture capital, and may file it between award notification and signature. Full documentary evidence is demanded only from the highest-ranked tenders.

  • Article 16 printed as “Article 10”
    Financial guarantees

    Printed in the proposal as "Article 10". Tender guarantees capped at 2%, and in absolute terms at €500,000 above €5 million of contract value and €100,000 below it. Performance and retention guarantees capped at 5%, rising to 10% only for highly complex or risky contracts on a documented risk analysis. No guarantee at all below €500,000. Every value halved for SMEs, innovative startups and innovative scaleups.

  • Article 17
    Innovation-related award criteria

    Quality must carry at least 50% of the total weighting. Innovation-related criteria must carry at least 15%, and three of them are capped at 15%, so the article sets a floor and a ceiling. Five innovation criteria are mandatory, one being the share of the contract value carried out in the Union. Evaluation must be completed within two months.

  • Article 18
    Formulating requirements for the characteristics of the procured R&D services

    A strict order of precedence, applied characteristic by characteristic: legal requirements first, then functional, then performance, then design. A six-tier hierarchy governs which standards may be referenced, and brand or patent references are not allowed.

  • Article 19
    Risk benefit sharing under market conditions

    The heart of the Act. Contractors keep their IP, subject to duties to protect it, use best efforts to commercialise it, and not act against the public interest. Buyers get at least free access for themselves and their other contractors. In emergencies buyers may compel non-exclusive FRAND licences. Failure to commercialise with legitimate reasons obliges the buyer to offer more time first; without legitimate reasons, or where results are used contrary to the public interest, the buyer may licence or require transfer of ownership without any financial compensation. Buyers may object to exclusive licensing or transfer, including where results would fall under third-country control. Non-PCP procurements may derogate and take ownership; if they take it for exclusivity and fail to exploit within four years they must offer to transfer or licence on FRAND terms.

  • Article 20
    Contract modifications

    Modifications below 15% of the initial value are non-substantial. A modification notice must be published within 20 calendar days. Above 50% of initial value, prior publication is required.

  • Article 21
    Value engineering

    A value engineering clause becomes mandatory above a threshold that falls year by year: €10 million after one year, €9 million after two, €8 million after three, €7 million after three again, €6 million after four and €5 million after five. Buyers may use one below those values. Contractors must mirror the clause in subcontracts above the same values.

  • Article 22
    Multiple sourcing

    The default. Derogation is possible only for consultancy CPVs, or in a non-PCP procurement where the market is too small, the benefits do not outweigh the costs, or security requires it.

  • Article 23
    R&D procurement call for tenders

    What the call must contain and how it is published.

  • Article 24
    Competitive development in phases and assessment of contract performance

    The pre-commercial procurement mechanism, written into law. Phases may be fast-tracked. A contractor dropped at a phase gate still gets paid for work satisfactorily completed. Value engineering savings are shared. New entrants may be admitted to later phases if competition collapses, and the buyer may continue with a single contractor as a last resort.

  • Article 25
    Payments

    Advance payment is the default unless an overriding interest says otherwise. A subcontractor may request direct payment from the public buyer.

  • Article 26
    Confidentiality and data protection

    Confidentiality obligations both ways, and data-localisation clauses that may confine processing, storage and access to eligible countries and forbid relocation without written authorisation.

  • Article 27
    Termination of R&D procurement contracts

    The ordinary grounds for termination, and what happens to results and payments when a contract ends early.

Chapter 2, Section 2 · Security (Articles 28 to 30)
  • Article 28
    Security considerations in R&D procurement

    How security requirements enter the procedure, and the delegated power to specify them.

  • Article 29
    Security measures during contract implementation

    A buyer may terminate in whole or in part, or exclude an operator mid-performance, where security or public-safety obligations are breached or a risk materialises or is likely to. Fenced: proportionate to the risk; preceded by a written assessment weighing severity, the impact on delivery of public services and alternative mitigations such as amending the contract or increasing monitoring; notified in writing with grounds and evidence, classified information excepted, unless severity, imminence or the risk of aggravating the threat justifies otherwise. The contractor gets not less than 10 calendar days to submit observations and propose remedies, shortened only in an emergency, and must cooperate on securing or transferring data and assets and on continuity of critical services.

  • Article 30
    Cybersecurity

    For products with digital elements in scope of the Cyber Resilience Act, Regulation (EU) 2024/2847, buyers must ensure the Annex I essential requirements are met, expressly including the manufacturer's ability to handle vulnerabilities, and must take that into account in the procurement itself. Stricter requirements are permitted, including for national security or defence, where consistent with Union law and proportionate. NIS2, Directive (EU) 2022/2555, applies without prejudice. Cyber requirements may sit in specifications, selection criteria, exclusion criteria, award criteria or performance conditions. Buyers shall exclude, at any time, an operator identified as a high-risk supplier of ICT components for use in key ICT assets.

Chapter 2, Section 3 · Joint procurement of R&D services (Article 31)
  • Article 31
    Common rules on joint R&D procurement

    Cross-border joint buying: how buyers from several Member States run one procedure together, which national law applies, and how responsibilities are allocated between them.

Chapter 3 · Commercialisation of research and innovation results (Articles 32 to 37)
  • Article 32
    Union Competence Centre for intellectual property-backed finance and commercialisation

    Set up inside EUIPO, with no separate legal personality, no financial autonomy and no decision-making power. Tasks: a voluntary valuation and disclosure framework, a multilingual matchmaking platform, a secondary market for IP disposal, and a Union-level helpdesk. It explicitly covers regulatory exclusivities for pharmaceuticals. Both the framework and the platform carry a four-year deadline from entry into force. Set-up may draw on EUIPO's accumulated budgetary surplus.

  • Article 33
    Data collection

    An evidence database on IP-backed finance, and a provision contemplating a possible Union-wide register of pledges over IP assets.

  • Article 34
    Intellectual property valuators

    Who they are and what the framework expects of them.

  • Article 35
    Certification of intellectual property valuators

    Voluntary. It is expressly not a professional licence and confers no binding or presumptive legal effect on a valuation. EUIPO is not liable for individual valuations.

  • Article 36
    Programming and reporting

    EUIPO must carry the new tasks in a separate section of its multiannual and annual work programmes and its annual report, so the money and the results can be seen on their own.

  • Article 37
    Amendment to Regulation (EU) 2017/1001

    Inserts a new point (f) into Article 151(1) of the EU trade mark Regulation, which is the legal hook that lets EUIPO do any of this. Watch this article in any compromise text.

Chapter 4 · Final provisions (Articles 38 to 41)
  • Article 38
    Committee procedure

    The comitology arrangements for implementing acts.

  • Article 39
    Exercise of the delegation

    Paragraph 2 confers delegated powers under Articles 11(2), 19(10), 22(5), 24(12), 28(5) and 31(8). Paragraph 3 allows revocation of 7(2), 19(11), 22(5), 24(12), 28(5) and 31(8). The two lists do not match.

  • Article 40
    Evaluation

    Four years after entry into force and every five years thereafter, the Commission evaluates the Regulation against its objectives, in particular on procurement as a driver of innovation and on the valuation, commercialisation and use of IP, and reports to the Parliament, the Council and the European Economic and Social Committee.

  • Article 41
    Entry into force

    The twentieth day after publication in the Official Journal. Binding in its entirety and directly applicable in all Member States. No deferred application date.

10. The Recommendation, point by point

Thirteen numbered points and eleven recitals. It is not binding, it needs no transposition, and it applies from the day the Council adopts it. What it does is tell twenty-seven Member States to build their sandboxes the same way.

A sandbox is not permission to ignore the law

The Commission is explicit that this is not deregulation: a sandbox is a temporary framework allowing experimentation, not a permanent change to the regulatory environment, and the safeguards stay in place. Derogations from Union secondary law are possible only where the Union act in question provides for them. A Council Recommendation creates no such power by itself.

The thirteen points
  • Point 1
    Definitions

    Five of them. A regulatory sandbox is a structured framework letting participants experiment with innovative solutions for a limited time in a controlled environment, supervised by one or more competent authorities, producing evidence-based regulatory learning. Experimentation is development, testing and validation in real-world conditions before the solution is made available. A controlled environment may be physical, digital or hybrid, inside or outside a laboratory. A competent authority is any national, regional or local public-sector body with supervision or enforcement responsibilities. A cross-border regulatory sandbox is one established, operated or supervised jointly by authorities from more than one Member State.

  • Point 2
    Designing national provisions

    Eight things a Member State should consider, (a) to (h): whether a sandbox is needed in that sector at all; applying the Union provisions already in force; stating the reasons and defining the objectives in the national act itself; making any derogations explicit, clearly defined and duly justified; derogating from Union secondary law only where the Union act itself allows it; allowing sandboxes to open on the authority's initiative or on application; checking whether test facilities, including those accredited under Regulation (EC) No 765/2008, could be used; and cooperating with research and technology infrastructures so participants can reach their facilities and services.

  • Point 3
    Conditions for experimentation

    Seven headings that every scheme must specify: the objectives and scope, which should include fostering solutions that cannot be developed under the rules as they stand; the authorised types of experimentation; restrictions including maximum duration and geographic limits; an assessment of risks with mitigation and safeguards protecting public interests, fundamental rights, constitutional and Treaty rights, safety of persons and property, consumers, the environment, public health, public order, international obligations and fair competition; the terms of evaluation; the conditions for extension, renewal, suspension, modification or termination, including serious-incident reporting; and the conditions for applying any derogation.

  • Point 4
    Duration

    Proportionate to the objectives, long enough to achieve the intended purpose and to generate sufficient regulatory learning. A sandbox too short to teach anything defeats its own point.

  • Point 5
    Participation and operation

    Seven headings, (a) to (g). Eligibility, application, selection, entry and exit, access to a specific environment including any financial conditions, and the modalities for explicit prior consent from participating natural persons. Liability for damage caused to third parties, including users, test subjects and consumers. Management of intellectual property rights, data protection, privacy and confidential information. The materials and resources each side provides. Monitoring, reporting, evaluation and knowledge sharing, with the actors involved in evaluation named in the national provisions. An exit strategy covering both deployment pathways for successful experiments and the orderly termination of unsuccessful or potentially harmful ones. And support measures, including guidance, training, awareness-raising and where appropriate funding, tailored to innovative startups, scaleups, SMEs and potential customers.

  • Point 6
    Cross-border sandboxes

    Member States are encouraged to set them up. Where they do, the authorities involved should conclude an agreement setting out at least the conditions for experimentation, the allocation of roles and responsibilities between authorities of different Member States for establishment, operation, supervision, monitoring and termination, and the legal provisions and administrative arrangements that apply.

  • Point 7
    Transparent and non-discriminatory assessment

    All requests to establish and to participate in a sandbox should be assessed on a transparent and non-discriminatory basis.

  • Point 8
    Permitted restrictions

    The exception to point 7, subject to the Union's obligations under applicable international agreements, to the extent necessary and on objective grounds: (a) restrict participation to Union citizens and entities established in the Union; (b) give priority to EU-established SMEs, innovative startups or scaleups; or (c) require participants to carry out certain research, development and innovation activities in the Union.

  • Point 9
    Regulatory learning

    Collect and analyse the results and the lessons learnt, then assess whether to fold them into the national regulatory framework. This is the purpose of the whole exercise. A sandbox that changes no rule has produced nothing.

  • Point 10
    Exit report

    On request from a participant, the competent authority must issue a report detailing the activities carried out, the results and the learning outcomes, with the aim of accelerating conformity assessment, standardisation or national market approval. For a company, this is the most commercially useful line in the instrument.

  • Point 11
    Publication

    Information on the establishment and outcomes of experimentation should be made public, fully protecting business secrets and confidential information, unless publication would be contrary to national or Union security interests.

  • Point 12
    Timing

    Member States should implement the Recommendation as soon as practicable. There is no deadline, because a Recommendation cannot set one.

  • Point 13
    Monitoring

    Member States are invited to monitor progress in implementation, with the Commission's support.

The Recommendation also maps where Union sandbox provisions already exist, and defers to them. Nine policy areas carry one, and Brubru covers most of them: artificial intelligence under Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, which requires at least one national sandbox per Member State; biotechnology under the Biotech Act, with five sandbox frameworks; cybersecurity under the Cyber Resilience Act; public-sector interoperability under the Interoperable Europe Act; energy and climate under the Net-Zero Industry Act and the small modular reactor and maritime strategies; financial services under the DLT Pilot Regime; mobility under the automotive action plan and military mobility; and life sciences and pharmaceuticals under the pharmaceutical and medical devices revisions.

The measured picture behind the proposal: the Commission’s 2025 survey of Member States found 132 national regulatory sandboxes across 15 Member States, concentrated in Germany and France, up from 40 in the 2021 survey. A 2026 assessment found experimentation clauses, pilot schemes or sandboxes in roughly 10% to 15% of Commission impact assessments between 2020 and 2025, mainly in digital, financial and emerging-technology areas.

11. Seven things wrong with the text

We read all 41 articles. Seven internal defects survive in the published proposal. None of them changes what the Act is trying to do, and all of them are the kind of thing that gets fixed in a corrigendum or by the Parliament's lawyer-linguists. They are here because a page that walks the articles should say what walking them actually found.

  • Article 16 is printed as “Article 10”. Between Article 15 and Article 17 the heading reads “Article 10, Financial guarantees”, duplicating the real Article 10 on procurement documents. The article is not missing: Article 10(1)(e) requires the documents to state guarantees “in accordance with the obligations laid down in Articles 13, 14, 15 and 16”. The Act cross-references an Article 16 it never prints. It is a numbering typo, not a gap.
  • Article 6 numbers its paragraphs 4 to 8 instead of 1 to 5, and then refers internally to “paragraph 1, second subparagraph, point (a) or (b)”, which under the printed numbering does not exist. We checked every article in the Act: this one is the only one affected.
  • Article 17(4) refers to “paragraph 2(e)”. Paragraph 2's list ends at (d).
  • Article 17(5) refers to “paragraph 1, points (c), (d) and (e)”. Paragraph 1 has no lettered points at all. Those criteria are in paragraph 4.
  • Article 21(1) repeats year three. The declining value engineering threshold reads €10m after one year, €9m after two, €8m after three and €7m after three. Two thresholds start on the same day.
  • Article 22(3) sends evaluation to Article 11. Article 11 governs access conditions and place of performance and never mentions evaluation. The award rules are in Article 17.
  • Article 39 confers and revokes different lists. Paragraph 2 confers delegated powers under Articles 11(2) and 19(10) among others; paragraph 3 allows revocation of 7(2) and 19(11). Article 7 contains no delegated-act empowerment at all, while Article 11's real empowerment cannot be revoked.

The explanatory memorandum adds two more, and these matter more for a reader than the numbering does. It says the proposal “consists of three Chapters” when the enacting terms carry four. And it says “Chapter I of the Regulation only applies to R&D services procurements that fall outside” the directives, when the article that actually says so, Article 2, says it of Chapter 2. If you read only the memorandum, you will describe the Act's structure wrongly, which is how most summaries of it came to do exactly that.

One candidate we dropped

Article 22(4)(e) cites “Article 11(6)” for reserving contracts. Article 11 does contain a reserve provision, and we could not establish which paragraph number it carries in the published layout, so we are not calling it an error. A finding you cannot prove does not belong on the list.

12. What happens next

The two instruments now travel on different tracks, and one of them is much shorter than the other.

The Regulation

Ordinary legislative procedure. It goes to the European Parliament and the Council, committee allocation and rapporteurs follow. Industry and internal market are the natural leads, with legal affairs on the IP chapter. Expect the usual eighteen months to three years, and expect Article 11 and Article 19 to carry most of the amendments.

The Recommendation

Adopted by the Council alone, on a Commission proposal, under Article 292 TFEU. No Parliament vote, no transposition, no deadline. It applies from adoption and its effect is entirely a matter of whether Member States choose to follow it.

  • Watch Article 37. The amendment to Regulation (EU) 2017/1001 is the hook that lets EUIPO do any of this. Remove it and Chapter 3 has no carrier.
  • Watch the 50% rule. The economics were modelled at 70% of R&D plus 20% of production. If the co-legislators move toward the modelled rule, the impact figures become defensible; if they move away from it, they become optimistic.
  • Watch European Business Wallets. Articles 4, 25, 32 and 33 all deliver obligations through a Regulation that is not yet adopted. If that file slips, these duties have no vehicle.
  • Watch the four-year clocks. The valuation framework and the marketplace are both due four years after entry into force, and entry into force is twenty days after publication, whenever that turns out to be.

13. What this means for you

If you run a startup or a scaleup

This is written with you in mind and it is worth reading Articles 15, 16 and 19. You cannot be failed for having no track record. Financial capacity requirements are capped at half the contract value and every guarantee is halved for you. You may file your financial evidence after you have won. And you keep your intellectual property, which is the part that changes whether a public contract is worth bidding for at all.

If you are a public buyer

You get a legal regime where you had Treaty principles and case law, and with it certainty on State aid. You also get obligations: consult the market first, describe a problem rather than a product, weight quality at half, buy from more than one supplier, evaluate within two months, and pay in advance unless you can say why not. Budget for the training. The impact assessment did.

If you hold or finance IP

The valuation framework and the certification of valuators are voluntary and carry no legal effect, so nothing forces your hand. What changes is the reference point: once a common methodology exists and EU institutions use it wherever Union programmes require valuing IP, it becomes the number everyone argues from. The possible register of pledges in Article 33 is the provision to watch if you lend against IP.

How the economic figures were produced

  • RHOMOLO, a spatial dynamic computable general equilibrium model over 235 NUTS-2 regions and 10 sectors. Capital is mobile across EU regions, labour is spatially fixed, migration is deliberately excluded, expectations are myopic, and the shocks are phased in linearly over ten years.
  • Three measures modelled: M1 IP valuation methodology and licensing; M2 R&D procurement procedures including cross-border; M3 preference for EU-made solutions. M1 drives most of the GDP gain through capital deepening; M2 supplies productivity gains.
  • Administrative costs computed with the Standard Cost Model, deliberately over the economically affected population rather than the legally eligible one, because the authors wanted plausible behavioural responses rather than purely theoretical legal eligibility. That choice is why the numbers are lower than a naive reading of the eligible population would give.
  • Costs stated: EUIPO set-up €2,423,520 to €2,566,080 over two years (17 to 18 FTE), then €736,000 a year (about 10.3 FTE). Public authorities: €45 million of additional adjustment costs for training on R&D procurement and the EU preference, plus €2.4 million for the public sector to build the valuation framework, the centre and the marketplace. In the model, public buyers carry €26.1 million a year of adjustment costs against efficiency gains of €64.8 million to €1,166.4 million a year. Administrative effort on IP for IP-rich firms falls about 75%, from 40 to 80 hours a year to 10 to 20.
