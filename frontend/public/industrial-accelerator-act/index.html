The Industrial Accelerator Act

Growing EU Industry, Securing Jobs for the Future. The European Commission's proposal to accelerate permitting, introduce low-carbon labels for basic materials, set EU origin requirements in public procurement, and screen foreign investments in strategic sectors. Here is everything you need to know.

Structure of the Proposal
I General Provisions (Arts 1-3) II Enabling Conditions for Industrial Production (Arts 4-6) III Strengthening Strategic Value Chains (Arts 7-16) IV Foreign Investment Contribution (Arts 17-24) V Industrial Manufacturing Acceleration Areas (Arts 25-27) VI Final Provisions (Arts 28-36)

Chapter I: General Provisions

Articles 1 to 3: what the Regulation is for, the 20% objective, and the definitions. Article numbers and headings below follow the Commission's proposal, COM(2026) 100.

Article 1
Subject matter and scope
Four tools for the Union's manufacturing base in selected strategic sectors: faster permit-granting (including decarbonisation projects in energy-intensive industries), lead markets built through Union origin and low-carbon requirements in public procurement and public support, conditions on foreign direct investment in emerging strategic sectors, and industrial manufacturing acceleration areas.
Article 2
Industrialisation objective
The Union and Member States shall seek to ensure that by 2035 manufacturing accounts for at least 20% of Union GDP. The explanatory memorandum puts today's share at 14.3%, down from 17.4% in 2000.
Article 3
Definitions
Defines industrial manufacturing projects (NACE C, except tobacco), energy-intensive industries (by reference to Annex I), their decarbonisation projects, and the single permit-granting procedure. The strategic sectors themselves are listed in Annex I: energy-intensive industries, the automotive industry and net-zero technologies.

Chapter II: Enabling Conditions for Industrial Production

Articles 4 to 6: one access point, one application, and decarbonisation projects treated as strategic

Article 4
Single access points
Each Member State sets up a national single access point for the single application, routing it to the right authority and showing the applicant every step and deadline. It runs on the European Business Wallets, for automated data exchange and re-use of data authorities already hold. Applies one year after entry into force.
Article 5
Permit-granting procedure
A single permit-granting procedure on a single application covering every permit, coordinated by one competent authority. Completeness must be acknowledged, or missing information requested, within 45 days; a second request is allowed within 30 days and may not open new areas. Sector-specific Union rules take precedence. Applies one year after entry into force.
Article 6
Energy-intensive industry decarbonisation projects
Applies the Net-Zero Industry Act's permitting rules (Chapter II, Section II of Regulation (EU) 2024/1735) to every such project, and treats each one as a strategic project under the proposed Regulation on speeding up environmental assessment.

Chapter III: Strengthening the Union's Strategic Industrial Value Chains

Articles 7 to 16: what counts as Union origin, what counts as low-carbon, and where public money must buy both

Article 7
Union origin
Content of Union origin is content originating in the Union, with origin determined under the Union Customs Code, Regulation (EU) No 952/2013.
Article 8
Content equivalent to Union origin in public procurement
Content from countries with an EU free-trade agreement or customs union, or party to the WTO Government Procurement Agreement where obligations exist, counts as Union origin. The Commission may exclude a country by delegated act for lack of national treatment, to avoid dependencies, or under an exception in the agreement. The most contested equivalence in Parliament.
Article 9
Content equivalent to Union origin in other public intervention
The same equivalence for public support schemes, limited to free-trade-agreement and customs-union partners, with the same three grounds for exclusion by delegated act.
Article 10
Low-carbon products
A product in Annex II is low-carbon when it meets delegated acts under the Construction Products Regulation or the Ecodesign Regulation. The Commission may also create voluntary classification systems by greenhouse-gas intensity for industrial products, with verified emissions data.
Article 11
Public procurement
Bidders owned or controlled from third countries without an access agreement are excluded from the procedures in Annexes II and III. Those procedures must apply the Union origin and low-carbon requirements, unless there is a single supplier, no suitable tender in the past two years, or disproportionate cost or technical incompatibility; a cost difference above 25% may be presumed disproportionate.
Article 12
Other forms of public intervention
Member States design support schemes to carry the Annex II and III requirements: schemes covering at least 45% of the national budget for the Annex II schemes, and 100% for the vehicle schemes in Annex III. A scheme may depart from them where they would cause significant delay (presumed above seven months) or raise the product's cost by more than 30%.
Article 13
Financial support for corporate vehicles
The "made in the European Union" criterion for support to corporate cars and vans under the clean corporate vehicles proposal follows Part II of Annex III.
Article 14
CO2 emission performance standards credits
Ties the "made in the EU" test for small zero-emission vehicle credits, and "low-carbon steel made in the EU", in the car CO2 standards to this Regulation's origin and low-carbon definitions.
Article 15
Certification of Union origin for vehicles
Six months after entry into force, manufacturers issue a document with the certificate of conformity certifying that a vehicle meets the Annex III origin requirements.
Article 16
Delegation of powers (chemicals)
Lets the Commission set Union-level demand-side measures for chemicals of Union origin made from sustainable carbon sources.

Annexes II and III: the numbers behind the lead markets

Annex II, from 1 January 2029, for public procurement and for support to buildings, infrastructure and civil vehicles: at least 25% of steel low-carbon, at least 5% of concrete and mortar low-carbon and of Union origin, and at least 25% of aluminium low-carbon and of Union origin.

Annex III, for electric and fuel-cell vehicles bought or supported with public money: assembly in the Union; at least 70% of component value (battery excluded) of Union origin; Union-origin battery cells plus further battery components; and, three years after entry into force, at least 50% of the e-powertrain and of the main electronic systems by value.

Chapter IV: Foreign Investment Contribution

Articles 17 to 24: large investments from dominant third countries must add value in the Union

Article 17
Scope
Foreign direct investment above EUR 100 million in batteries, electric vehicles, solar PV, or critical raw materials, where the investor's country holds more than 40% of global manufacturing capacity. Such investments may not proceed without explicit approval.
Article 18
Value-added criteria
Each Member State designates an Investment Authority. From 12 months after entry into force it approves only investments meeting four of six conditions: at most 49% ownership; a joint venture with Union partners; licensing of intellectual property and know-how; R&D in the Union of at least 1% of the target's gross revenue; at least 50% Union workers; and a published strategy for Union value chains.
Article 19
Prior notification
The investor notifies the Investment Authority where the asset is located. Control starts at 30% of share capital, voting rights or ownership.
Article 20
Review and approval
Admissibility within 30 days (extendable by 15), then transmission to the Commission, which may give a written opinion within 30 days on scope, conditions and whether to approve.
Article 21
Review by the Commission
The Commission may take over the assessment on its own initiative for investments with significant impact on value creation or above EUR 1 billion, or at a Member State's request.
Article 22
Monitoring and enforcement
Investors report on compliance. Penalty payments are at least 5% of the investor's average daily aggregate turnover.
Article 23
Monitoring by the Commission
The Commission tracks global manufacturing capacity in each sector and publishes it, and may fine investors for false or missing information.
Article 24
Delegation of powers
The list of sectors can be extended by delegated act to other net-zero and nuclear fuel-cycle technologies, excluding digital, AI, quantum and semiconductors.

Chapter V: Industrial Manufacturing Acceleration Areas

Articles 25 to 27: at least one area per Member State, permitted in advance

Article 25
Designating acceleration areas
Every Member State designates at least one area within 12 months of entry into force, clustering projects in the Annex I sectors, chosen for security of supply, capacity, value chains and innovation. The most amended article in ENVI after Annex III.
Article 26
Enabling conditions
Coordinated financing (including for SMEs and small mid-caps), research and innovation investment, and an energy-needs analysis for each area, reviewed at least every three years.
Article 27
Permit-granting in acceleration areas
One aggregated baseline permit per area, issued after the area-level assessments; projects then need only the installation-specific permits and count as strategic projects under the proposed environmental-assessment Regulation.

Chapter VI: Final Provisions

Articles 28 to 36: evaluation, delegated powers, penalties, and three amended Regulations

Articles 28-29
Evaluation and review
Evaluation two years after entry into force, then every three years. Chapters III and IV reviewed after three years, looking at origin requirements for shipbuilding and rail rolling stock.
Articles 30-32
Delegation, committee, penalties
Delegated powers under Articles 8, 9, 10, 16 and 24 for an indeterminate period, revocable by Parliament or Council; a comitology committee; national penalties that are effective, proportionate and dissuasive.
Article 33
Amending the Single Digital Gateway
Amends the annexes of Regulation (EU) 2018/1724.
Article 34
Amending the Net-Zero Industry Act
Rewrites Regulation (EU) 2024/1735: new definitions (industrial and stationary storage batteries, hydronic heat pumps) and new Articles 28a to 28h on Union origin in support schemes, auctions and procurement for net-zero products, with limits on high-risk suppliers. The most amended article in both the joint draft report and the ENVI draft opinion.
Article 35
Amending the Construction Products Regulation
Rewrites the Commission's power, in Article 22(9) of Regulation (EU) 2024/3110, to set environmental sustainability labelling requirements for construction product families and categories.
Article 36
Entry into force
Enters into force the day after publication. The single access point and single procedure (Articles 4 and 5) apply one year later.

Joint Committee Debate (2 June 2026)

First consideration at the INTA-ITRE-IMCO joint committee, with Commissioner Stéphane Séjourné

On 2 June 2026 the joint committee of INTA, ITRE and IMCO (CJ80) held its first consideration of the proposal. Commissioner Stéphane Séjourné (DG GROW) presented the text and took around an hour of questions; the committees also had before them the EPRS Initial Appraisal of the Commission Impact Assessment (PE 788.126). Séjourné's diagnosis: China produces over 50% of the world's steel and batteries and 90% of the solar panels used in Europe; its share of global manufacturing rose from 6% (2000) to about 30%, while Europe's fell from 21% to 14%. He cited roughly 20,000 industrial jobs lost in the EU per month this mandate, driven by mass subsidies (around 4% of Chinese GDP), currency undervaluation and non-reciprocal market access.

The three pillars Séjourné set out:

1. European preference in public financing. Where public money is used (tenders, subsidies), priority for products made in Europe across energy-intensive industries (steel, aluminium, cement, chemicals), automotive and clean tech (batteries, wind, solar, heat pumps, electrolysers) and nuclear, with per-sector shares of critical components of EU origin (for example battery cells and solar inverters), building on the Net-Zero Industry Act.

2. Conditions on foreign direct investment. Europe stays open, but investments above EUR 100m from a country holding more than 40% of global capacity face conditions: real technology transfer, at least 50% of jobs in the EU, foreign participation capped at 49%, mandatory R&D in Europe and supply of European components. "Europe cannot just be an assembly platform."

3. Speed and simplification. A single digital one-stop shop and priority industrial zones to cut multi-year permitting to months, with a self-declaration model and a posteriori controls instead of upfront reporting. He argued the compliance cost is marginal next to the cost of dependence, and pointed to Turkey and Canada already raising reciprocity.

The three co-rapporteurs

Co-rapporteurOpening position
Anna Cavazzini
INTA (Greens/EFA)		Cited Saxony's collapsed solar industry and Volkswagen's factory closure; "200,000 EU industrial jobs lost since 2024" to unfair Chinese competition. Christophe Grudler
ITRE (Renew)		An industrial, economic and sovereignty crisis (Chinese exports to Europe up 30% since the start of the year). Welcomed that public money would back EU industrial activity, but flagged three fixes: "Made in Europe must be meaningful" since the proposal's scope covers about 80 countries on top of the 27, which is "made anywhere, made without Europe"; more ambition (thresholds and component lists too limited, too many derogations); and applicability, so that traceability of origin does not become an obstacle for SMEs.
Pierre Jouvet
IMCO (S&D)		After factory and SME visits, stressed huge expectations and complexity. "Made in Europe" must mean local production: as worded, a product can count as EU-made if the last major transformation happens in Europe even if most components come from outside, and 80+ countries are covered. Wants clear rules of origin, lower derogation thresholds (25 to 30% would let criteria be flouted; price gaps run 35 to 60% by sector), a genuinely integrated value chain, and, as a red line for S&D, social conditionality applied across the whole text, not just public tenders.

Where the groups converged and clashed. The dominant criticism, shared across Renew, S&D, Greens, ECR, PfE and The Left, was that the proposal treats content from the roughly 80 countries with an EU trade agreement as "EU origin". Sara Matthieu (Greens, ITRE) warned of Chinese car-part makers investing in Morocco being counted as Made in Europe; David Cormand (Greens) said a "Buy European Act" with 80 to 90 countries "should be the 27", with exceptions opt-in not opt-out; Kathleen Van Brempt (S&D) demanded the Commission produce the actual list of eligible trade-agreement partners ("India is clearly out, but what about Canada?"); the vehicle rule (70% EU origin excluding the battery, with Article 8 counting trade-agreement content as EU) was challenged by Nicola Danti (Renew); Dan Nica (S&D) tied the file to unenforced CBAM and the CO2-price gap with imports. The EPP urged caution: Dirk Gotink warned against rigid reindustrialisation targets ("exactly what Xi Jinping does in his five-year plan") and against "closing the door on our friends"; Tomislav Sokol pressed on Annex scope and alignment with the public-procurement directive revision. PfE and ECR were sharper: Paolo Borchia (PfE) noted the proposal's negative Regulatory Scrutiny Board opinion and unconsidered SME effects; Klara Dostálová (PfE) attacked the wide scope of delegated and implementing acts (key definitions and product lists left for later) as undermining investor predictability. The Greens and The Left (Hanna Gedin, also for Marina Mesure) pushed for higher ambition and "green protectionism", while EPP voices warned Made in Europe must not simply mean more expensive.

Commissioner's reply. Séjourné grouped some 60 questions: the text is one pillar of a wider strategy (single market, trade diversification, energy prices), aiming to return to 2000s industrialisation levels while decarbonising. He defended acting against Chinese firms that trade at a loss as a "predatory strategy" to destroy European industry and raise prices later, citing German machinery SMEs bought by Chinese funds, closed, and reopened in China. The list of trade-agreement partners, the scope of delegated acts, SME safeguards and sector definitions were left as the substance of the parliamentary work now beginning.

The Joint Draft Report, 9 September 2026

267 amendments signed by all three co-rapporteurs together, the first text Parliament has written on this file

One report, three signatures

Because this is a Rule 58 joint committee file, there is no lead committee and no single rapporteur. INTA, ITRE and IMCO decide together, and the draft report PE792.067 is signed jointly by Anna Cavazzini (Greens/EFA), Christophe Grudler (Renew) and Pierre Jouvet (S&D). It is dated 9 September 2026 and entered the Legislative Observatory's documentation gateway on 11 September. The document code, CJ80, is the joint-committee series rather than any one committee's.

Three groups writing one text together is unusual and it matters for reading what follows: none of these amendments is a solo position to be traded away. They are the starting point the three committees have already agreed among themselves.

What the 267 amendments do

The split is 171 amendments to the articles, 66 to the recitals and 30 to the annex. They are not evenly spread. Article 34 alone draws 58, and that is the article rewriting Regulation (EU) 2024/1735, the Net-Zero Industry Act, so a quarter of the report is consequential surgery on an act adopted two years ago.

The explanatory statement sets out the common line. The co-rapporteurs welcome the proposal and share its objective, but consider the framework should be strengthened and more effectively targeted, so that demand and investment in the Union translate into genuine industrial value creation, long-term capacity inside the Union, and real emission reductions.

The five things they want changed
  • Strategic sectors that can evolve. Demand-side measures extended to plastics, the maritime manufacturing industry added, and the list of strategic sectors made expandable by a defined method against objective, transparent criteria rather than case by case.
  • An opt-in test for third-country content. Content from outside the Union would count as equivalent to Union origin only where clearly defined conditions are met: reciprocity, no new strategic dependencies, substantially equivalent conditions of competition, respect for fundamental labour standards and climate commitments, and safeguards against circumvention, with particular consideration for neighbouring countries already integrated into Union value chains.
  • Rules of origin that follow the value. The co-rapporteurs call this question central to the effectiveness of the whole Regulation. They invite the Commission to re-examine the definition of last substantial transformation in Article 60 of the Union Customs Code, Regulation (EU) No 952/2013, for the products and components covered, with the aim of introducing a minimum threshold of 50 % Union added value.
  • Procurement used strategically, with escape valves. Public procurement and other public intervention should create demand for Union-origin and low-carbon products, while remaining proportionate and workable: flexibility is kept where no reasonable alternative or suitable tender exists, or where the requirement would mean disproportionate cost or technical incompatibility.
  • Foreign investment that adds something. Foreign direct investment stays welcome but should strengthen emerging strategic manufacturing rather than create new dependencies, with conditions targeted on genuine added value through technological development, research and development, employment in the Union and stronger European value chains.
Two dimensions the proposal under-weighted

Social. The amendments require effective compliance with applicable social and labour law obligations as a condition of public procurement and public support, and promote the creation and retention of high-quality jobs, on the argument that public money should build productive capacity, skills and the employment that goes with them inside the Union.

Circular. Competitiveness, decarbonisation and circularity are treated as mutually reinforcing rather than in tension. The framework for low-carbon products is strengthened, and recycled materials, secondary raw materials and scrap are recognised as contributing to low-carbon production.

Why the customs cross-reference is the one to watch

A 50 % Union added-value threshold inside the definition of last substantial transformation would reach far beyond this Regulation, because Article 60 of the Union Customs Code determines non-preferential origin for every product that crosses the Union border. The co-rapporteurs are careful: they invite the Commission to re-examine the definition for the products and components covered by this Regulation, and describe it as a first step. Read it alongside the customs reform, where the Union Customs Code has itself just been replaced.

The Environment Committee's Opinion

282 amendments from the rapporteur, 767 from members: where ENVI wants the Act changed

A stricter "Made in Europe" (draft opinion PE789.106, 17 June 2026)

ENVI is an opinion committee: it advises INTA, ITRE and IMCO and does not vote on the report. Its rapporteur for opinion, Mathilde Androuët (Patriots for Europe), finds the proposal too weak against foreign interference. She argues Union origin should rest on cumulative, verifiable criteria: substantial transformation in the Union and European added value of at least 60% of the ex-works price, rather than treating free-trade partners as Union origin.

Her 282 amendments concentrate on Article 34 (37, the Net-Zero Industry Act rewrite), Annex III on vehicles (19) and Article 12 on support schemes (13).

What the rapporteur proposes, in the text
  • Nuclear in the Net-Zero Industry Act. A new category of "dispatchable low-carbon technologies": nuclear, hydroelectric and geothermal, plus storage, waste-heat recovery and carbon capture (amendment 215).
  • Auctions not decided on price alone. A minimum weight for environmental and resilience criteria in net-zero auctions.
  • Tighter vehicle thresholds. E-powertrain Union-origin share raised from 50% to 60% (amendment 270); battery components phased in, four of Union origin from 2029 and five from 2032 (amendment 269); every stage of final assembly in the Union (amendment 271).
  • Equivalence only with equal standards. A third country's content counts as Union origin only if its environmental standards are equivalent, taking its energy mix into account (amendment 108).
  • Harder to opt out. The delay that lets a support scheme skip the requirements rises from seven to nine months, and an exemption must show no equivalent Union solution exists (amendment 135).
What members tabled (PE791.124 and PE791.125, 23 July 2026)

767 amendments, numbered 283 to 1049 with no gaps: 540 to the articles, 141 to the annexes, 86 to the recitals. This numbering is ENVI's own; members' amendments to the joint draft report were not yet published on 23 September.

Most amended: Annex III on vehicles (79), Article 25 on acceleration areas (55), Article 34 (51), Article 10 on low-carbon products (49), Article 8 on origin equivalence in procurement (45), Article 9 (43) and Annex II (43).

Amendments signed by at least one member of each group: S&D 167, EPP 162, Renew 125, Greens/EFA 115, ESN 99, The Left 63, ECR 50, Patriots for Europe 40, non-attached 8. The most active tablers were Christophe Clergeau (S&D, 120), Sakis Arnaoutoglou (S&D, 112), Rasmus Nordqvist (Greens/EFA, 106), Nikolas Farantouris (S&D, 100), Anja Arndt (ESN, 99) and Marc Jongen (ESN, 91).

Four fault lines
  • Electric vehicles bought or supported with public money (Annex III). A cross-group push to raise the Union-origin value share from 70% to 80%: Dimitris Tsiodras (EPP), Sakis Arnaoutoglou (S&D) and seven ECR members led by Stefano Cavedagna, all citing coherence with the Automotive Package and the use of low-carbon Union steel.
  • Who counts as "Made in EU" (Articles 8 and 9). Tsiodras and Arnaoutoglou table the same justification: over 75% of EU steel imports come from free-trade partners, so equivalence could let nearly 80 countries qualify. Arndt and Jongen (ESN) would stop third-country content being treated as Union origin at all. The ECR members accept trade agreements, but not as a route for foreign products into preferences meant for European industry.
  • Carbon classes (Article 10). The ECR members would remove the voluntary greenhouse-gas intensity classes as burdensome and overlapping; ESN rejects carbon-based lead markets as not technology-neutral; Jeannette Baljeu, Ana Vasconcelos and Katri Kulmuni (Renew) want carbon capture counted in the method; Jessica Polfjärd (EPP) wants one definition of low-carbon steel shared with the car CO2 rules.
  • Acceleration areas (Article 25). ESN calls a mandatory area interference in national planning; Arnaoutoglou wants island, border, mountain and remote regions to benefit; Per Clausen (The Left) wants no weakening of environmental assessment; Martin Hojsík and Michal Wiezik (Renew) want new sites supplied by domestic energy.

Why it matters for the joint committee

The origin question splits groups rather than following them: an EPP member, an S&D member and the ECR members table the same 80% threshold, while ESN rejects equivalence outright. The rapporteur's own group signed fewer members' amendments than any other group except the non-attached, so ENVI's final opinion will turn on compromises with S&D and the EPP, which tabled the most.
