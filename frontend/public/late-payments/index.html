The Problem: Late Payments Kill SMEs

Why the Commission proposed replacing the 2011 Directive with a Regulation

The core issue

Thousands of small and medium-sized enterprises go bankrupt every year waiting for their invoices to be paid. Late payments are the single biggest cause of cash flow problems for SMEs, and the 2011 Directive has failed to solve the problem because Member States implemented it inconsistently and enforcement was weak.

Directive 2011/7/EU
Current framework
Public authorities: 30 days (exceptionally 60). Businesses: 60 days unless otherwise agreed. Minimum EUR 40 compensation. Statutory interest of 8% above ECB reference rate. But enforcement is inconsistent.
Why a Regulation?
Direct applicability
A Regulation applies directly in all Member States without national transposition. This eliminates the inconsistent implementation that weakened the 2011 Directive and creates a uniform playing field across the Single Market.

Commission Proposal

COM(2023)533, published 12 September 2023. Part of the SME Relief Package.

Article 1
Scope
Applies to B2B and B2G commercial transactions (delivery of goods or provision of services for remuneration). Excludes B2C, damages compensation, and debts subject to insolvency proceedings.
Article 3
Payment deadlines
Maximum 30 calendar days from invoice receipt for all commercial transactions. No contractual freedom to extend beyond 30 days. This is the most controversial provision.
Article 5
Interest for late payment
Interest accrues automatically from the day after the payment deadline expires. Creditor must provide all necessary invoice information. Interest runs until full payment of principal.
Article 6
Rate of interest
Harmonised across the EU. New provision replacing the fragmented national approaches under the 2011 Directive.
Article 8
Compensation for recovery costs
Flat fee payable per transaction: EUR 50 (invoices up to EUR 1,500), EUR 100 (EUR 1,501 to EUR 15,000), EUR 150 (above EUR 15,000). Payable without a reminder.
Article 9
Unfair contractual terms
Null and void: any contractual term that excludes interest or compensation, extends payment beyond 30 days, or is grossly unfair to the creditor. Replaces Article 7 of the 2011 Directive.
Article 12
Expedited procedures
Creditors shall obtain an enforceable title within 90 calendar days of lodging an action, for unchallenged claims regardless of amount.
Article 13
Enforcement authorities
Each Member State must designate enforcement authorities to ensure compliance. They can take measures to ensure payment deadlines are met.

Rapporteur's Approach

Roza Thun und Hohenstein (Renew, Poland) tabled the IMCO draft report (PE756.002) on 14 November 2023

The rapporteur's philosophy

The rapporteur broadly supported the Commission's proposal but introduced key modifications to balance SME protection with business flexibility. Her main contributions were: introducing payment flexibility up to 60 days for B2B when mutually agreed; requiring enforcement authorities to be independent from public procurement bodies; adding a retail sector exemption for slow-moving and seasonal goods; and creating a public reporting framework for contracting authorities' payment practices.

Payment flexibility

While keeping the 30-day default, the rapporteur proposed allowing B2B payment terms of up to 60 days when expressly agreed between parties. This was the single most significant departure from the Commission text, which allowed no flexibility.

Independent enforcement

The rapporteur insisted that enforcement authorities must be independent from other public authorities, including those involved in public procurement procedures. This was designed to prevent conflicts of interest when public authorities are themselves late payers.

Retail sector exemption

Extended payment terms of up to 120 days proposed for the retail sector dealing in slow-moving or seasonal goods. This recognised the specific cash flow dynamics of retail supply chains.

Transparency reporting

Contracting authorities should report annually on their payment practices, categorising payments into intervals (1-30, 31-60, 61-90, 90+ days) and reporting average payment time. Reports must be submitted to enforcement authorities and be publicly accessible.

MEP Amendments in IMCO

380 amendments tabled (PE757.130: amendments 26-223; PE757.363: amendments 224-405)

The amendment battle

IMCO members tabled 380 amendments to the rapporteur's draft report, reflecting deep divisions on the balance between SME protection and contractual freedom. The key battleground was the maximum payment period: the Commission wanted a strict 30-day limit with no exceptions, the rapporteur proposed 60-day B2B flexibility, and several MEPs pushed for even greater flexibility or sector-specific carve-outs.

Payment periods (key contested area)

Multiple amendments sought to modify the payment deadline framework. Some MEPs backed the Commission's strict 30-day limit, arguing that any flexibility would be exploited by large companies at the expense of SMEs. Others pushed for broader exemptions, including 90-day terms for specific industries like construction and agriculture.

Compensation waivers

Amendment 51 removed the "without the necessity of a reminder" language for flat fee compensation. The adopted text requires that compensation is payable automatically but does not require the debtor to pay without being asked. Restrictions were added on waiving rights when the debtor is a public authority or large undertaking.

Enforcement independence (Amendment 65)

Enforcement authorities must be independent from other public authorities, including those involved in public procurement procedures. This was explicitly added to prevent conflicts of interest, since public authorities are often themselves late payers.

Proportionality (Amendment 66)

Enforcement authorities shall take "proportionate" measures (word added) to ensure payment deadlines are complied with. This was designed to prevent overly aggressive enforcement that could disrupt business relationships.

Transparency (Amendment 67)

Enforcement authorities must make publicly available aggregated information on the number of complaints lodged against undertakings and public authorities for violations of Article 3 (payment deadlines).

Micro-undertaking grace period

Micro-undertakings (fewer than 10 employees) receive a 12-month additional grace period as debtors, recognising their particular vulnerability to sudden regulatory changes in payment practices.

The committee vote, and the man who carried the file afterwards

The roll-call annex to report A9-0156/2024 records the IMCO vote of 20 March 2024 as 33 in favour, 10 against, 2 abstentions. Reading the names rather than the totals explains why the file has not moved since.

Renew split 3 to 4 against its own rapporteur. Sandro Gozi, Róża Thun und Hohenstein and Stéphanie Yon-Courtin voted in favour. Andrus Ansip, Vlad-Marius Botoș, Svenja Hahn and Ivars Ijabs voted against. The EPP split too, eight in favour against four opposed.

Ivars Ijabs voted against the report. On 3 October 2024, after the European elections, he became the rapporteur responsible for the very position he had opposed. That is not a contradiction on his part, it is a fact worth knowing when reading anything Parliament says about this file from 2025 onwards: the member now negotiating for Parliament did not support the mandate he inherited.

Two national parliaments also filed reasoned opinions on subsidiarity, both on 9 January 2024: the Italian Chamber of Deputies (PE757.362) and the Swedish Parliament (PE757.364). Neither reached the threshold for a yellow card, but both signalled the national-level resistance that the Council later formalised in its non-paper of 7 June 2024.

EP Adopted Text

T9-0299/2024, adopted 23 April 2024. IMCO vote: 33 for, 10 against, 2 abstentions.

What Parliament agreed on

The European Parliament adopted its first reading position on 23 April 2024, incorporating the rapporteur's key modifications and several committee amendments. The text maintains the 30-day default but adds significant flexibility for B2B relationships, sector-specific accommodations, and stronger enforcement safeguards. It was adopted with a comfortable majority (33-10-2 in committee), though opposition reflects industry concerns about contractual freedom.

Payment deadlines (as adopted)
30 days default, 60 days B2B, 120 days retail
Default maximum: 30 calendar days. B2B flexibility: up to 60 days when mutually agreed in writing. Retail sector: up to 120 days for slow-moving or seasonal goods.
Enforcement (as adopted)
Independent authorities with public reporting
Independent enforcement authorities in each Member State. Anonymous complaints permitted. Public reporting of complaints data. Expedited title procedures reduced from 90 to 60 calendar days. [Am.65, 66, 67, 80]
Digital tools (as adopted)
Article 17: digital enforcement
Member States shall use digital tools for effective enforcement. Invoice and credit management tools, including factoring, and financial literacy training must be available and accessible to SMEs. [Am.80]
Scope (as adopted)
Insurance included, micro-enterprises protected
Insurance contract payments included (reversing Commission exclusion). Micro-undertakings receive 12-month additional grace period as debtors. Consignment contracts permitted under freedom of contract.

Stakeholder Positions

Industry, SME associations, and professional bodies have taken strong positions

Oppose BusinessEurope, Eurochambres, EuroCommerce

In a joint statement (March 2025), these three major business federations called on the Commission to withdraw the proposal entirely. Their concerns:

  • Strict payment terms limit contractual freedom in B2B commercial relations
  • The EP's amendments, while adding flexibility, create a complex framework with categories and exemptions that confuse businesses
  • Risk of reducing the number of suppliers, likely penalising SMEs who would face more rigid requirements
  • Could require EUR 2 trillion in additional financing and increase corporates' interest payments by EUR 100 billion
  • May incentivise trade with non-EU partners not captured by the Regulation
  • A majority of Member States also oppose (see their non-paper of 7 June 2024)
Concerned ICISA (International Credit Insurance and Surety Association)

ICISA reports that while limited support was expressed for stricter regulation, many bodies representing corporates called for greater flexibility and a rethink. The Parliament's version softened some proposals but "ultimately maintained the weakening of contractual freedom." Member states expressed concern about interaction with existing national regimes.

Analytical AccountancyEurope

In their factsheet, AccountancyEurope provides a balanced analysis. The Regulation is expected to have a big impact on SMEs: improved liquidity, less administrative burden from chasing payments, and a level playing field. However, they note that SMEs as debtors must also ensure they can pay within the 30-day period, which has significant balance sheet implications.

Advisory EESC and Committee of the Regions

The European Economic and Social Committee (CES3705/2023, 17 January 2024) and the Committee of the Regions (CDR4941/2023, 31 January 2024) both provided opinions on the proposal. These advisory bodies generally supported the objective of combating late payments while raising concerns about implementation challenges for smaller public authorities.

Council Blockage

A majority of Member States oppose the proposal

Why the Council blocked the proposal

After the European Parliament adopted its first reading position in April 2024, the proposal moved to the Council. However, a majority of Member States have expressed opposition through a non-paper dated 7 June 2024. Their key concerns mirror industry positions:

  • Loss of contractual freedom in B2B relationships
  • Interaction with existing national regimes that many Member States are comfortable with
  • Lack of flexibility even in the EP-amended version
  • Proportionality concerns about replacing a directive with a regulation

No general approach or Presidency compromise text has been published. The file is effectively stalled, and BusinessEurope, Eurochambres, and EuroCommerce have called for the Commission to withdraw the proposal entirely.

The Commission went back for evidence: the 2025 survey, presented to IMCO on 26 January 2026

While the file sat blocked in Council, DG GROW ran a fresh survey and took the results to the IMCO committee on 26 January 2026 (presentation). It ran from 7 July to 25 September 2025 and drew 3,503 replies, half of them from micro-enterprises or the self-employed, led by Germany (719), Italy (610), Portugal (555), Spain (387), France (360) and Poland (335).

73% still report late payment problems, and for 30% it is systemic, happening always or often. Almost 60% of late payments occur in business-to-business transactions. The cause respondents put first is not illiquidity but the lack of sanctions and ineffective enforcement. The worst consequences they report are reduced investment and growth, higher working-capital needs, and a threat to the survival of the business itself.

The finding that matters politically is on caps. Almost 48% want a maximum payment term, and they prefer a short one: 30.71% at 30 days against 17.13% at 60 days, with 27% preferring the status quo. On the choice of instrument respondents split evenly between a Regulation and a revised Directive, but a large majority want the rules reinforced and harmonised either way.

That evidence sits awkwardly with where Parliament landed. Parliament softened the Commission's flat 30 days into 60 by agreement and 120 for slow-moving goods, yet the businesses surveyed back the stricter cap by nearly two to one. The presentation also notes that calls to keep the present B2B framework came primarily from a single sector: books.
